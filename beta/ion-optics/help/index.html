Beta · help and methods

Simulator help

An in-browser simulator for electrostatic and magnetic charged-particle optics: draw electrodes on a two-dimensional planar or cylindrically symmetric grid, solve for the potential, and trace ions and electrons through lenses, mirrors, energy analyzers, time-of-flight spectrometers and RF devices.

Beta: not yet validated by the author. The validation suite below compares the simulator with published and exact values, but its results have not yet been reviewed. Use the simulator for exploration, and check numbers that matter against an independent calculation.

Quick start

  1. Start from a preset. The Einzel lens loads on a first visit, solves and runs by itself. Presets in the top bar opens ten more devices, each with a short note and a suggestion to try.
  2. Change voltages. Every electrode in the Electrodes tab has a voltage box and a slider. With Live on, the trajectories run again while you drag. A voltage may be a number, a parameter name or an expression, and units can be typed (2 kV).
  3. Edit the geometry. Pick an electrode (or Void) in the view bar and draw boxes, tubes, plates, discs, circles, annuli, polygons, lines and quadrics on the canvas, or type exact numbers in the inspector. A geometry change marks the fields stale; small grids are solved again automatically, larger ones when you press Solve.
  4. Define particles and detectors. The Particles tab sets each source's species, positions, directions, energies and start times; the Recording tab adds planes. A plane that stops particles is a detector.
  5. Read the results. The dock below the canvas has Summary (counts, transmission, waist, spot size, energy diagnostics), Hits, Planes (detector images, phase space, emittance), TOF, Lens (focal properties), Profile (potential and field along a line), Scan & tune, Benchmarks and Log. Every table and plot exports to CSV.
  6. Save or share. Work is saved automatically in this browser. Project saves and opens files (.ionopt.json); Share copies a link that contains the whole project.

Constants are the CODATA 2022 values; derived ones (for example the acceleration constant e/u) are computed from them. Particles are relativistic by default; switch that off in the tracer settings to compare with nonrelativistic formulas.

Coordinates. The canvas shows x to the right and y up; particles always move in three dimensions (x, y, z).

  • Planar: the electrodes extend without limit along z, so the potential depends on x and y only and particles move freely along z (the RF quadrupole preset uses this for ions traveling along the rods).
  • Cylindrical: everything is a body of revolution about the x axis. The vertical axis is the radius r = √(y² + z²), and the canvas draws the lower half as a mirror image. A box becomes a ring or a disc, a circle a torus (or a sphere when it is centered on the axis), a line a cone or a flat annulus.
  • Mirror planes halve or quarter the work for symmetric devices: the solved region ends on the mirror plane, and the other half is its reflection.

Parameters and expressions. Any number can be an expression of parameters: + − * / ^, parentheses, sqrt abs sin cos tan (degrees), asin acos atan atan2 (returning degrees), exp ln log10 min max pow floor ceil round hypot and pi. The Einzel preset, for example, draws its tubes from a parameter D, and the hemispherical analyzer sets its voltages from the pass energy Ep. Expressions are parsed and evaluated by the simulator; they are never run as program code.

Geometry rules

  • Later shapes win. Shapes are painted in list order, and each one overwrites what lies under it. A shape assigned to Void carves free space out of earlier shapes (a hole, a slit, a gap). Reorder shapes by dragging them in the Shapes tab, or with [ and ].
  • Exact boundaries. Each grid line is intersected exactly with every shape, so a surface that falls between grid nodes keeps its true position: the solver uses the true distance to the surface (Shortley and Weller's method) instead of moving the surface to the nearest node. Curved electrodes are therefore accurate to second order in the cell size, and a plate thinner than a cell still blocks the field and the particles.
  • Zero-thickness lines (the Line tool) are sheets: they hold their electrode's voltage on both sides. Use them for thin apertures, deflector shields and meshes.
  • Meshes. Set an electrode's kind to mesh to make it transparent to particles. For the field a mesh is an ideal equipotential surface; a particle that reaches it passes with the probability given by the mesh transmission and is otherwise absorbed. The field is never averaged across a mesh, so the field jump at a mesh is kept sharp.
  • Domain edges are held at an electrode's voltage, have no normal field (a symmetry or open edge), are the axis (cylindrical), or follow a profile: segments of different electrodes, or a linear ramp between two voltages that acts as an ideal resistive divider, for example the walls of an ideal ion mirror.
  • Beyond the domain a particle either stops (it is recorded as having left) or drifts on in a straight line with no electric field; a magnetic field still acts there. Electrodes are not tested for hits outside the grid.
  • Warnings in the Log and on the Shapes tab point out shapes smaller than a cell, features thinner than half a cell, electrodes of different voltages that touch, shapes outside the domain or hidden by later ones, and apertures resolved by too few cells.

Voltages without new solves. Each electrode's field is solved once with that electrode at 1 V and all others at 0 V. Because Laplace's equation is linear, the field for any set of voltages is the weighted sum of these basis fields, so changing a voltage, a waveform or a parameter that only sets voltages takes a few milliseconds and needs no new solve. Time-dependent voltages (cosine, pulse or a table of values, each repeating or not) are grouped into channels and summed at every time step.

Accuracy

Three things limit the accuracy of a result: the grid (how finely the electrodes and the field are resolved), the field interpolation between grid nodes, and the trajectory integration. The simulator reports on each.

  • Cells across the smallest aperture. Lens properties depend on how well the field in and near the smallest opening is resolved. For thin-walled electrodes, 20 cells across the aperture radius give focal properties good to about 1 part in 1000, 40 cells about 2 parts in 104, and 80 cells about 1 part in 104. Electrodes with sharp (re-entrant) corners converge more slowly, with an error proportional to the cell size to the power 4/3: the default Einzel lens, at 40 cells, is good to a few parts in 1000. Use the Accuracy check to measure your own geometry. Below 16 cells a warning appears. In two dimensions, halving the cell size multiplies the node count, the memory and roughly the solve time by four. The Domain tab shows the node count, the memory and the estimated solve time as you change the cell.
  • Field interpolation. Cubic (the default) builds a smooth potential from the nodal values and their derivatives, and the force is its exact gradient, so energy is conserved to the precision of the integrator: best for analyzers, mirrors and long flights. Linear interpolates the nodal field; on very coarse grids (fewer than about 16 cells per aperture radius) it can give smaller focal errors. In cells cut by an electrode both modes use the nodal field on the particle's own side of the electrode only.
  • Trajectory accuracy. The tracer settings offer four levels:

  • Energy diagnostics. In static fields the simulator compares each particle's total energy (kinetic plus potential) at the end with its value at the start, and adds up the difference between the change in kinetic energy and the work done by the field. The Summary tab counts the particles whose energy error exceeds 10−6 of the energy scale (10−4 with linear interpolation). Warnings usually mean that a trajectory passed through cells cut by an electrode, where the field is only first-order accurate, or crossed a mesh at a coarse tolerance; a smaller cell or a tighter tolerance removes them.
  • Accuracy check. The button in the Summary tab solves the fields again with half the cell size, runs the first 200 particles again with a 100 times tighter tolerance, and reports how much each result changed. When twice the cell size also divides the domain it solves once more with that cell, so that the observed order of convergence p can be measured, and estimates the remaining grid error by Richardson extrapolation: for the current cell size it is the change × 2p/(2p − 1), for the finer one the change/(2p − 1). The order is clamped to between 1 and 2, and taken as 1 (the cautious choice) when it cannot be measured. Smooth geometries converge with p ≈ 2; electrodes with sharp corners give p ≈ 4/3, so the error of the current result is several times the change.
  • Solver tolerance. Each basis field is solved until the estimated error is below 10−10 V per volt applied. The fields pill in the top bar shows the method used, the iterations and the estimated error.

How it works

The methods are standard ones from the open literature; the references are listed at the end of this page.

The field

The potential obeys Laplace's equation, ∇²φ = 0 (there is no space charge in this version). It is discretized with finite differences on a square grid. Where a grid line meets an electrode between two nodes, the difference formula uses the true, shorter distance to the surface (Shortley and Weller, 1938), which keeps the scheme second-order accurate at curved and oblique boundaries. In cylindrical symmetry the axis row uses a finite-volume form.

The equations are solved by geometric multigrid: V-cycles with two red-black Gauss–Seidel sweeps before and after each coarse-grid correction (Briggs, Henson and McCormick; Trottenberg, Oosterlee and Schüller). Every coarse grid is built from the exact geometry at its own spacing, so thin plates and meshes do not disappear on coarse levels. If multigrid stalls, the solver switches automatically to BiCGSTAB (van der Vorst, 1992) preconditioned by a multigrid cycle, and then to successive over-relaxation with Carré's adaptive choice of the over-relaxation factor (Carré, 1961; Hageman and Young). It stops when the estimated algebraic error and the scaled residual are both below the tolerance.

From the grid to a force

In cells away from electrodes the potential is interpolated by a bicubic Hermite surface built from the nodal potential and its derivatives (estimated with fourth-order differences), so both the potential and the field are continuous and the force is the exact gradient of the interpolated potential. In cells cut by an electrode or a mesh the nodal field is interpolated bilinearly, using only nodes on the particle's own side of the surface and extrapolating from them where a corner lies behind it; a field is never averaged across an electrode. Ideal analytic fields (uniform, quadrupole, coaxial, spherical) can be added on top of the solved field.

Particle motion

Particles follow the relativistic Lorentz-force equations, written for the proper velocity γv. The default integrator is the adaptive Dormand–Prince 5(4) Runge–Kutta method (Dormand and Prince, 1980) with Hairer's continuous output (Hairer, Nørsett and Wanner), which also locates events inside a step. For long flights in magnetic fields the relativistic Boris method (Boris, 1970), with the exact rotation angle (Zenitani and Umeda, 2018), conserves speed in a pure magnetic field to rounding error (Qin and coworkers, 2013). With the exact rotation angle the drift in crossed electric and magnetic fields comes out too slow by about θ²/12 (θ = the rotation angle per step, 2×10−4 at 128 steps per turn), so the automatic choice uses Boris only when there is no electric field; with both fields it keeps the adaptive Runge–Kutta method, which is slower for long flights but follows the drift to the set tolerance. Steps never cross a magnetic field edge or a corner of a voltage pulse.

Electrode hits are found by testing straight sub-chords of every step against the exact geometry wherever the step comes near an electrode, then bisecting to 10−9 mm. Recording planes are located by root finding on the continuous output, and the events of a step are processed in time order. Magnetic fields come from uniform fields, hard-edged field regions and circular current loops (complete elliptic integrals, after Simpson and coworkers, 2001, and Abramowitz and Stegun §17.6).

Random starting conditions come from a counter-based generator: particle number i of a source always gets the same numbers, so a run gives identical results however it is split among the browser's worker threads.

Analysis

Lens properties follow DiChio, Natali and Kuyatt (1974): rays parallel to the axis are traced through the lens in both directions, the focal points and principal planes are found from their straight asymptotes, and the values are fitted in the ray height and extrapolated to the axis. Arrival-time peaks give the resolving power t/(2 FWHM); because a peak with a steep edge (common after an ion mirror) has no stable half-maximum width, the TOF tab also gives t/(2 × 2.3548 σ) from the rms width, the robust value that Scan & tune uses, and the narrowest interval holding 76.1% of the arrivals. The FWHM comes from a smoothed histogram and is marked unreliable when, over a bootstrap of the arrivals, it follows the bin width or is statistically unstable; even when it is marked reliable, the FWHM of a sharply peaked (cusp-shaped) distribution is biased high by up to about a quarter, so its resolving power reads low and the rms-based and 76% values are the ones to compare. Detector images give rms spot sizes, waists (found per species, so a mass spectrometer reports the image of each species rather than the crossing point of their lines) and emittances. Equipotential lines are traced with marching squares, the two-dimensional form of Lorensen and Cline's method; the potential maps use a perceptually uniform map in the style of viridis (van der Walt and Smith), the color-vision-deficiency-friendly cividis map (Nuñez, Anderton and Renslow, 2018) and a blue-white-red diverging map after Moreland (2009).

Validation

The Benchmarks tab runs a suite of test cases in this browser and compares each result with a published value or an exact formula. There are 38 gates (all must pass before the beta can be released), 3 informational comparisons with published tables whose geometry is not fully known, and 4 planned cases for features still to come. Every case builds its own setup and runs through the same code as your projects. Run quick takes a few seconds on a desktop computer; Run full uses finer grids and takes longer. Each row shows the reference with its source, the computed value, the deviation, the tolerance and the typical deviation found when the tolerances were calibrated; Open setup loads the case as a project.

Every tolerance except the printed-digits ones is at least twice the deviation measured in calibration runs; digits checks compare with the value as printed, so their margin can be smaller, and where an exact value is available they also compare with it at a tight tolerance. Where a tolerance is written as two numbers, the first applies to the quick level and the second to the full level. "Digits" means agreement to half a unit in the last digit printed by the source.

Presets

Einzel lens
Three tubes focus a parallel beam; the waist lies before the near-axis focus because of spherical aberration.
Two-tube immersion lens
The deep-gap geometry of the NBS lens studies; the Lens tab reproduces the published focal properties.
Two-aperture lens
Two thin apertures focus while they accelerate the beam tenfold.
Parallel-plate mirror
A uniform retarding field behind a mesh; return points and depth match closed forms.
127° cylindrical deflector
A cylindrical energy filter: rays leaving a point refocus after 127°; the exit slit selects the pass energy.
Hemispherical analyzer
Electrons of 99, 100 and 101 eV land about 2 mm apart after a 180° turn.
Reflectron TOF
A gridded ion mirror compensates the energy spread of a single-field source and separates m/z 100 from 101.
RF quadrupole mass filter
Hyperbolic rods with RF and DC voltages pass m/z 100 and reject 90 and 110.
Paul trap
A ring and end caps with RF trap ions above the low-mass cut-off.
90° magnetic sector
A sector magnet focuses a fan of ions and separates neighboring masses by 1 mm.
Velocity-map imaging lens
Three plates in the style of Eppink and Parker (1997) image equal velocities to equal points; the dimensions are generic, not those of the paper.

Limitations

  • Two dimensions. Geometries are planar (infinitely long along z) or cylindrically symmetric. Three-dimensional grids are planned.
  • Quasistatic fields. The field follows the electrode voltages instantly, which is accurate when the device is much smaller than the wavelength; the simulator warns when 2πf L/c exceeds 0.05 for the domain length L.
  • No space charge in this version: particles do not repel each other and do not change the field, so high-current beams are not modeled.
  • Sharp magnetic edges. Magnetic field regions end abruptly; fringe fields are not modeled. Current loops are exact.
  • Field-free drift outside the grid. Beyond the solved region the electric field is taken as zero.
  • Ideal surfaces. Electrodes absorb the particles that hit them (no secondary electrons, reflection or charging). A mesh is an ideal equipotential sheet with a transmission probability; the small lenses formed by real mesh holes are not modeled.
  • Phones open a viewer mode with smaller limits (grids up to 400,000 nodes and 20,000 particles); geometry editing needs a larger screen. On desktops grids up to 4 million nodes and a million particles per run are allowed, with a confirmation above about 5 seconds of computing.

Privacy

Everything runs in your browser; no project, result or file is uploaded. Your current project and the ten most recent ones are kept in this browser's local storage. A share link carries the whole project after the # in the address, a part that browsers do not send to the server; anyone with the link can open a copy. An optional cache of solved fields (off by default) uses the browser's own database and can be cleared at any time.
