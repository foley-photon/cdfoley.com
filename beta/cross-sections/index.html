Absorption Cross-Section Atlas

Gas-phase absorption cross sections σ(λ) of about 1000 molecules, from the vacuum ultraviolet to the near infrared, overlaid from five public collections on one plot. Read σ at your laser line, see which molecules absorb there, interpolate between measurement temperatures, convert units, and turn a cross section into an absorbance, an optical depth, or the fraction of molecules a pulse photolyzes.

Key terms
Cross section σ
The effective area a molecule presents to a photon: the fraction of light absorbed by a thin layer is σ times the number of molecules per unit area. Units cm² molecule⁻¹.
Molar absorptivity ε
The same quantity per mole and in base 10, as used in solution spectroscopy: ε = σNA/(1000 ln 10) in L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹.
Optical depth τ
τ = σnL for number density n and path L; the transmitted fraction is e−τ. Absorbance (base 10) is τ/ln 10.
Absorption coefficient α
What a gas in a mixture takes from a beam per unit path: α = σ × mole fraction × number density of the mixture. The light falls to 1/e over the path 1/α, the 1/e length.
Quantum yield φ
The fraction of absorbed photons that lead to a given product. Absorption alone does not say what happens next.
Recommended
A dataset an evaluation panel (NASA JPL or IUPAC) chose as the best available at the time of the evaluation.

Beta, in review. Values are shown as each source gives them; check the original paper before you rely on a number.

How it is tested, and where to report a problem

The data archive behind this page is checksummed file by file, the Bremen and CfA references were checked against Crossref (the 2013 Mainz snapshot carries no DOIs), and spot checks compare the archived datasets at well-known wavelengths (ozone at the 253.65 nm mercury line, among others) with established values; the unit conversions use CODATA 2022 constants. The arithmetic is tested against hand-worked values in the tool’s repository, and the site’s copy of the data is checked file by file before each new release of the page code goes live. There is no self-test tab yet. Found a problem? Open an issue at github.com/foley-photon/cross-section-atlas, or use the email link at the foot of the page.

    How one dataset is chosen, and what the ranking can tell you
    • One dataset stands for each molecule. Among its datasets with a value at the wavelength, the page takes, in this order: measured values before upper limits; optical absorption before electron energy loss spectra, which have low resolution; points no more than 5 nm apart around the wavelength; a spectrum before a single measured point or a list of band maxima; the natural sample before a single isotopologue; room temperature (280 to 300 K) before no stated temperature before any other temperature (the nearest to 296 K first); a dataset an evaluation recommended; the newest; the one with the most points.
    • The Note column says when the choice is a weak one: an upper limit, a value measured at one wavelength only, an electron energy loss spectrum, a value interpolated across a gap of more than 5 nm, a single isotopologue (named after the reference), a temperature below 250 K or above 350 K, or a spectrum that is structured at a preset line: “σ spans a factor of 63 within ±0.25 nm” means the dataset’s own points that close to the line differ by that much, so the value depends on the resolution and on exactly where the line sits. Treat such a row as a pointer to the data, not as a number to use.
    • One value is listed and not counted: O₂ above 245 nm. The recommended O₂ table ends at 245 nm. Beyond it the rule lands on the one series that runs on to 440 nm (Bogumil et al., 2003). At 248.4 nm that series is 7 times above the two older tables, at 266 nm about 100 times above the high-resolution spectrum, and past 294 nm nothing else is there to compare. The row says “not a firm value” and stays out of the sum for air.
    • Left out: line-intensity lists, which are not σ against wavelength, and values below 10⁻⁴⁰ cm², which are numerical underflow in the source files.
    • At a preset line the value is the point value of that dataset’s full spectrum, the number the laser-line table shows for it. Between the lines the table uses a coarse list, the mean of σ over 2 nm: good for a ranking, not for a calculation.
    • The ranking compares unlike things: datasets of different age, resolution and temperature. Neighbors within a factor of two are a tie. In banded spectra (O₂ below 200 nm, NO, SO₂, NH₃) the value at one wavelength depends on the resolution and on exactly where the line sits; at a preset line the Note column says so where the chosen dataset shows it. Between the lines the 2 nm means average over such structure and carry no such note.
    • σ is per molecule. How much light a gas takes also depends on how much of it is there: a major gas with a small σ can absorb more than a trace gas with a large one. The air view therefore weights each σ by the gas’s share of the air, α = σ × mole fraction × number density of air at 1 atm and 296 K, and opens in the order of α. The shares and their sources are in the fold under the table. For another mixture, put σ, the partial pressure and the path into the calculator below.
    • The line above the air table names the gas with the largest α, and a second gas within a factor of two of it, and adds up α over the gases with a value. An upper limit is listed with ≤ (and ≥ for its 1/e length) and left out of the sum. So is the O₂ value above 245 nm; the line says what it would have given. A row with any other note, and every 2 nm mean, shows α and the length with ≈, to two figures. Where a flagged value leads or carries a tenth of the sum or more, or the values are 2 nm means, the figures are marked ≈, the line says why, and it gives the sum without the flagged values. The line says a gas “takes the most light” only when N₂, O₂ and H₂O all have a value in the sum and the leading value is not flagged. Otherwise it says “has the largest listed value” and names the main gases that are missing.
    • Not listed means no data at this wavelength in these five collections. It does not mean the molecule is transparent there.

    How it works

    • Values between points are linear interpolations of the tabulated data. A value at a laser line with a width averages σ over a Gaussian of that full width at half maximum. If the data are tabulated more coarsely than the line, the tool says so: the average is then only an interpolation, and narrow structure (the bands of O₂, NO or SO₂) can make it wrong by large factors. A spectrum with more than 2000 points is thinned for the web to 2000 that keep its shape on a log scale; between the kept points, in banded or noisy regions, σ can differ from the source by large factors. At the preset laser lines the table therefore gives the point value of the full spectrum, which the build stores; anywhere else, and for a line with a width, it says when a value comes from a thinned spectrum.
    • Who absorbs here? The build picks one dataset per molecule for each wavelength by a fixed rule (measured before upper limit, optical before electron energy loss, points no more than 5 nm apart, spectrum before single point or band maxima, natural sample before isotopologue, room temperature, recommended, newest, most points) and stores its σ at each preset laser line, read from the full spectrum, and its mean over each 2 nm bin. With each value at a line it stores how far σ ranges among that dataset’s points within 0.25 nm of the line (largest over smallest, where three or more points lie there and the ratio is 2 or more). The page sorts those values. It compares one dataset per molecule, not a consensus value.
    • Who absorbs here, in air. For each gas of the air view the page multiplies σ by the gas’s mole fraction x and by the number density of air, n = p/kBT = 2.479 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ at 1 atm and 296 K (ideal gas): α = σxn, shown per meter, and the 1/e length is 1/α. Dry-air shares come from the 1976 U.S. Standard Atmosphere (N₂, O₂, H₂, and typical clean-air values near the surface for CO, O₃, NO₂, NO, SO₂ and NH₃) and from NOAA’s global surface means of mid 2026 (CO₂, CH₄, N₂O). Water vapor comes from the relative humidity: x = RH × psat/p, with psat = 2783 Pa at 296 K from the Antoine equation of the NIST Chemistry WebBook, and every dry-air share is multiplied by 1 − x. The sum of α runs over the gases with a value at the wavelength; upper limits are left out of it, and so is O₂ above 245 nm, where its datasets do not agree.
    • Units. ε (base 10) = σNA/(1000 ln 10); ε (base e) = σNA/1000; k (cm⁻¹ atm⁻¹ at 273.15 K) = σn₀, with Loschmidt's constant n₀ = 2.686 780 111 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ (CODATA 2022). Physicists' units: 1 Mb = 10⁻¹⁸ cm², 1 barn = 10⁻²⁴ cm², 1 Å² = 10⁻¹⁶ cm², a₀² = 2.800 285 × 10⁻¹⁷ cm², and the oscillator-strength density df/dE = σ/(2π²reħc), so σ in Mb ≈ 109.76 × df/dE in eV⁻¹ (Berkowitz's convention for VUV photoabsorption).
    • Beer–Lambert. n = p/kBT, τ = σnL, transmitted fraction e−τ, absorbance τ/ln 10.
    • Photolysis per pulse. Photon fluence Φ = F/(hc/λ); fraction excited 1 − e−σΦ, fraction photolyzed 1 − e−σΦφ, for an optically thin sample and one-photon absorption. The saturation fluence hν/σ is where a fraction 1 − 1/e of the molecules has absorbed. The photolyzed fraction assumes that a molecule which absorbs and does not dissociate is back in the ground state within the pulse; if it is not, the fraction is φ(1 − e−σΦ), the same at low fluence and smaller near saturation.
    • Temperature. Where one series (one paper's files of the same resolution, pressure and version) was measured at several temperatures, σ at an intermediate temperature is interpolated linearly in T from the same values the table shows, never extrapolated.

    Worked example

    The page as it opens: ozone at the KrF line, 248.4 nm, then 1 Torr of ozone at 296 K over 10 cm and a pulse of 10 mJ/cm².

    • The first row of the laser-line table is the recommended dataset, Sander et al. (2011) at 293–298 K: σ = 1.079 × 10⁻¹⁷ cm² at 248.4 nm (40257.6 cm⁻¹, 4.9913 eV). The other two datasets shown give 1.064 and 1.045 × 10⁻¹⁷ cm², 1 to 3 % lower; that spread is how far the sources disagree here. Press “use ›” on the first row.
    • Number density: n = p/kBT = 133.322 Pa / (1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K × 296 K) = 3.262 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³.
    • Optical depth: τ = σnL = 1.079 × 10⁻¹⁷ × 3.262 × 10¹⁶ × 10 = 3.52, an absorbance of 1.529. The cell transmits e−3.52 = 2.96 % and absorbs 97.04 %.
    • One pulse: a 248.4 nm photon carries hc/λ = 7.997 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, so 10 mJ/cm² is Φ = 1.250 × 10¹⁶ photons cm⁻². Then σΦ = 0.1349 and the fraction excited is 1 − e−0.1349 = 0.1262. With φ = 1 the same fraction is photolyzed.
    • Saturation fluence: hν/σ = 7.997 × 10⁻¹⁹ J / 1.079 × 10⁻¹⁷ cm² = 74.11 mJ cm⁻², so this pulse is well below saturation.
    • Who absorbs here? At 248.4 nm six of the thirteen gases of air have data. Per molecule (“By σ per molecule”) O₃ leads with the same 1.079 × 10⁻¹⁷ cm², then SO₂ (6.575 × 10⁻²⁰), NO₂ (1.642 × 10⁻²⁰), N₂O (2.285 × 10⁻²⁴), O₂ (1.984 × 10⁻²⁴) and CO₂ (3.601 × 10⁻²⁵). Two rows carry a note. CO₂: its table has points 36.3 nm apart there, so the value is an interpolation across that gap. O₂: not a firm value. No table reaches 248.4 nm for N₂, H₂O, CH₄, H₂, CO, NO or NH₃. “All molecules” lists 568 of the 1004.
    • In air, as the panel opens: 1 atm and 296 K give n = 101 325 Pa / (1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K × 296 K) = 2.479 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³. At 50 % relative humidity water vapor is 0.5 × 2783 Pa / 101 325 Pa = 1.37 % of the air, so every dry-air share is multiplied by 0.9863.
    • O₃: 40 ppb × 0.9863 = 39.5 ppb, so α = 1.079 × 10⁻¹⁷ × 3.945 × 10⁻⁸ × 2.479 × 10¹⁹ = 1.055 × 10⁻⁵ cm⁻¹ = 0.00105 m⁻¹, a 1/e length of 948 m. The other four in the sum are far below: CO₂ ≈ 3.8 × 10⁻⁷ m⁻¹ (420 ppm; its row is flagged, so its figures are marked ≈), SO₂ 1.61 × 10⁻⁷ (986 ppt), NO₂ 4.01 × 10⁻⁸ (986 ppt) and N₂O 1.90 × 10⁻⁹ (335 ppb). The five add up to α = 0.00106 m⁻¹, a 1/e length of 947 m. The flagged CO₂ value is 0.04 % of that sum, so the figures are not marked as rough.
    • O₂ is the open question at this line. Its share is 20.95 % × 0.9863 = 20.7 % of the air, so its listed value would give α = 1.984 × 10⁻²⁴ × 0.2066 × 2.479 × 10¹⁹ = 1.016 × 10⁻⁵ cm⁻¹ ≈ 0.001 m⁻¹, as much as ozone. Load O₂ and compare its datasets at 248.4 nm: that series says 1.98 × 10⁻²⁴ cm², two older tables say 2.96 and 2.80 × 10⁻²⁵, and a high-resolution spectrum between its bands says 2.9 × 10⁻²⁸. So the page lists the O₂ row last, marks it, and leaves it out of the sum. With 2.96 × 10⁻²⁵ cm² instead, O₂ would add 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ m⁻¹ and the 1/e length would be about 830 m.
    • The line above the table therefore says that O₃ has the largest listed value, not that it takes the most light: O₂ is left out, and no table reaches 248.4 nm for N₂ or H₂O. The sum has no scattering in it either.

    Assumptions and limits

    • Data are shown as their sources give them, including the wavelength scale (air or vacuum, where stated) and the spectral resolution of the measurement. Above 200 nm, air and vacuum wavelengths differ by about 0.075 nm at 250 nm and 0.11 nm at 400 nm. The wavenumber and photon-energy axes are 10⁷/λ and hc/λ of the wavelength as given, so an air wavelength lands about 3 parts in 10⁴ high (12 cm⁻¹ at 250 nm).
    • Cross sections of banded spectra depend on resolution, temperature and pressure; compare like with like.
    • Only tables of σ against wavelength in cm² molecule⁻¹ are shown. Files of another kind are left out: σ against temperature at one wavelength, fit parameters, temperature gradients, the O₂–O₂ collision-induced absorption (in cm⁵ molecule⁻², which needs the square of the O₂ density), and data in relative units. Files marked as upper limits are shown and labeled as such.
    • On a log axis, points more than 12 decades below the largest value shown are left out of the plot; some modeled spectra run down to 10⁻³⁰⁰ cm² in their band wings.
    • “Who absorbs here?” picks its one dataset per molecule from file names and point spacing, not from a review of the data. The Note column flags the weak cases it can see; it cannot see a table that is simply wrong. One such case was found by comparing datasets and is named on the page: O₂ above 245 nm, which is listed and left out of the sum for air. There may be others. Before using a value, load the molecule and compare its datasets on the plot.
    • A file of band maxima is a list of separate points. The laser-line table and “Who absorbs here?” read it point by point (the nearest listed point within 1 nm); the plot still joins the points with a line.
    • “Who absorbs here?” covers the molecules and wavelengths these five collections cover. A molecule with no table at a wavelength is missing from the list whether or not it absorbs there, and the gases of air that no collection tabulates (argon, for one) never appear.
    • The sum for air is not the extinction of air. It has only the gases with a table at the wavelength, no argon, and no scattering: Rayleigh scattering by the air and scattering by aerosols take light too. It also has no collision-induced absorption (the O₂–O₂ files are left out, above), which adds to the oxygen continuum at atmospheric pressure. Above 245 nm it has no oxygen at all, because the O₂ datasets there differ by a factor of 7 at 248.4 nm and by far more beyond. Whatever O₂ absorbs in that range is missing from the sum.
    • The mole fractions are one typical set, listed with their sources in the fold under the air table. CO₂, CH₄ and N₂O are global surface means of mid 2026. CO, O₃, NO₂, NO, SO₂ and NH₃ are the 1976 Standard Atmosphere’s typical values for clean air near the surface; in city air, indoors or near a discharge they can be many times higher. α scales with the share. Only the relative humidity can be changed; the pressure and temperature of the air are fixed at 1 atm and 296 K, and the Antoine fit for water holds from 273 to 303 K.
    • In air, σ is used as tabulated, most often near 298 K and at the resolution and pressure of the measurement. In banded regions (O₂ below 200 nm, NO, SO₂) one σ at one wavelength does not give the transmission of a real laser line through air: the bands are narrower than most tables resolve, and their strongest parts saturate first. At 193.3 nm the O₂ value sits on the flank of a line: the dataset’s points within 0.25 nm span a factor of 63, so the row is flagged and the figures for air are marked ≈. The flag only sees structure that the chosen dataset resolves, and only at the preset lines.
    • Absorption is not photolysis: what happens after the photon is absorbed is set by the quantum yields.
