Glazing Performance Calculator (Open-Data Beta)

Does a glazing model built only from openly licensed data match the current calculator and the manufacturers' published numbers? Build single, double, triple or vacuum glazing and see both models side by side: measured glass optical constants, thin-film low-e coatings calibrated to published datasheets, and nine measured IGDB products. Everything runs in your browser.

Key terms
U-factor
The heat that flows through each square meter of glazing for each degree of temperature difference between inside and outside, in W/m²·K (US units: Btu/h·ft²·°F; 1 Btu/h·ft²·°F = 5.678 W/m²·K). Lower insulates better. The R-value, next, is the same property turned upside down.
R-value
The resistance of the glazing to heat flow: the U-factor turned upside down, so a higher R-value insulates better, and halving the U-factor doubles the R-value. US practice writes it as “R-4”, in h·ft²·°F/Btu. The SI value, in m²·K/W, is often called RSI and is about 5.7 times smaller. The R-values here are center-of-glass values from indoor air to outdoor air, so they include the thin layers of air on the two outer glass faces (the air films).
Center of glass
The middle of the glazing, away from the edge seal and the frame. These results are center-of-glass values; the whole-window U-factor on an NFRC label also counts the frame and the edges and comes out higher, and the whole-window R-value lower.
SHGC
Solar heat gain coefficient: the fraction of the sunlight striking the glass that ends up as heat indoors, transmitted directly or absorbed in the panes and released inward. It runs from 0 to 1: low values keep rooms cool, high values help passive solar heating.
Visible transmittance (VT)
The fraction of daylight that passes through, weighted by the eye’s sensitivity. Clear double glazing transmits about 80 %.
Low-e coating
A thin, transparent coating of doped tin oxide or silver that reflects the long-wave infrared radiated by room-temperature surfaces. Its emissivity, from 0 to 1, says how strongly a surface radiates heat: 0.84 for bare glass, about 0.15 for a tin-oxide hard coat, and about 0.015–0.07 for silver coatings.
Surface numbers
Glass surfaces are counted from outdoors: a double unit has surfaces #1 (outdoor face) to #4 (indoor face), so a coating on #2 is on the inner face of the outer pane, inside the sealed gap.
Gas fill
Argon, krypton, or xenon conducts heat less well than air, so a filled gap insulates better. Here a gas fill is 90 % gas and 10 % air.
Vacuum insulating glass (VIG)
Two panes a fraction of a millimeter apart with the air pumped out, held apart by a grid of small pillars and sealed at the edges. Heat crosses only by radiation and through the pillars.
Classic and open-data models
Two ways to make each pane’s spectra. The classic model is the current calculator’s: smooth parametric curves for each glass and coating. The open-data model computes the spectra from published optical constants: measured glass, and a stack of thin films for each coating. Both feed the same optics and the same thermal model.
Measured record (IGDB)
The measured transmittance and reflectance spectra of one product, from the International Glazing Database that LBNL maintains. Nine records are available here. A pane that uses one is identical in both models.
Calibrated and held out
A calibration row is a published datasheet configuration that a coating’s layer thicknesses were fitted to. A held-out row was not used in the fit, so it is an independent check of the model.
ASTM E891 and G173
Two reference solar spectra used to weight the solar quantities. E891 (direct normal) is the one in LBNL’s NFRC calculations, so it is the one behind manufacturers’ datasheets. G173 is the newer reference, with more ultraviolet and visible light: with it, SHGC for the default unit comes out 0.020 higher.
Datasheet tolerance
How far a model value may be from a published value and still count as a match. For a calibration row: 1 point of visible transmittance, 0.015 in SHGC and 0.01 Btu/h·ft²·°F in U-factor. Held-out rows are allowed 2 points, 0.02 and 0.015. In the comparison tables, ✓ is within the tolerance, ! is within twice the tolerance, and ✗ is outside twice the tolerance.

    How the open-data model works

    The open-data model replaces the current calculator's parametric glass and coating curves with physics computed from published optical constants. Everything else is shared: both models feed the same multiple-reflection optics, the same ISO 15099 center-of-glass thermal model and the same NFRC 100 and 200 conditions, so the two columns of the results differ only in how each pane's spectra are made. The thermal model gives the center-of-glass U-factor; the R-value shown beside it is 1 divided by that U-factor, the same result in another form.

    Glass. Each pane uses the refractive index \(n(\lambda)\) and extinction coefficient \(k(\lambda)\) that Rubin measured for soda-lime float glass: clear, low-iron and three tints (green, gray and bronze). The internal transmittance of a pane of thickness \(d\) is

    \[ \tau(\lambda) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{4\pi k d}{\lambda}\right), \]

    and the two air–glass surfaces add Fresnel reflection, so any thickness can be modeled. Against the measured IGDB clear-glass records shipped with this page, the Rubin glass is within about 0.1 point in visible transmittance and 0.5 to 0.8 point in solar transmittance.

    Coatings. A low-e coating is a stack of thin films a few nanometers to a few hundred nanometers thick: silver for infrared reflection, dielectric layers (titanium dioxide, silicon nitride) to suppress visible reflection, and a thin nickel blocker. Its optics follow from the characteristic matrix of each layer at normal incidence,

    \[ \begin{bmatrix} B \\ C \end{bmatrix} = \left( \prod_j \begin{bmatrix} \cos\delta_j & i\sin\delta_j/N_j \\ i N_j \sin\delta_j & \cos\delta_j \end{bmatrix} \right) \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ n_s \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \delta_j = \frac{2\pi N_j d_j}{\lambda}, \]

    with \( R = |(n_0 B - C)/(n_0 B + C)|^2 \) and \( T = 4 n_0 n_s/|n_0 B + C|^2 \), where \(N_j = n_j - i k_j\) is the complex index of layer \(j\). The optical constants are public-domain (CC0) data from the refractiveindex.info database; the pyrolytic hard coat uses published Drude–Lorentz models of tin oxide and fluorine-doped tin oxide.

    The coated pane. The film and the thick glass combine incoherently:

    \[ T = \frac{T_c\,(1-R_0)\,\tau}{1 - R_g R_0 \tau^2}, \]

    where \(T_c\) is the film's transmittance, \(R_0\) the bare glass surface's reflectance, and \( R_g \) the film's reflectance seen from inside the glass. The classic model reuses the air-side value for \(R_g\). Within the visible band the two differ by up to about 14 points for the double-silver stack and 5 points for the triple-silver stack, mostly near the ends of the band, and by less than 1 point for the single-silver stack.

    Calibration. Four generic stacks (hard coat, single, double and triple silver) had their layer thicknesses fitted offline only to published datasheet values: visible transmittance, reflectance, SHGC, UV transmittance (computed as LBNL WINDOW computes it for datasheets) and, for the hard coat, solar transmittance. Color windows taken from the manufacturers' appearance statements keep the reflected color neutral. The emissivity of the coated face is then inferred from the published air and argon U-factors. The datasheets give U-factors, not R-values, so the R-values in the comparison panel are converted from them. A fitted stack is an effective model of a coating family, not any manufacturer's actual design. The panel “How the open coatings are built” shows every layer and every residual, and the comparison panel shows the datasheet rows the fit never saw.

    Measured products. The nine named products are unmodified International Glazing Database (IGDB) records that LBNL distributes in its open-source pyWinCalc and Windows-CalcEngine projects. Your browser reads each file when you first select the product and resamples its spectra to the calculator's 5 nm grid for that calculation only; a measured pane is identical in both models.

    Solar spectrum. The solar quantities (solar transmittance, SHGC, UV) depend on the solar spectrum used to weight them. This page defaults to the ASTM E891 direct-normal spectrum, which LBNL's NFRC calculations in Windows-CalcEngine use, so its numbers are comparable with manufacturers' datasheets computed in LBNL WINDOW; the datasheet comparisons always use it. ASTM G173 (direct plus circumsolar), the newer reference spectrum, has more ultraviolet and visible light; with it, SHGC comes out about 0.01–0.03 higher for silver low-e coatings.

    Worked example

    The default unit: 6 mm clear glass with the double-silver coating on #2, a 12.7 mm gap of 90 % argon, and 6 mm clear glass, 1 m tall, with the ASTM E891 spectrum and both models shown.

    • Open-data model: \(U = 1.40\) W/m²·K (0.247 Btu/h·ft²·°F), SHGC = 0.394 and VT = 69.6 %. These are the large figures on the tiles.
    • R-value: from the full-precision U-factor, \(R = 1/1.4048 = 0.7118\) m²·K/W, shown as 0.712. In US units, \(5.678263 \times 0.7118 = 4.04\) h·ft²·°F/Btu, or R-4.04.
    • Classic model: \(U = 1.37\) W/m²·K (0.241 Btu/h·ft²·°F), R-4.16 (0.732 m²·K/W), SHGC = 0.368 and VT = 73.8 %. These are the small “classic” figures on the tiles.
    • Δ column (open − classic): +0.039 W/m²·K in U-factor (+0.007 Btu/h·ft²·°F), +0.026 in SHGC and −4.15 points in VT.

    The U-factor difference is inside the datasheet tolerance of 0.01 Btu/h·ft²·°F. The SHGC and VT differences are outside 0.015 and 1 point, and the note under the tiles says so. The open coating has a higher emissivity (0.042 against 0.025), which raises U. Its thin-film stack also absorbs more sunlight in the outer pane (35.3 % against 27.6 %) and reflects less (27.1 % against 37.1 %), which raises SHGC. The two models are furthest apart in UV transmittance: 16.6 % against 38.5 %.

    The nearest published configuration is Vitro Solarban 60 on #2, 6/13/6 mm, a held-out row in the comparison panel. The datasheet gives VT 70 %, SHGC 0.39 and an argon U-factor of 0.24 Btu/h·ft²·°F. With the 13 mm gap of that row, the open-data model gives 69.6 %, 0.398 and 0.248, all within the held-out tolerance. The classic model gives 73.8 %, 0.372 and 0.241: its VT is 3.8 points high, outside the 2-point tolerance but within twice the tolerance.

    Assumptions and limits

    • Center-of-glass values at normal incidence only. Frames, edge spacers, angular optics, laminates, films and shades are not modeled.
    • The R-value is 1 divided by the center-of-glass U-factor. It is an air-to-air value that includes the indoor and outdoor air films at NFRC 100 winter conditions. It is not the material-only R-value printed on insulation, and a whole window, with its frame and edge, has a lower R-value.
    • Nickel stands in for the nickel-chromium blockers used in production, for which no open optical data exist.
    • The hard coat's emissivity comes from its published U-factors, not from the film's infrared optics.
    • Some manufacturers' clear glass is clearer than Rubin's: Cardinal's 6 mm clear double glazing reads about 1.8 points of visible transmittance higher than the open model.
    • The color windows used in the calibration are published descriptors (“neutral”), not measured color coordinates.
    • The thermal model is the same as the current calculator's.
