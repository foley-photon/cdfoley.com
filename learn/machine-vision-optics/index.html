Machine Vision Optics for Inspection

A camera inspection system is a measuring instrument, and the defect it must find decides how to build it. This article explains how to choose the camera, lens, and lighting so that the smallest defect that matters shows up clearly, stays in focus, and is not smeared by motion, and how to prove the system works before you trust it. Each section gives the practical rule first, then the equations.

Key terms
Field of view (FOV)
The width and height of the area on the part that one image covers.
Pixel size on the part
The length of the part that one pixel covers: the field of view divided by the number of pixels across it. In this article’s main example it is 36.6 µm.
Magnification
The size of the image on the sensor divided by the true size on the part. Machine vision setups usually work well below 1; the examples here use about 0.1.
f-number
The aperture setting on the lens, written f/2.8, f/8, and so on. A larger number means a smaller opening: more depth of field, but less light and more blur from diffraction.
Depth of field
How far the part can move toward or away from the camera and still look sharp enough, judged against a blur limit you choose, usually one or two pixels.
Contrast and MTF
Contrast is how strongly a defect or a fine pattern differs in brightness from its surroundings. The modulation transfer function (MTF) is the share of that contrast the lens keeps: close to 100 % for coarse detail, falling to zero for detail too fine to pass.
Telecentric lens
A lens whose magnification does not change when the part moves closer or farther, so a measured size does not depend on the part’s height. Its front lens must be larger than the field of view.
Bright field and dark field
In bright field the camera sees light reflected straight off the surface, so defects that scatter light look dark. In dark field it sees only scattered light, so defects look bright on a dark background.
Escape and false call
An escape is a defective part the system passes. A false call is a good part it rejects or sends to manual review.
Probability of detection (POD)
The share of defects of a given size that the system finds, measured on parts whose defects are known in advance.

Start from the defect

Write down four things before you pick hardware:

  1. The smallest defect that matters, as a size and a type: a scratch, chip, particle, bubble, stain, or dimensional error.
  2. The contrast mechanism. Does the defect absorb, scatter, refract, change color, or change height? This decides the lighting more than anything else.
  3. Throughput: part size, line speed, and the time available per part.
  4. The cost of each error type. An escape (a missed defect) and a false call (a good part rejected or sent to manual review) usually have very different costs, and the system’s threshold will trade one for the other.

Everything that follows is about getting enough pixels and enough contrast on that defect, in focus, without blur, every time.

The resolution chain: pixels, optics, and contrast

Three things limit how small a defect the system can see: how finely the pixels sample the part, how much fine detail the lens passes, and whether the defect stands out from the noise. The weakest of the three sets the limit.

Sampling. The size of one pixel projected onto the object is \(p_\text{obj} = \text{FOV}/N_\text{pixels}\). A 150 mm field imaged onto 4096 pixels gives 36.6 µm per pixel. The finest pattern the sensor can record has one light and one dark line per two pixels: the Nyquist frequency, \(\nu_N = 1/(2p)\) for a pixel pitch p (the spacing of the pixels on the sensor), or 145 line pairs per millimeter (lp/mm) for 3.45 µm pixels. Finer periodic patterns alias: they show up as false, coarser patterns.

Optics. Even a perfect lens blurs fine detail, because light diffracts at its aperture. The modulation transfer function (MTF) gives the share of a pattern’s contrast that the lens keeps at each spatial frequency. For a diffraction-limited lens with a circular aperture, in incoherent light, it falls to zero at the cutoff frequency

\[ \nu_c = \frac{1}{\lambda N_w}, \qquad N_w = N\,(1 + |m|), \]

where λ is the wavelength, N is the f-number set on the lens, and \(N_w\) is the working f-number at magnification m (this form assumes equal entrance and exit pupils). The Airy-disk diameter to the first dark ring, \(2.44\,\lambda N_w\), is the smallest blur spot the lens can form.

Contrast is the currency. Resolution charts measure whether a pattern can be resolved, but inspection succeeds or fails on whether the defect’s signal stands clear of noise: sensor noise, surface texture, and part-to-part variation. A defect that changes intensity by 5 gray levels on a background that varies by 10 is not reliably detectable at any resolution. As a starting point, the lens needs about 10 % contrast at the defect’s spatial frequency to show the detail at all, and 20 % or more is a safer design target; then engineer the lighting so the defect signal is several times the background noise. To compare cameras’ noise and sensitivity, use the EMVA 1288 figures (quantum efficiency, dark noise, saturation capacity) that many camera makers publish.

Choosing magnification, field of view, and working distance

The sensor size and the field of view fix the magnification, and the magnification and the distance to the part fix the focal length: \(m = \text{sensor width}/\text{FOV}\). With the thin-lens approximation and the working distance WD measured from the lens to the object,

\[ f \approx \frac{\text{WD}\cdot m}{1+m}, \quad \text{WD} \approx f\,\frac{1+m}{m}. \]

Example. A 12 MP sensor of 4096 × 3000 pixels at 3.45 µm, such as Sony’s IMX304, measures 14.1 × 10.4 mm. Covering a 150 mm field needs m = 0.094. At a 500 mm working distance that calls for f ≈ 43 mm; with a stock 50 mm lens, the working distance becomes about 580 mm. Then check three things:

  • The lens’s image circle covers the sensor diagonal: 17.5 mm here, which needs a lens rated for the 1.1″ format (17.6 mm), the practical limit of C-mount.
  • The lens is rated for the pixel size. A lens designed for larger pixels may keep little contrast at 145 lp/mm, so read its MTF curve at the sensor’s Nyquist frequency.
  • Distortion at the field edge is acceptable for any measurement you intend to make, or you have a calibration plan.

Sensor formats. Inch format names such as 1/2.3″ or 2/3″ are a legacy of television camera tubes, not a dimension: a 2/3″ sensor is about 11 mm across its diagonal. The lens format and mount follow the diagonal: C-mount covers up to about 1.1″ (17.6 mm), TFL (M35) APS-C (27.9 mm), TFL-II (M48) APS-H (about 35 mm), and F-mount, M58, and M72 full frame (43.3 mm). Larger sensors, up to over 120 mm in diagonal in the configurator’s list, need large-format optics.

Depth of field and the f-number trade-off

Stopping the lens down to a larger f-number buys depth of field, but it costs light and, through diffraction, sharpness, so each job has a best f-number. Depth of field is how far the object can move along the optical axis before the blur in the image exceeds an acceptable diameter c on the sensor, usually one or two pixels. When the depth of field is small compared with the working distance, as in most inspection setups (thin lens, equal pupils),

\[ \text{DOF} \approx \frac{2\,N\,c\,(1+m)}{m^2}. \]

With c = 2 pixels (6.9 µm) and m = 0.1, the depth of field is about 6 mm at f/4, 8.5 mm at f/5.6, and 12 mm at f/8. The extra depth costs two things:

  • Light: the light reaching the sensor scales as \(1/N^2\), so each full stop (a factor of √2 in N) halves it. That pushes you toward longer exposures (motion blur) or brighter, more expensive lighting.
  • Diffraction: the Airy diameter at f/8 and m = 0.1 is 11.8 µm, about 3.4 pixels. That is already larger than the 2-pixel blur budget, so even the best focus no longer looks sharp.

A useful rule of thumb puts the best f-number where the diffraction blur roughly equals the allowed blur, \(2.44\,\lambda N_w \approx c\). For this example that gives N ≈ 4.7, so f/4–f/5.6 is the sweet spot, with about 7 mm of depth. At that balance the depth of field is \(\text{DOF} \approx b^2/(1.22\,\lambda)\), where \(b = c/m\) is the blur allowed on the part (69 µm here). Only that blur and the wavelength matter, so a different sensor or magnification that keeps the same resolution on the part adds no depth, and stopping down further only trades resolution for depth. If the parts need more depth, tighten the part presentation or refocus for each part. The lens calculator gives the exact near and far limits for your setup.

Telecentric lenses

If the system measures sizes and the part’s height varies, an ordinary lens adds an error, because its magnification changes with distance. A telecentric lens removes that error, at the price of size. With a conventional (entocentric) lens and the focus fixed, \(\Delta m/m \approx -\Delta z/\text{WD}\), with WD measured to the lens’s entrance pupil. At a 300 mm working distance, a ±1 mm height variation changes the magnification by ±0.33 %, which is ±0.33 mm across a 100 mm part. That error is fatal for gauging.

An object-space telecentric lens places its aperture stop at the rear focal point of the optics in front of it, so that the chief rays (the rays through the center of the stop) are parallel to the axis in object space. Magnification is then constant through the depth of field. Telecentricity is specified as the residual chief-ray angle: at 0.1°, a 1 mm height change shifts an edge by only about 1.7 µm. Telecentric lenses also look straight into bores and along vertical walls without perspective, and paired with a telecentric (collimated) backlight they give high-contrast silhouettes for edge measurement.

The cost is size: the front lens must be at least as large as the field of view, so size, weight, and price climb quickly. One commercial telecentric lens for a 230 × 192 mm field has a 322 mm front lens, is 775 mm long, and weighs about 18 kg. Larger parts usually need several cameras, line-scan imaging, or calibrated conventional lenses with tight height control.

Lighting for glass and other specular parts

Lighting decides contrast, and contrast decides detection. For transparent and mirror-like parts such as glass, polished metal, and coated films, the lighting geometry matters more than anything else in the system.

  • Bright field (coaxial or on-axis). Light reflects specularly from a flat surface straight back into the lens, and scratches, pits, and stains scatter it away and appear dark. This works best for flat, reflective surfaces. On glass, each uncoated surface reflects about 4 % at normal incidence (\(R = ((n-1)/(n+1))^2\) for n = 1.5), so the rear surface makes a second, offset ghost image that the algorithm must handle.
  • Dark field (low-angle ring or line light). Specular reflection misses the lens entirely, and only light scattered by scratches, particles, chips, and edges reaches the camera. The background is dark and defects are bright. Dark field can detect features much smaller than a pixel, because a sub-pixel scratch still scatters enough light to brighten a pixel. It cannot size them: the brightness depends on how strongly the feature scatters, not only on its size.
  • Backlight (transmission). For silhouettes, edges, and chips, and for bubbles, stones, and inclusions inside transparent material. A collimated backlight with a telecentric lens gives the most accurate edges.
  • Polarization. A polarizer on the light and an analyzer on the lens suppress glare, at a cost in light. Crossed polarizers on transparent parts reveal stress birefringence (photoelasticity), which shows residual stress in glass; ASTM F218 describes the quantitative measurement.
  • Structured light and deflectometry. Deflectometry views the reflection of a known pattern, such as fringes on a screen, in a specular surface. A reflected ray turns by twice the surface tilt, so small slope errors (waviness, distortion, dents) become large, measurable pattern distortions.
  • Wavelength and color. For particles much smaller than the wavelength, scattering rises as \(1/\lambda^4\) (Rayleigh scattering), so blue or UV light can improve sensitivity to fine particles and haze, and a shorter wavelength also gives a smaller diffraction blur. On a monochrome camera, light of the same color as a feature makes it bright and the opposite color makes it dark, which turns color differences into contrast. Monochromatic light plus a matching bandpass filter also rejects ambient light.

On continuous sheets, line-scan cameras with line lights are standard. Several lighting channels, such as bright field and dark field, can be captured at once with separate cameras, or by alternating strobes on successive lines.

Sizing the smallest detectable defect

These rules of thumb are starting points; contrast, noise, and the algorithm move them either way:

  • Detection: about 3–5 pixels across the defect when contrast is good. Two pixels is the sampling minimum: marginal, and sensitive to where the defect falls on the pixel grid.
  • Classification (telling a scratch from a particle from a bubble): 5–10 pixels across.
  • Measurement: 10 or more pixels across the feature, and a measurement resolution of about one tenth of the tolerance (the “rule of ten” of measurement systems analysis). Sub-pixel edge methods can locate a well-resolved, high-contrast edge to roughly 0.1–0.3 pixel, but verify that with a gauge study rather than assuming it.

Example: 100 µm chips on a 150 mm field with 36.6 µm pixels are only 2.7 pixels across, below the 3–5 pixel detection range. Halve the field per camera, move to an 8k line-scan sensor, or use dark field, where the chip’s scattered light makes it far more detectable than its pixel count suggests. The vision system configurator runs this check for every sensor in its list.

Motion blur, exposure, and strobing

A moving part smears during the exposure. Keep the smear to a fraction of a pixel by shortening the exposure or by flashing the light. A part moving at speed v blurs across

\[ \text{blur (pixels)} = \frac{v\,t_\text{exp}}{p_\text{obj}} \]

during an exposure \(t_\text{exp}\). For a conveyor at 0.5 m/s with 36.6 µm object pixels and a blur budget of half a pixel (the strict end of the usual half-pixel to one-pixel budgets), the exposure must be 36.6 µs or shorter. That is well within reach of strobed LED lighting, which supplies short, intense pulses so the lens can stay at its best aperture.

The shutter type matters too. A global shutter, like an interline CCD, exposes every pixel at once. A rolling shutter starts and ends the rows one after another, so a moving part comes out skewed unless a short strobe in a shielded station sets the exposure. Full-frame CCDs have no electronic shutter at all and need a mechanical shutter or a strobe in the dark. More than half of the sensors in the configurator’s list have global shutters.

Line-scan cameras avoid the problem differently: the line rate is synchronized to motion, here \(v/p_\text{obj} \approx 13.7\) kHz for square pixels. Driving the line trigger from an encoder on the conveyor keeps the pixels square when line speed varies. The motion blur calculator covers both cases.

Validating an inspection system

An inspection system is a gauge, and it needs the same scrutiny as any other gauge.

  • Build a defect library. Collect real and seeded defects spanning the size range around the specification limit, for every defect type and on the full range of good-part appearance: color, coating, and texture variation.
  • Measure probability of detection (POD) versus size. Plot POD as a function of defect size rather than quoting one number: the question is not the smallest defect the system can find but the largest it can miss. The US Department of Defense handbook MIL-HDBK-1823A suggests at least 60 defect sites for hit-or-miss results, and at least three times as many defect-free sites to estimate the false-call rate. A classical shortcut, demonstrating 90 % POD with 95 % confidence at a single size, takes 29 detections in 29 trials, because 0.9²⁹ ≈ 0.047 < 0.05.
  • Quantify false calls on a large set of good parts. A 0.5 % false-call rate means 5 manual reviews for every 1000 good parts, and reviewers who learn to ignore the system.
  • Run attribute agreement analysis (AIAG MSA) for pass/fail decisions against expert judgment. Run a standard gauge R&R for any dimensional measurement the system reports.
  • Verify continuously. Check golden and limit samples at the start of each shift, monitor light intensity, check focus, and archive images of rejects for traceability and retraining.

Escapes and false calls trade against each other through the decision threshold. Choose the operating point deliberately, using the costs you wrote down at the start, and re-check it whenever lighting, optics, or the product changes. Inspection data also belongs on control charts. A rising false-call rate is often the first sign of a dirty lens, an aging light, or an upstream process drift. See SPC and process capability.
