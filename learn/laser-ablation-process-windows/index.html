Laser Ablation Process Windows

A laser ablation recipe that works on the development bench has to keep working as the laser ages, the protective window gets dirty, the focus drifts by a few tens of micrometers, and the material changes from lot to lot. This article shows how to build in that margin from the start: measure the ablation threshold properly, choose the fluence and pulse overlap on physical grounds, map the process window with designed experiments, and monitor the variables that actually drift. It is written for the engineers, technicians, and managers who run or buy such a process, and each step links to a calculator so you can try it with your own numbers.

Key terms
Fluence
Laser energy delivered per unit area of the surface, usually in J/cm². It, not the power setting alone, decides whether material is removed.
Peak fluence
The fluence at the center of a Gaussian (bell-shaped) spot, where it is highest: twice the pulse energy divided by the area of the 1/e² circle. Thresholds measured from crater sizes are quoted this way.
Ablation threshold
The lowest peak fluence that removes material. For metals hit by one 10 ps pulse at 1064 nm it is about 0.5–2 J/cm², and it falls when many pulses hit the same spot.
Liu method
Measuring crater diameters made at several pulse energies and plotting the diameter squared against the logarithm of the energy. The straight line gives both the threshold and the true beam size on the part.
Optimum fluence
The peak fluence that removes the most material per joule with a Gaussian beam: about 7.4 times the threshold. Anywhere from about 4 to 15 times keeps at least 90 % of the best efficiency.
Incubation
The drop in threshold as more pulses hit the same spot, because each pulse leaves changes that help the next one. The incubation coefficient S is 1 with no such effect; measured values for metals are mostly 0.7–0.9.
Pulse overlap
How much of a spot’s diameter the next pulse covers again, set by the scan speed, the repetition rate, and the spot size. A 30 µm spot scanned at 1 m/s with 100 kHz pulses gives 67 %.
Heat accumulation
Leftover heat from earlier pulses raising the temperature, when pulses arrive faster than heat can leave the spot. It shows up as melt, burrs, discoloration, and cracks.
Process window
The range of settings (fluence, overlap, focus, passes) in which every quality requirement is met at the same time. A robust process runs near its center, not at an edge.
Statistical process control (SPC)
Charting a measured characteristic over time so that a drift is caught before parts go out of specification. The capability index Cpk says how comfortably the process fits inside its specification limits.

Fluence is the variable that matters

Laser ablation is a threshold process. Material is removed only where the incident energy per unit area, the fluence, exceeds a material-specific threshold \(F_\text{th}\). So the number to specify and control is the fluence and how far it sits above threshold, not the laser’s power setting. For a Gaussian beam of 1/e² radius \(w_0\) (the radius at which the fluence has fallen to 13.5 % of its central value) and pulse energy \(E\), the fluence across the spot is

\[ \begin{gathered} F(r) = F_0 \exp\!\left(-\frac{2r^2}{w_0^2}\right), \\[4pt] F_0 = \frac{2E}{\pi w_0^2}. \end{gathered} \]

\(F_0\) is the peak fluence, twice the pulse energy divided by the 1/e² area. Laser-damage standards (ISO 21254-1) and crater-size (Liu) measurements quote thresholds as peak fluence. Some papers and datasheets instead divide the energy by the whole 1/e² area, which gives an average fluence of exactly half the peak value, so check which convention a source uses before comparing numbers. The fluence calculator gives both from the pulse energy and spot size. Average power, pulse energy, and spot size are only inputs; specify and control the process in terms of \(F_0\) and its ratio to threshold. Setting the Gaussian profile equal to \(F_\text{th}\) gives the diameter of the ablated region:

\[ D^2 = 2w_0^2 \ln\!\left(\frac{F_0}{F_\text{th}}\right). \]

Measuring the threshold: the Liu method

A threshold copied from a paper is a starting point at best: it depends on the material’s surface, the wavelength, the pulse duration, and the number of pulses. The diameter relation above gives a clean way to measure both the threshold and the actual beam radius at the work surface on your own parts. Fire single pulses (or a fixed number of pulses) at several energies and measure each crater diameter. Then plot \(D^2\) against \(\ln E\). The data fall on a straight line with slope \(2w_0^2\) that crosses \(D^2 = 0\) at the threshold energy \(E_\text{th}\), so \(F_\text{th} = 2E_\text{th}/(\pi w_0^2)\). This is J. M. Liu’s 1982 technique, originally proposed for measuring spot sizes. It remains the standard way to determine ablation thresholds because it does not require knowing the focused spot in advance.

A few practices make the measurement trustworthy:

  • Span the range. Use at least five energies from about 1.5 to 20 times threshold, spread over at least a factor of 10. Points very close to threshold are noisy. A line that bends suggests a second ablation regime (common in metals at higher fluence; Nolte et al., 1997), a beam that is not Gaussian, or craters made with different pulse counts; fit each straight part separately.
  • Define the crater edge consistently. Decide whether you are measuring the ablation edge or the outer ring of melting or discoloration, and use the same microscope, illumination, and criterion every time. The modification threshold and the ablation threshold differ.
  • Use the slope as a spot-size check. If the fitted \(w_0\) disagrees with the expected focus, the sample is probably not at the waist. That alone is worth knowing.
  • Repeat at the process pulse count. Single-pulse thresholds are not the thresholds that matter in a scanned process (see incubation below).

Depth per pulse and the efficiency optimum

More fluence is not always better. Just above threshold, most of each pulse falls in the wings of the spot below threshold and is wasted. Far above it, depth grows only slowly and the extra energy goes into heat, plasma, and debris. In between there is a fluence that removes the most material per joule, and for a Gaussian beam it has a simple value. For ultrashort pulses on metals and many absorbing materials, the depth removed per pulse follows a logarithmic law,

\[ L = \delta \ln\!\left(\frac{F_0}{F_\text{th}}\right), \]

where \(\delta\) is an effective energy-penetration depth. In metals it is close to the optical penetration depth at low fluence (about 13 nm for copper near 800 nm, from the optical constants of Johnson and Christy) and several times larger at higher fluence, where heat carried by the electrons takes over (Nolte et al., 1997). Treat \(\delta\) as a parameter fitted to measured depths, not one taken from optical constants. Integrating this depth over a Gaussian spot gives the removed volume per pulse, \(V = \tfrac{\pi}{4} w_0^2 \delta \ln^2(F_0/F_\text{th})\). Dividing by the pulse energy \(E = \pi w_0^2 F_0/2\) and maximizing gives a result that is easy to remember:

\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{V}{E} = \frac{\delta}{2F_0}\ln^2\!\left(\frac{F_0}{F_\text{th}}\right) \\[4pt] \Longrightarrow\quad F_{0,\mathrm{opt}} = e^2 F_\text{th} \approx 7.4\,F_\text{th}. \end{gathered} \]

Račiukaitis et al. (2009) derived this optimum, and Neuenschwander et al. (2010) brought it into wide use; Furmanski et al. (2007) had found nearly the same value, 7.6 \(F_\text{th}\), numerically for overlapping trenches. For a top-hat beam, which is uniform across the spot, the optimum is \(e\,F_\text{th} \approx 2.7\,F_\text{th}\). The optimum is broad (figure below). Anywhere from about 4 × to 15 × threshold, the efficiency stays within about 10 % of the peak, which is why 5–15 × is a common rule of thumb (Neuenschwander et al., 2014). At 2 × threshold it falls to less than half.

For nanosecond pulses, melting, melt expulsion, and vaporization dominate, so the logarithmic law and its \(e^2\) optimum are only rough guides. The general point still holds: removal per joule is poor both just above threshold and far above it, so measure depth or volume against fluence and find the best point for your own material.

Incubation: thresholds drop with repeated pulses

A threshold measured with one pulse overstates the threshold of a scanned process, often by several times. On copper with picosecond pulses, for example, it falls from about 2.0 J/cm² for one pulse to 0.19 J/cm² after 1000 pulses on the same spot (Žemaitis et al., 2018). Each pulse leaves changes that make the next one more effective: defects and plastic deformation, roughening, and chemical changes such as oxidation. The widely used empirical model of Jee et al. (1988) is

\[ F_\text{th}(N) = F_\text{th}(1)\,N^{S-1}, \]

where the incubation coefficient \(S = 1\) means no incubation. Reported values for metals are mostly 0.7–0.9 (copper with picosecond pulses about 0.7–0.8; Račiukaitis et al., 2009; Žemaitis et al., 2018). Dielectrics incubate too (Ashkenasi et al., 1999), so measure \(S\) for your own material. With \(S = 0.85\), the threshold after 100 pulses is half the single-pulse value. The power law has no floor, while measured thresholds level off at large pulse counts (Di Niso et al., 2014; Neuenschwander et al., 2014), so fit \(S\) over the pulse counts you actually use. In practice:

  • Measure at the pulse count of the real process. A recipe designed from single-pulse data will cut deeper and wider than intended when it is scanned with overlap.
  • Watch for slow edge growth. With repeated passes, a track edge that sits just below the single-pulse threshold may eventually ablate.
  • Expect substrate damage to incubate too. When you selectively remove a coating, the substrate’s damage threshold incubates as well; multi-pulse (S-on-1) damage tests, as in ISO 21254-2, usually give lower thresholds than single-pulse (1-on-1) tests. Check substrate damage at the full pass count.

Pulse overlap and the effective number of pulses

Overlap sets how many pulses each point of a scanned track receives, and so how far incubation lowers its threshold and how much heat builds up. In a scanned process, the spacing between pulses along the scan direction is the pulse pitch \(\Delta x = v/f_{rep}\) (scan speed divided by repetition rate), and the spacing between adjacent lines is the hatch \(\Delta y\). Geometric overlap is usually quoted relative to the 1/e² spot diameter (Račiukaitis et al., 2009),

\[ \mathrm{OL} = 1 - \frac{\Delta x}{2w_0}, \qquad N_{geo} = \frac{2w_0}{\Delta x}, \]

where \(N_{geo}\) is the number of pulses per spot diameter. Some groups measure overlap against the crater diameter instead, which depends on fluence, so check the definition before copying an overlap from a paper. For Gaussian beams a dose-based count is the fluence-equivalent number of pulses. Summing the overlapping Gaussian profiles along the line gives an accumulated fluence on the track centerline of \(\sqrt{\pi/2}\,F_0 w_0/\Delta x\). That is the same as

\[ N_{eff} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\;\frac{w_0}{\Delta x} \]

pulses delivered at full peak fluence. Extending the same sum to two dimensions recovers an intuitive result: the areal energy density of a hatched field is just \(E/(\Delta x\,\Delta y)\). Many papers instead call \(N_{geo}\), the number of pulses within the 1/e² diameter, the effective pulse number (Skoulas et al., 2017), and \(N_{eff}\) is \(\sqrt{\pi/2}/2 \approx 0.63\) times it. An incubation coefficient is fitted with one particular count, so use the same definition as the source of your \(S\).

For example, at 1 m/s, 100 kHz, and a 30 µm spot diameter, the pitch is 10 µm. That gives 67 % overlap, 3 pulses per spot diameter, and \(N_{eff} = 1.9\). Low overlap leaves scalloped edges and incomplete clearing between pulses. Separate craters join into a continuous line only when the pitch is smaller than the crater diameter \(D\), which takes more than 41 % overlap at \(F_0 = 2F_\text{th}\) but any positive overlap at \(e^2 F_\text{th}\). High overlap smooths the edges but raises incubation and heat accumulation. Continuous scribes and milled surfaces commonly use about 50–90 % overlap, chosen by experiment for edge quality and a smooth floor. Surface structuring with picosecond pulses was smoothest at 50–75 % (Neuenschwander et al., 2014), and alumina trenches had flat floors at about 83–89 % (Furmanski et al., 2007). Heat accumulation and the heat-affected zone

A recipe that is clean at low power can melt and burr when it is moved to a faster laser, because heat no longer has time to leave between pulses. Heat spreads a distance of roughly \(L_{th} = 2\sqrt{Dt}\) in time \(t\), where \(D\) is the thermal diffusivity (authors also use \(\sqrt{Dt}\) or \(\sqrt{2Dt}\), so check the convention before comparing numbers). Two time scales matter: the pulse duration and the time between pulses.

Room-temperature diffusivities: the 300 K values of Bergman et al. for the metals and silicon, and a typical datasheet value for soda-lime glass, as in the thermal diffusion calculator. Diffusivity changes with temperature, so treat these lengths as estimates.

The diffusion length during the pulse sets the minimum heat-affected zone, which is a main reason picosecond and femtosecond pulses give cleaner, more precise ablation than nanosecond pulses (Chichkov et al., 1996). The diffusion length between pulses controls heat accumulation. Heat has to spread out of the spot before the next pulse arrives, which takes about \(w_0^2/4D\). For a 30 µm spot that puts the onset of accumulation near 70 kHz in stainless steel but about 2 MHz in copper. Above it, the surface temperature ratchets upward from pulse to pulse (Weber et al., 2014), and you get melt, oxidation, recast, burrs, and microcracking in brittle materials. In scanned ultrashort-pulse ablation of metals, a smooth, reflective surface turns rough and dark below a critical scan speed (Bauer et al., 2015), and in glass written with femtosecond pulses the modified zone grows above about 200 kHz (Eaton et al., 2005).

Standard countermeasures are to lower the repetition rate while holding peak fluence near the optimum, to increase pitch and add passes with cooling time between them, and to scan faster so consecutive pulses land farther apart. Galvanometer scanners with a 100 mm lens reach about 10 m/s and polygon scanners about 100 m/s, so a laser firing millions of pulses per second can keep a moderate overlap and build depth in many fast passes (Neuenschwander et al., 2014). Mapping the process window with designed experiments

Physics gets you to a sensible starting point; designed experiments (DOE, a planned set of runs that changes several settings at once so their effects and interactions can be separated) find the settings that actually work. A process window is the region of parameter space where every quality response is within specification at the same time. It is best mapped in dimensionless coordinates (peak fluence relative to threshold, and overlap or pulses per point) because those transfer between lasers, lenses, and spot sizes far better than raw power and speed. The usual sequence (Montgomery, Design and Analysis of Experiments; NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook, chapter 5) is:

  1. Screen. Run a two-level fractional factorial over the candidate factors: pulse energy, pitch (speed or repetition rate), hatch, focus offset, and passes. Responses include depth or clearing, width, edge quality, residue, substrate damage, and cycle time. This identifies the factors that matter.
  2. Model. Run a response-surface design (central composite, Box and Wilson, 1951; or Box–Behnken, 1960) around the promising region for the two or three important factors. Fit quadratic models to each response.
  3. Overlay. Plot each response’s specification limits on the same factor axes. The window is the intersection.
  4. Center. Choose the operating point that maximizes the distance to the nearest window edge, measured in units of expected variation (power drift, focus tolerance), rather than the point with the best average result.

Include focus offset as a factor. Peak fluence scales as \(1/w(z)^2 \propto 1/[1 + (z/z_R)^2]\), where \(z_R\) is the Rayleigh range, so the window’s extent in \(z\) is your real depth of focus for this process. The fluence is 10 % lower at \(z = \pm z_R/3\), 20 % lower at \(\pm z_R/2\), and half at \(\pm z_R\). To get this focus range from the Gaussian beam calculator, set its spot-size tolerance to the growth that gives the fluence drop your window allows: 5.4 % for a 10 % drop, 11.8 % for 20 %.

Robustness: why the operating ratio matters

Where you sit relative to threshold decides how much a small loss of laser power changes the part. The logarithmic relations above show this directly. Differentiating gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\Delta D}{D} &\approx \frac{1}{2\ln(F_0/F_\text{th})}\,\frac{\Delta F}{F}, \\[6pt] \frac{\Delta L}{L} &\approx \frac{1}{\ln(F_0/F_\text{th})}\,\frac{\Delta F}{F}. \end{aligned} \]

Consider a 10 % drop in delivered fluence, which a contaminated protective window or an aging diode can easily cause. Running at twice threshold, the track narrows by about 8 % and the depth per pulse falls by about 15 % (the linear forms above give 7 % and 14 %, slightly underestimating a step this large). Running at \(e^2 F_\text{th}\), the same drop narrows the track by about 3 % and reduces depth by about 5 %. Operating well above threshold buys robustness. The upper limit is set by heat input, substrate damage, and debris, which the DOE has to establish. A process sitting too close to threshold is a common root cause of yield loss that gets blamed on “material variation.”

In-line monitoring and SPC for yield

A window found once is not a window kept: optics get dirty, lasers age, and material lots change. Once the window is defined, keep the process inside it with statistical process control (SPC) on the variables that drift:

  • Delivered energy at the work plane. Check it on a schedule with a calibrated thermopile placed below the protective window, not just from the laser’s internal monitor. Track protective-window transmission and replace windows on a data-driven interval.
  • Focus and beam quality. Run a periodic caustic or spot check, especially after maintenance. Thermal lensing in optics with absorbing contamination shifts focus as average power rises.
  • The product characteristic itself. Use machine vision measurement of track width, position, and clearing on every part, or on a statistically sampled subset. This is the only direct check on output.
  • Material inputs. Track coating thickness, absorptance, and surface condition from incoming inspection. Changes here move \(F_\text{th}\).

Put the key characteristics and the delivered power on control charts, and write reaction plans that say what to check first when a chart signals. Before release, require demonstrated capability. The usual index is Cpk, the distance from the process mean to the nearer specification limit in units of three standard deviations; common minimums are 1.33 for a process in production and 1.67 for new processes and critical characteristics (AIAG PPAP manual; Montgomery, Introduction to Statistical Quality Control). The companion article on SPC for laser processes covers measurement-system analysis and chart selection.

Checklist

  • Measure \(F_\text{th}\) by the Liu method at the process pulse count, and quote peak fluence.
  • Start near \(F_0 \approx e^2 F_\text{th}\) for efficiency, then let quality responses move the operating point.
  • Specify overlap with its spot-diameter convention, and state which pulse count (pulses per spot diameter or fluence-equivalent) goes with your \(S\).
  • Check thermal diffusion lengths against pitch and repetition period before scaling up power.
  • Map the window with DOE in dimensionless coordinates, and center the operating point.
  • Monitor delivered energy, focus, and the product characteristic with SPC and reaction plans.
