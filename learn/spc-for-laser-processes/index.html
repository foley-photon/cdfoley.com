SPC and Process Capability in Production

Statistical process control (SPC) uses simple statistics on routine measurements to tell a process’s normal variation from a real change, so that a drifting laser process is caught and corrected before it makes scrap. This article covers what to measure, how to trust the measurement, how to chart and read it, how to judge whether the process fits the specification, and how to turn a signal into a fix that stays fixed: the path from “works on a good day” to a steady 99 % yield.

Key terms
Characteristic
A measurable property of the part or the process, such as line width, scribe depth, or laser power at the work surface.
Common and special causes
Common causes are the many small influences behind a process’s routine, random variation. A special cause is a specific change, such as a dirty window or a new material lot, that moves the process away from its usual behavior and can be found and removed.
Control chart
A plot of a measurement in time order with a center line and upper and lower control limits. A point outside the limits, or an unlikely pattern inside them, signals a special cause.
Control limits
The lines about three standard deviations of the plotted value either side of the center line, calculated from the process’s own variation. They are not the specification limits.
Specification limits
The lowest and highest acceptable values (LSL and USL), set by the drawing or the customer.
Subgroup
A few parts, typically 4 or 5, made and measured close together under the same conditions; the chart plots one average and one range per subgroup.
Gauge R&R
A study of how much of the measured variation comes from the measurement itself: repeatability (same gauge and operator) and reproducibility (different operators or set-ups).
Cpk and Ppk
Capability indices: how many times three standard deviations fit between the process mean and the nearer specification limit. Cpk uses the short-term variation within subgroups, Ppk the overall variation; 1.33 means the nearer limit is 4 standard deviations away.
Control plan and reaction plan
The control plan lists, for each characteristic, how it is measured, how often, and how it is controlled; the reaction plan says what the operator does when a check fails or a chart signals.

Start with the characteristic, not the chart

SPC only works on a quantity that can be measured, that matters to the customer or to the next process step, and that responds to the process inputs you control. For a laser process there are two kinds of characteristic.

  • Output characteristics are what the customer sees: ablation line width and depth, kerf or scribe width, edge chipping, residue, electrical isolation, weld penetration, and defect counts per part.
  • Process (input) characteristics cause the outputs: pulse energy or average power at the work surface, focal height, spot size, scan speed and pulse spacing, assist-gas flow, and part temperature.

Chart both kinds. Output charts tell you the customer is at risk; input charts tell you why, and usually earlier.

Prefer variables (measured) data to attribute (pass/fail) data wherever possible (Montgomery, Section 7.4). A measured width carries information on every part, while a pass/fail result carries information only when something fails. With zero failures in \(n\) parts, the 95 % upper confidence bound on the defect rate is about \(3/n\), the “rule of three” (Hanley and Lippman-Hand, 1983). Demonstrating a defect rate below 0.1 % from pass/fail data alone therefore takes about 3000 consecutive good parts; the sigma-level converter computes the exact bound. A capability study on a measured characteristic can reach the same conclusion from 100 or so values, provided the process is stable and its distribution has been checked, because the conclusion then rests on the fitted distribution’s tail.

Design the process to be insensitive before you control it. For a Gaussian beam with \(1/e^2\) radius \(w_0\), the diameter \(D\) ablated by a pulse of peak fluence \(F_0\) (pulse energy per unit area at the beam center) follows \(D^2 = 2w_0^2\ln(F_0/F_\text{th})\), where \(F_\text{th}\) is the threshold fluence below which no material is removed (Liu, 1982). Differentiating gives

\[ \frac{\Delta D}{D} \approx \frac{1}{2\ln(F_0/F_\text{th})}\,\frac{\Delta F}{F} . \]

At a peak fluence of 1.5× threshold, a 3 % drop in pulse energy narrows the line by about 3.8 % (the linear form gives 3.7 %). At 5× threshold the same drop narrows it by only about 1 %. Operating well inside the process window is the cheapest control there is. The fluence and ablation calculators and the process-window article cover this in depth.

Measurement systems first

Before charting a characteristic, make sure the gauge can see its variation. Every observed value includes the measurement system’s own variation, and for independent errors the two add in quadrature:

\[ \sigma_\text{observed}^2 = \sigma_\text{process}^2 + \sigma_\text{measurement}^2 \]

If the gauge’s standard deviation is half the process’s, the observed spread is 12 % wider than the real one (\(\sqrt{1.25} = 1.118\)) and every Cpk you report is 11 % too low. A gauge with \(\sigma_\text{measurement}\) comparable to \(\sigma_\text{process}\) makes the chart mostly a chart of the gauge. Before charting, run a gauge repeatability and reproducibility (gauge R&R) study: several operators or set-ups measure the same parts several times, and an analysis of variance splits the spread into part-to-part, repeatability, and reproducibility (Montgomery, Section 8.8). The common guideline from the AIAG MSA manual:

%GRRInterpretation
< 10 %Acceptable
10–30 %May be acceptable, depending on the application and cost
> 30 %Not acceptable; fix the measurement before the process

%GRR is the gauge’s share of either the tolerance or the total variation: compare it with the tolerance when the gauge sorts parts against the specification, and with the total variation when it feeds a control chart. Also check that the number of distinct categories (ndc), how many groups of parts the gauge can reliably tell apart, is at least 5. ISO 22514-7 offers an alternative based on measurement uncertainty. For in-line optical sensors such as laser displacement and confocal height sensors, vision measurement, and power monitors, include the variables that change in production in the study: part reflectivity and color, temperature, fixture position, and focus. Check bias against a reference standard and stability over days, not just repeatability over minutes.

Control charts: I–MR and X̄–R

A control chart answers one question: has anything changed? A Shewhart chart compares each new point with limits set three standard deviations of the plotted value either side of the center line, computed from the process’s own short-term variation. The limits have nothing to do with the specification limits, and they should never be replaced by them. The two workhorse charts:

Individuals and moving range (I–MR) is for one measurement at a time: a daily power check at the work surface, a per-lot measurement, or slow processes. With \(\overline{MR}\) the average absolute difference between consecutive points:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{UCL, LCL} &= \bar{x} \pm 2.660\,\overline{MR} \\ \text{UCL}_{MR} &= 3.267\,\overline{MR} \\ \hat\sigma &= \overline{MR}/1.128 \end{aligned} \]

X̄ and range (X̄–R) is for small subgroups (n = 2–9, most often 4–5), such as five consecutive parts every hour. For n = 5, the limits are \(\bar{\bar{x}} \pm 0.577\,\bar R\) on the X̄ chart and \(2.114\,\bar R\) on the R chart (lower limit 0). For subgroups larger than about ten, use X̄–S instead. The constants follow from the distribution of the range of normal data and are tabulated by Montgomery (Chapter 6) and in the control chart calculator; ISO 7870-2 is the international standard for these charts.

Limits are first calculated from 20–25 subgroups (or 50–100 individual values) of a period believed to be typical. In this Phase I, subgroups with a found special cause are removed and the limits recalculated; in Phase II, the limits are frozen and each new point is judged against them (Montgomery, Section 5.3). Revise trial limits as data accumulate.

Rational subgrouping decides whether an X̄–R chart is useful or misleading. A subgroup should contain only short-term, common-cause variation: consecutive parts from the same head, fixture, and material lot. Mixing laser heads, scanner fields, or nests within a subgroup inflates R, widens the limits, and hides real shifts. Stratify instead, with one chart per head or nest, or a chart of head-to-head differences. The control chart calculator computes I–MR, X̄–R, and X̄–S limits from pasted data and flags violations.

Reading the chart: Western Electric and Nelson rules

A point beyond the limits is not the only sign of trouble. The Western Electric rules (1956) divide the chart into zones at 1σ, 2σ, and 3σ, where σ is the standard deviation of one plotted point, and flag patterns that are unlikely under common-cause variation alone. Each rule counts the points on one side of the center line:

  1. One point beyond 3σ.
  2. Two of three consecutive points beyond 2σ on the same side.
  3. Four of five consecutive points beyond 1σ on the same side.
  4. Eight consecutive points on the same side of the center line.

Nelson’s 1984 set adds tests for six points in a row steadily rising or falling (a trend), fourteen points alternating up and down, fifteen consecutive points within 1σ (stratification: the limits are too wide, often because of mixed subgroups), and eight in a row beyond 1σ on either side (a mixture of two populations). Nelson uses nine, not eight, for the run rule. The public draft of the new AIAG & VDA SPC manual (2026) lists rules 1–3, the nine-point run, and the fifteen-point test.

Every rule you add trades false alarms for sensitivity. The average run length (ARL), the average number of points plotted before a signal, measures both. With rule 1 alone, a stable process with known limits produces a false signal on 0.27 % of points, an ARL of 370. Running all four Western Electric rules catches small shifts much sooner but lowers the in-control ARL to 91.75 points (Champ and Woodall, 1987). The six-point trend test is the weakest addition: once the zone rules are in use, it adds false alarms but catches a drift hardly any sooner (Davis and Woodall, 1988). Pick the rules that match the failure modes you expect, and make sure every signal leads to a defined action. A rule that operators learn to ignore is worse than no rule.

Laser processes produce recognizable patterns:

  • Downward trends in delivered power or ablation width: contamination building up on protective windows or scan-lens covers, diode or pump aging, fiber connector degradation.
  • Step changes after maintenance: focus offset, alignment, a different lens or window, or recalibrated power.
  • Cycles that follow shifts or ambient temperature: thermal drift of mounts and scanners, thermal lensing at high power, chiller behavior.
  • Mixtures and stratification from multiple heads, fixtures, or material suppliers feeding one chart.

Capability: Cp, Cpk, Pp, and Ppk

A control chart says whether the process is stable; capability says whether a stable process fits inside the specification, and with how much room to spare. The indices compare the process spread with the specification limits (LSL, USL):

\[ \begin{gathered} C_p = \frac{\text{USL}-\text{LSL}}{6\sigma_\text{within}} \\[10pt] C_{pk} = \min\!\left(\frac{\text{USL}-\mu}{3\sigma_\text{within}},\, \frac{\mu-\text{LSL}}{3\sigma_\text{within}}\right) \end{gathered} \]

Cp measures potential (spread only); Cpk also penalizes an off-center mean. \(\sigma_\text{within}\) is the short-term standard deviation from the control chart, \(\bar R/d_2\) or \(\overline{MR}/1.128\). Pp and Ppk use the same formulas with the overall sample standard deviation, which includes between-subgroup drift. When Ppk is much lower than Cpk, the process is drifting or shifting between subgroups, and that is the first thing to fix (Wheeler, 2013). Names differ between standards: ISO 22514-2 and the new AIAG & VDA SPC manual (2026, public draft) calculate both pairs from the overall variation, keep the names Cp/Cpk for a process shown to be stable, and call the within-subgroup indices Cw/Cwk. Check which convention a requirement uses before comparing numbers.

A capability index predicts future output only for a process in statistical control, and it converts to a defect rate only if the distribution is known, usually assumed normal. Check a histogram or probability plot first; the calculator draws both and runs an Anderson–Darling test. Characteristics bounded by physics, such as depth stopped by a substrate, or roughness, are often skewed and need a transformation or the quantile (percentile) method of ISO 22514-2. Estimates are also noisy: 100 values pin Ppk down only to about ±0.2 (Bissell, 1990).

The table assumes a normal distribution with the nearer limit exactly 3, 4, 5, or 6σ from the mean (1.33 and 1.67 stand for 4/3 and 5/3); PPM is parts per million out of specification. For automotive parts, the AIAG PPAP manual judges an initial process study by its index: above 1.67 meets the acceptance criteria, 1.33–1.67 may be acceptable after review with the customer, and below 1.33 does not. For two-sided specifications, Montgomery recommends at least 1.33 for an existing process, 1.50 for a new process or a safety-critical characteristic, and 1.67 for a new process with a safety-critical characteristic. Customer-specific requirements always take precedence. For perspective, a single centered characteristic that yields only 99 % has Cpk ≈ 0.86. In practice, most yield loss in a mature laser process comes not from Gaussian tails but from special causes: contamination, handling damage, incoming-material variation, and set-up errors. Finding and removing those causes is what control charts are for. The sigma-level converter translates between indices, defect rates, and sigma levels.

Control plans, reaction plans, and the PFMEA

Charts change outcomes only when they are wired into the way the line runs. Three documents do that wiring.

The process FMEA (failure mode and effects analysis) lists how each step can fail, the effects and causes, and the current prevention and detection controls. The 2019 AIAG & VDA FMEA handbook replaced the old risk priority number with an action priority (high, medium, or low), read from a table that weighs severity first, then occurrence, then detection, instead of multiplying three ratings.

The control plan turns PFMEA controls into routine: for each characteristic, the specification, the measurement method, the sample size and frequency, the control method, and the reaction plan (AIAG Control Plan manual). An example for a laser ablation step:

Reaction plans make SPC actionable. When a rule fires, the operator knows exactly what to do: contain the parts made since the last good point, work a short ordered checklist, and escalate on a defined trigger. Wherever possible, use error-proofing instead of detection. Power-window interlocks, part-presence and orientation checks, and recipe verification remove whole failure modes rather than catching them.

Problem solving: 8D and 5-Why

When a problem escapes, a structured method keeps the team from fixing the symptom. The 8D (eight disciplines) method, introduced by Ford in 1987, runs:

  1. D0–D1: respond to the emergency (D0, a later addition) and form a cross-functional team.
  2. D2: describe the problem in measurable terms. An is/is-not analysis (which heads, lots, shifts, and part positions) narrows the search quickly.
  3. D3: contain it. Protect the customer before you understand the cause.
  4. D4: find the root cause of the occurrence and of the escape (why the controls did not catch it). Confirm it by turning the problem on and off.
  5. D5–D6: choose, implement, and validate permanent corrective actions with data, and remove containment only after validation.
  6. D7: prevent recurrence by updating the PFMEA, control plan, work instructions, and similar processes.
  7. D8: close out and recognize the team.

The 5-Why technique, from the Toyota Production System (Ohno), asks “why?” until it reaches a cause that can be fixed. A chain for a typical laser problem shows how the root cause usually sits several layers below the symptom:

  1. Ablation width went below specification. Why? Fluence at the part was low.
  2. Why? Pulse energy at the work surface had dropped 8 % while the laser’s internal monitor read nominal.
  3. Why? The protective window was coated with ablation debris.
  4. Why? Fume-extraction airflow at the nozzle had fallen.
  5. Why? The extraction filter had clogged, and it had no differential-pressure monitoring or replacement interval.

The permanent action is a filter ΔP sensor with an alarm and a preventive-maintenance interval, plus a work-surface power check in the control plan. The escape point was the reliance on an internal power monitor, which cannot see losses downstream of the laser.

From sensors to 99 %+ yield

High yield in laser processing seldom comes from a single breakthrough. It comes from a loop that runs every day:

  1. Instrument the process. In-line power monitoring, height sensing, and vision measurement turn periodic manual checks into continuous data.
  2. Automate collection. Scripts that pull controller logs, sensor data, and inspection results into one database are worth more than any single chart. Manual data entry is where SPC programs go to die.
  3. Pareto the losses. Rank defect modes by frequency and cost, and attack the top two.
  4. Use DOE to center the process window. Find settings where the outputs are insensitive to the inputs that drift.
  5. Lock in gains. Every fix flows into the PFMEA, control plan, and reaction plans, and is verified with a before-and-after capability study.
  6. Watch the leading indicators. Input charts (power, focus, cycle time, pump-down time) move before output charts do.

Yield is multiplicative across steps. The rolled throughput yield, the chance that a part passes every step the first time without rework, is the product of the first-pass yields when the steps fail independently (ISO 13053-1): five steps at 99 % each give 95.1 %, so every step has to be well controlled. The sigma-level converter computes it, and OEE and takt time, where OEE multiplies availability, performance, and quality rate, tie the quality picture to throughput and cost.
