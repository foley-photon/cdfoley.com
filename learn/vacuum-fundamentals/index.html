Vacuum

Vacuum Fundamentals

How to read and convert vacuum pressures, why gas behaves so differently at low pressure, and how to estimate a pumping line’s conductance, a chamber’s pump-down time, its outgassing load, and the leak rate it can tolerate. The thread running through all of it: once a chamber reaches high vacuum, the pipes between chamber and pump and the gas coming off the walls usually decide how fast it pumps down, not the size of the pump.

Key terms
Millibar and torr
The pressure units of everyday vacuum work. 1 mbar is 100 pascals, the SI unit; 1 Torr is 1/760 of a standard atmosphere, about 1.33 mbar. Atmospheric pressure is about 1013 mbar or 760 Torr.
Mean free path
The average distance a gas molecule travels before it hits another molecule. In room-temperature air it is about 66 µm at 1 mbar and 66 mm at 0.001 mbar.
Molecular flow
The low-pressure regime in which molecules hit the walls far more often than each other. Gas no longer flows like a fluid, and a pump can only catch the molecules that happen to wander into its inlet.
Pumping speed
The volume of gas a pump removes per second at its inlet, in L/s or m³/h, as rated on its datasheet. The speed delivered to the chamber is lower whenever a pipe or valve sits in between.
Conductance
How easily gas passes through a pipe, valve, or opening, in the same units as pumping speed. A pipe throttles a pump when its conductance is not much larger than the pump’s speed.
Gas load
The amount of gas flowing per second, given as pressure times volume per second (mbar·L/s); also called throughput. Leaks, outgassing, and process gas are all gas loads, and the chamber settles where the pump removes as much as comes in.
Outgassing
The slow release of gas, mostly water vapor, from the inside surfaces and materials of a vacuum system. After the first few minutes of pumping it usually sets the pressure.
Bake-out
Heating the evacuated chamber, typically to 150–250 °C for a day or two, to drive off adsorbed water. It lowers the outgassing rate by orders of magnitude.
Leak rate
The gas load entering through a defect, in mbar·L/s. A virtual leak is gas trapped inside the chamber, for example under a screw, that bleeds out slowly and looks like a leak.
Gas-dependent gauge
Most vacuum gauges infer pressure from a gas property and are calibrated for nitrogen, so in other gases they read wrong unless corrected. Capacitance diaphragm gauges, which measure force, are the exception.

Pressure units and ranges

Vacuum work uses several pressure units, often on the same drawing. The SI unit is the pascal (Pa); the millibar (mbar, equal to the hectopascal) dominates European equipment and most pump datasheets; the torr survives in North American labs and older literature. Vacuum pressures are absolute, measured from zero, although some industrial gauges show vacuum as a negative pressure relative to the local atmosphere. The defining relations are exact:

\[ \begin{aligned} 1\ \text{atm} &= 101\,325\ \text{Pa} \\ &= 1013.25\ \text{mbar} = 760\ \text{Torr}, \\[4pt] 1\ \text{mbar} &= 100\ \text{Pa} = 0.750\ \text{Torr} \end{aligned} \]

The pressure unit converter does the arithmetic and names the vacuum range for any value. Pressure spans more than fifteen orders of magnitude between atmosphere and the best laboratory vacuum, so it is divided into ranges, each with its own physics, pumps, and gauges:

Ultra-high10⁻⁷ – 10⁻¹²Turbo + ion, NEG, or sublimation pumps, after bake-outBayard–Alpert, extractor, or inverted-magnetron ionizationHydrogen

These are the boundaries of the first edition of ISO 3529-1 (1981), still used in much of the vacuum literature, with extreme-high vacuum below 10⁻¹² mbar as in CERN’s vacuum courses; the converter applies them and also names the ISO 3529-1:2019 range where it differs. The current international vocabulary, ISO 3529-1:2019, agrees down to 10⁻³ mbar but moves the high/ultra-high boundary to 10⁻⁸ mbar (10⁻⁶ Pa) and starts extreme-high vacuum at 10⁻¹¹ mbar (10⁻⁹ Pa): modern pumps reach 10⁻⁸ mbar with elastomer seals, while lower pressures need metal seals and bake-out. Either way, the ranges are conventions, not physical thresholds.

Gas kinetics: mean free path and flow regimes

Whether the gas in a pipe behaves like a fluid or like independent molecules decides which conductance formula applies and how a pump works. The deciding number is the mean free path, the average distance a molecule travels between collisions with other molecules:

\[ \lambda = \frac{k_B T}{\sqrt{2}\,\pi d_m^2\, p} \]

Here \(d_m\) is the molecule’s effective (hard-sphere) diameter. For air, \(d_m \approx 0.372\ \text{nm}\) (Kennard’s hard-sphere value from the viscosity at 15 °C), the value the mean free path calculator uses, so at 20 °C \(\lambda \approx 6.6\ \text{mm}\) at 1 Pa, or \(\lambda\,[\text{mm}] \approx 0.066 / p\,[\text{mbar}]\). The classic rule \(\lambda \approx 5\ \text{cm}\) at 1 mTorr is the same number in other units.

What matters is how λ compares with the size of the system, expressed as the Knudsen number \(\mathrm{Kn} = \lambda / D\), where \(D\) is a characteristic dimension such as a tube diameter. The usual boundaries, which the calculators also use, are below; the change between regimes is gradual.

  • Viscous (continuum) flow, Kn < 0.01. Molecules collide mostly with each other, the gas flows as a fluid, and conductance rises with pressure. For air this means \(p\cdot D \gtrsim 0.66\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{m}\) (0.66 mbar·cm, about 0.5 Torr·cm).
  • Molecular flow, Kn > 0.5. Molecules travel wall to wall without meeting each other, and conductance depends only on geometry, gas species, and temperature, not on pressure. For air this means \(p\cdot D \lesssim 1.3\times10^{-2}\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{m}\) (about 0.01 Torr·cm); texts that start molecular flow at Kn = 1 halve that.
  • Transitional (Knudsen) flow lies in between, where interpolation formulas such as Knudsen’s bridge the two limits.

For a 25 mm line, flow is viscous above roughly 26 Pa (0.26 mbar) and molecular below about 0.5 Pa (5 × 10⁻³ mbar). High vacuum systems, with their turbopumps and ionization gauges, work in the molecular regime, where intuition about “suction” fails: a pump cannot pull gas toward it. It can only capture molecules that wander into its inlet, so no pump’s speed can exceed the conductance of its own inlet opening, about 11.6 L/s per cm² for air.

Two more kinetic quantities explain why surfaces dominate high vacuum. At 20 °C the number density \(n = p/k_BT\) is 2.5 × 10²⁵ m⁻³ at atmosphere and still 2.5 × 10¹⁶ m⁻³ at 10⁻⁶ mbar. The wall impingement rate

\[ \begin{aligned} \Phi &= \frac{p}{\sqrt{2\pi m k_B T}} \\ &\approx 2.9\times10^{22}\ \text{m}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}\ \text{per Pa} \\ &\phantom{{}\approx{}}\text{(N}_2\text{, 20}^\circ\text{C)} \end{aligned} \]

means that at 10⁻⁶ mbar a clean surface receives enough nitrogen molecules to form a monolayer in about 3 s, if every molecule sticks. The mean free path calculator computes all of these for common gases.

Throughput, pumping speed, and conductance

This section explains why a pump rarely delivers its rated speed to the chamber. Vacuum systems are analyzed with an electrical analogy. Throughput \(Q\) is the gas flow, as pressure × volume per time (mbar·L/s or Pa·m³/s; 1 Pa·m³/s = 10 mbar·L/s). A pump of speed \(S\) at inlet pressure \(p\) removes \(Q = pS\); its rated speed is measured at its inlet flange (ISO 21360-1). A tube, valve, or port has a conductance \(C\) defined by \(Q = C\,(p_1 - p_2)\). Conductances combine like electrical conductances: in parallel they add; in series their reciprocals add.

The result that drives most vacuum design is the effective pumping speed at the chamber:

\[ \frac{1}{S_\text{eff}} = \frac{1}{S} + \frac{1}{C} \quad\Rightarrow\quad S_\text{eff} = \frac{S\,C}{S + C} \]

Whichever of \(S\) and \(C\) is smaller dominates. In molecular flow, for air at 20 °C, the standard results are:

  • Thin aperture: \(C = 11.6\,A\ \text{L/s}\), with \(A\) in cm².
  • Long round tube (\(L \gg d\)): \(C = 12.1\,d^3/L\ \text{L/s}\), with \(d\) and \(L\) in cm.
  • Tube of any length: the entrance’s aperture conductance times the tube’s transmission probability \(\alpha\), the chance that a molecule entering one end leaves by the other; \(\alpha\) falls from 1 for a thin aperture to \(4d/3L\) for a long tube. Santeler’s formula
    \[ \alpha \approx \left[\,1 + \frac{3L}{4d}\left(1 + \frac{1}{3 + \tfrac{6L}{7d}}\right)\right]^{-1} \]
    is within 0.7 % of the exact value at any length, while the older shortcut of adding aperture and long-tube resistances (Dushman’s approximation) runs up to 12 % high. The conductance and pump-down calculator uses Berman’s more accurate closed form.

The \(d^3\) dependence is unforgiving: halving a tube’s bore cuts its molecular conductance by a factor of eight. Molecular conductance also scales as \(\sqrt{T/M}\), so the same tube conducts helium about 2.7 times better than air. In laminar viscous flow, by contrast, \(C \approx 136\, d^4 \bar p / L\ \text{L/s}\) for air at 20 °C (\(d\) and \(L\) in cm, mean pressure \(\bar p\) in mbar), which is huge at roughing pressures. Between the limits the calculator uses Knudsen’s transitional formula:

So a roughing line hardly matters near atmosphere but throttles the pump toward the end of roughing: with the calculator’s default 35 m³/h pump on this line, the chamber sees essentially the full pump speed near atmosphere but only a third of it at 0.1 mbar. In molecular flow every pipe costs speed, which is why high vacuum pumps are flanged directly to the chamber whenever possible.

Worked example. A 300 L/s turbopump is connected to a chamber through a 40 mm bore tube 30 cm long. The tube’s entrance alone would conduct 11.6 × π(2 cm)² ≈ 146 L/s; with \(L/d = 7.5\) its transmission probability is 0.139, so \(C\) ≈ 20 L/s (Dushman’s approximation gives 22 L/s), and the chamber sees about 19 L/s, roughly 6 % of the pump’s rating. The same pump on a 100 mm bore, 20 cm long port (\(\alpha\) = 0.357, \(C\) ≈ 324 L/s) delivers about 156 L/s. A useful design target is \(C \ge 2\text{–}3\,S\), so that \(S_\text{eff}\) is at least 67–75 % of \(S\); elbows, valves, baffles, and cold traps each subtract further, and the calculator runs these combinations.

One caution: the reciprocal rule assumes elements joined through volumes much larger than their openings. When a port opens straight into a pump inlet of the same bore, it counts the shared opening twice; combining transmission probabilities instead (Oatley’s method, refined by Santeler) gives roughly 190 L/s for the 100 mm port. For real geometries, trust a Monte Carlo model or the vendor’s data.

Pump-down: four regimes

A chamber pumped down from atmosphere passes through distinct regimes. Each has its own time dependence and its own lever for speeding it up.

  1. Volume gas. At first the gas in the chamber volume dominates, and at constant speed \(p(t) = p_0\,e^{-t/\tau}\) with time constant \(\tau = V/S_\text{eff}\), so the time to reach \(p\) is \(t = (V/S_\text{eff})\ln(p_0/p)\). A 100 L chamber on a 10 L/s (36 m³/h) roughing pump has τ = 10 s and reaches 1 mbar from atmosphere in about 69 s, if the pump holds its speed; real pumps slow down near their ultimate pressure (single-stage rotary-vane pumps below about 1 mbar, or 10 mbar with gas ballast), so allow margin.
  2. Surface desorption. Below roughly 10⁻²–10⁻³ mbar on a roughing pump, and lower once a faster high vacuum pump takes over (the crossover is system-dependent), molecules leaving the walls, mainly water, outnumber those left in the volume. The pressure now falls roughly as \(1/t\), the signature of water bound to the surface with a wide spread of binding energies. A bigger pump lowers the curve but does not change its slope: every additional decade of pressure takes about ten times longer.
  3. Diffusion from the bulk. Gas dissolved in a material diffuses to the surface and desorbs, giving a slower \(1/\sqrt{t}\) decline wherever polymers such as O-rings, cable insulation, or plastic parts are present. Hydrogen dissolved in steel also leaves by diffusion, but at room temperature so slowly that its rate hardly changes during a pump-down; only heating reduces it.
  4. Permeation. Gas passing through the walls themselves, such as helium through glass and elastomer O-rings, or water and air through polymer seals, adds a constant gas load. With the hydrogen from steel it sets a floor that no amount of pumping time removes; only a bigger pump or better seals lower it.

The base pressure a chamber finally reaches is \(p_\text{ult} \approx p_\text{pump} + Q_\text{total}/S_\text{eff}\), where \(p_\text{pump}\) is the pump’s own base pressure. The pump-down calculator takes both terms. In most systems the gas-load term dominates, so the base pressure on a pump’s datasheet says little about the chamber.

Outgassing and bake-out

Once the volume gas is gone, the walls set the gas load, so materials, cleanliness, and baking usually matter more than pump size. Outgassing rates are quoted per unit area; the table gives them in mbar·L/(s·cm²). For unbaked materials a rate means little without the pumping time, and values for nominally identical materials scatter by orders of magnitude. Treat these as typical, measure what matters in your own system, and compare results with ISO/TS 20177:2018, which standardizes how outgassing rates are measured and reported:

Material and conditionRate
Stainless steel, cleaned, unbaked, after 1 h10⁻⁹ – 10⁻⁷
Stainless steel, cleaned, unbaked, after 10 h3 × 10⁻¹⁰
Stainless steel baked 24 h at 150 °C (mostly H₂)3 × 10⁻¹²
Fluoroelastomer (FKM), unbaked, after 1 h4 × 10⁻⁷ – 10⁻⁶
Neoprene, after 10 h10⁻⁵

One square meter (10⁴ cm²) of unbaked steel at 10⁻⁸ mbar·L/(s·cm²) produces 10⁻⁴ mbar·L/s, which holds a chamber pumped at 200 L/s at about 5 × 10⁻⁷ mbar. A little of the wrong material can dominate: in a CERN example, 1 cm² of neoprene, 0.01 % of the inner surface of a 1 m² steel vessel, releases more gas after 10 h of pumping than all the steel. The calculator’s optional outgassing input adds such a load to the pump-down.

Water is the main gas in an unbaked system in the high vacuum range. It adsorbs on every surface exposed to room air, and its desorption rate rises steeply with temperature, so heat is the most effective way to shorten the desorption phase:

  • Bake-out. Heating a stainless-steel system to roughly 150–250 °C for one to two days (at least about 12 h above 120 °C) removes most adsorbed water, after which hydrogen diffusing out of the steel is the main gas. Bake temperatures are limited by the weakest component: fluoroelastomer seals (about 150 °C), viewports, feedthroughs, the turbopump inlet (often about 120 °C), sensors, and the product itself. Hotter bakes need metal-gasket knife-edge (CF) flanges, standardized in ISO 3669, which tolerate 350–400 °C.
  • Partial heating still helps. Even moderate heating of the chamber walls shortens the desorption phase; a cooler bake works if it lasts longer.
  • Vent with dry nitrogen. Venting with dry gas and minimizing the time a chamber stands open to humid air limits water re-adsorption. Every exposure to room air undoes part of the previous pump-down or bake.
  • Keep it clean. Fingerprints, cutting fluids, and hydrocarbon films outgas slowly and persistently. Clean, degrease, and handle vacuum-side parts with gloves.

Leaks, virtual leaks, and rate-of-rise

When a chamber pumps down slowly, the first question is whether gas is leaking in or coming off the walls. Leak rates are gas loads, quoted in mbar·L/s (or Pa·m³/s): a leak of 1 mbar·L/s raises the pressure in a closed 1 L volume by 1 mbar every second. A quick system-level diagnostic is the rate-of-rise test: isolate the chamber from its pump and record pressure versus time. The total gas load is

\[ Q = V\,\frac{dp}{dt} \]

A 50 L chamber rising from 1 × 10⁻³ to 2 × 10⁻³ mbar in 100 s has \(Q\) = 5 × 10⁻⁴ mbar·L/s. The shape of the curve indicates the source, although both causes often add:

  • Real leak: a straight-line rise that continues as the pressure climbs, because the gas comes from an effectively unlimited source outside.
  • Outgassing: a rise that bends over and levels off as the gas and the walls approach equilibrium.
  • Virtual leak: gas trapped in an internal pocket (a blind tapped hole under a screw, a double weld enclosing a gap, an O-ring groove, stacked washers, a porous casting) slowly bleeds into the chamber. It behaves like a leak with a long time constant, and spraying helium on the outside finds nothing. The fixes are design fixes: vented screws, single continuous welds on the vacuum side, and no trapped volumes.

Locating a real leak is best done with a helium leak detector, a mass spectrometer tuned to mass 4, used either by spraying helium outside an evacuated part or by sniffing outside a helium-filled part. In vacuum mode the best detectors resolve about 5 × 10⁻¹² mbar·L/s; sniffing is less sensitive, because air already contains 5 ppm of helium. The signal takes about \(3V/S_\text{He}\) to reach 95 % of its final value (\(V\) the evacuated volume, \(S_\text{He}\) the helium pumping speed at the part), so spray sparingly, start at the top (helium rises), and wait.

Leak specifications should come from the application. In a pumped system, leaks should typically contribute no more than a tenth of the working pressure. A sealed device of internal volume \(V\) that may gain no more than \(\Delta p\) over its service life \(t\) needs a total leak-plus-permeation rate \(Q < V\,\Delta p / t\). For 1 L, 10⁻³ mbar over 10 years, that is about 3 × 10⁻¹² mbar·L/s, at the detection limit of the best helium leak detectors. A rate-of-rise test cannot see a leak that small, because the device’s own outgassing is orders of magnitude larger; sealed vacuum products therefore also carry internal getters to pump what still comes in.

Choosing gauges

No single gauge covers the full range, and most read correctly only for the gas they were calibrated in, usually nitrogen.

GaugeRange (mbar)Gas dependence and notes
Capacitance diaphragm (CDG)1000 – 10⁻⁵None: it measures force. Each head covers three to four decades below its full scale; the reference for process control and backfilling.
Pirani (thermal conductivity)~1000 – 10⁻⁴Strong. Inexpensive; most accurate at about 10⁻³–1 mbar; can be badly wrong in He or Ar.
Cold cathode (inverted magnetron)10⁻² – 10⁻⁹Moderate. Rugged, no filament; can be slow to start at low pressure; UHV models reach about 10⁻¹¹ mbar.
Hot cathode (Bayard–Alpert)~10⁻³ – 10⁻¹⁰Moderate. Relative sensitivity ≈ 0.18 for He and ≈ 1.3 for Ar versus N₂; extractor gauges reach about 10⁻¹² mbar.
Residual gas analyzer (RGA)< 10⁻⁴Measures composition directly: water (18), air leaks (28, 32, 40), hydrogen (2), hydrocarbons.

Except for capacitance gauges, read vacuum gauges as good to tens of percent, not as precise numbers. For an ionization gauge in a known gas, divide the nitrogen-equivalent reading by the relative sensitivity: a Bayard–Alpert gauge showing 1.8 × 10⁻⁷ mbar in pure helium means about 1 × 10⁻⁶ mbar. Tabulated factors are nominal and can be off by 10–20 % for an individual gauge.

Two practical rules follow. First, never control a backfill or vent of a non-air gas with a thermal-conductivity gauge. Above roughly 1 mbar its reading can be off by large factors (one maker’s Pirani shows only about 40 mbar when the chamber is full of argon at atmospheric pressure), and chambers have been over-pressurized that way. Use a capacitance manometer. Second, put the gauge where the pressure matters. In molecular flow there are real pressure gradients, and a gauge mounted next to the pump reads lower than the pressure at the work.

An RGA is the fastest way to diagnose a slow system. A spectrum dominated by mass 18 (with 17) means water, which needs time or heat. Peaks at 28 and 32 in roughly a 4:1 ratio, with argon at 40, point to an air leak. Clusters of peaks repeating every 14 mass units (one CH₂ group, as in 29, 43, 57) indicate hydrocarbon contamination.

Quick reference

  • λ (air, 20 °C) ≈ 6.6 mm × (1 Pa / \(p\)), or about 5 cm at 1 mTorr.
  • Viscous flow when \(p\cdot D\) > 0.66 Pa·m; molecular flow when \(p\cdot D\) < 1.3 × 10⁻² Pa·m (air).
  • Molecular-flow conductance (air, 20 °C): aperture \(11.6\,A\ \text{L/s}\) (\(A\) in cm²); long tube \(12.1\,d^3/L\ \text{L/s}\) (\(d\), \(L\) in cm); any tube, \(\alpha\) × its aperture conductance.
  • \(S_\text{eff} = S\,C/(S + C)\); aim for \(C \ge 2\text{–}3\,S\).
  • Volume pump-down: \(t = (V/S_\text{eff}) \ln(p_0/p)\). Base pressure: \(p \approx p_\text{pump} + Q/S_\text{eff}\). Rate-of-rise: \(Q = V\,dp/dt\).
