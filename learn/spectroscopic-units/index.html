Energy Units in Spectroscopy

The same light can be described as 1064 nm, 9398 cm⁻¹, 1.165 eV, 281.8 THz, or 112.4 kJ/mol: a wavelength, a wavenumber, a photon energy, a frequency, and an energy per mole. Each community has its favorite, and mixing them up causes a steady supply of errors. This article explains what each unit is for, how to convert line positions, shifts, and widths correctly, and what room-temperature thermal energy means for which lines appear in a spectrum.

Key terms
Photon energy
The energy carried by one particle of light. It equals the gap between the two energy levels of the atom or molecule that the light connects (a transition). Shorter wavelengths carry more energy.
Electronvolt (eV)
The energy an electron gains across 1 volt. Visible photons carry about 1.8–3.1 eV; breaking a typical chemical bond takes 3–5 eV.
Wavenumber
The number of wavelengths that fit in one centimeter of vacuum, in cm⁻¹: 10 000 divided by the wavelength in micrometers. It is proportional to photon energy and is the usual unit of infrared and Raman spectroscopy.
Molar energy
Energy per mole (6.022 × 10²³ molecules or photons), in kJ/mol or kcal/mol, the way chemists quote bond and reaction energies. 1 eV per molecule is 96.49 kJ/mol.
Hartree
The atomic unit of energy used in quantum chemistry, 27.21 eV.
Raman shift
How far scattered light is moved from the laser that produces it, in cm⁻¹. It equals a vibrational (or rotational) energy of the sample, so it is the same whichever laser is used.
Linewidth
The width of a spectral line at half its peak height (FWHM). The same width can be quoted in nm, GHz, or cm⁻¹, and the conversion between nm and the other two depends on the wavelength.
Thermal energy
The typical energy of random thermal motion, kBT. At room temperature it is about 207 cm⁻¹ or 25.7 meV; energy levels far above it are almost empty.
Air and vacuum wavelength
Light travels slightly slower in air, so its wavelength there is about 0.03 % shorter. Many tables of visible lines list air wavelengths; wavenumbers always refer to vacuum.

One quantity, many units

Every unit in this article measures the same thing: the energy of one photon, or equivalently the energy gap it bridges. Converting between them means multiplying by fixed constants:

\[ \begin{gathered} E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = hc\,\tilde{\nu} \\[4pt] \tilde\nu\,[\mathrm{cm^{-1}}] = \frac{10^7}{\lambda\,[\mathrm{nm}]} \\[4pt] hc = 1239.842\ \mathrm{eV\,nm}. \end{gathered} \]

Since 2019 the SI has fixed exact values for \(h\), \(e\), \(k_B\), and \(N_A\), as it has for \(c\) since 1983, and the thermochemical calorie is defined as exactly 4.184 J. Conversions between eV, cm⁻¹, Hz, J, K, kJ/mol, and kcal/mol therefore carry no uncertainty. Only the hartree, an atomic unit tied to the measured Rydberg constant, has one, 1.1 × 10⁻¹² relative (CODATA 2022). The anchors worth memorizing are 1 eV = 8065.54 cm⁻¹ = 96.485 kJ/mol = 23.061 kcal/mol = 11 604.5 K, and 1 cm⁻¹ = 29.979 GHz. The energy & wavelength converter does every conversion live with the CODATA 2022 constants.

Each field settled on the unit its instruments measure most directly or its calculations produce:

Quantum chemists’ “chemical accuracy” target, 1 kcal/mol (Pople), is 1.59 mEh, 350 cm⁻¹, or 43 meV. That is coarse by spectroscopic standards: gas-phase line positions are routinely measured to 0.01 cm⁻¹ or better, and databases such as HITRAN list each one with its uncertainty.

Why spectroscopists use wavenumbers

Use wavenumbers whenever energies are added or subtracted, which is most of spectroscopy. Wavenumber is proportional to energy, so level spacings add and subtract linearly. A vibrational fundamental (the first step up a vibration’s ladder of levels) at 3000 cm⁻¹ has its first overtone (the jump two steps up) near 6000 cm⁻¹, slightly lower because of anharmonicity: the bond softens as it stretches. Its combination band with a 1500 cm⁻¹ mode, in which both vibrations are excited at once, lies near 4500 cm⁻¹. In wavelength the two fundamentals sit at 3.33 µm and 6.67 µm and the combination band at 2.22 µm, a relationship that is much harder to see at a glance (Herzberg; Bernath).

Wavenumbers are also what interferometers deliver. A Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer records the signal against optical path difference, and the Fourier transform of that record is a spectrum evenly spaced in wavenumber (Griffiths and de Haseth); a grating, by contrast, spreads light out by wavelength. And the cm⁻¹ scale happens to have convenient magnitudes for molecules. Rotational constants run from below 0.01 cm⁻¹ for large molecules to 60.85 cm⁻¹ for H₂, vibrational fundamentals from about 100 to 4000 cm⁻¹ (H₂, at 4161 cm⁻¹, is the highest), and valence electronic transitions mostly from about 10 000 cm⁻¹ (1 µm) upward (Huber and Herzberg).

Converting shifts and linewidths

Wavelength is the reciprocal of energy, not proportional to it, so differences and widths need care: convert the two ends, or use the derivative, never the same number of nanometers everywhere. A Raman shift is a difference in wavenumber, and the scattered wavelength follows from

\[ \lambda_{s} = \left(\frac{1}{\lambda_0} - \frac{\Delta\tilde\nu}{10^7}\right)^{-1}\ [\mathrm{nm}]. \]

With 532 nm excitation, a 1000 cm⁻¹ Stokes line (scattered light that has given energy to the sample) appears at 561.9 nm and the anti-Stokes line (light that has taken energy from it) at 505.1 nm. These are not symmetric about 532 nm. A 3000 cm⁻¹ C–H stretch appears at 633.0 nm. With 785 nm excitation, the same 1000 cm⁻¹ shift lands at 851.9 nm. These are vacuum wavelengths; a spectrometer calibrated against air wavelengths reads about 0.03 % lower, 0.16 nm at 562 nm.

For small intervals, differentiate \(\tilde\nu = 10^7/\lambda\):

\[ \begin{gathered} \Delta\lambda\,[\mathrm{nm}] \approx 10^{-7}\,\lambda^2[\mathrm{nm}]\;\Delta\tilde\nu\,[\mathrm{cm^{-1}}] \\[4pt] \Delta\nu\,[\mathrm{GHz}] = 29.979\,\Delta\tilde\nu\,[\mathrm{cm^{-1}}]. \end{gathered} \]

A 1 nm spectrometer bandpass therefore corresponds to 268 cm⁻¹ at 193 nm, 40 cm⁻¹ at 500 nm, and only 8.8 cm⁻¹ at 1064 nm. A 0.1 cm⁻¹ laser linewidth is 3.0 GHz at any wavelength, but it is 0.0025 nm at 500 nm and 0.011 nm at 1064 nm. When comparing resolutions across a spectrum, convert to cm⁻¹ or GHz first; the IUPAC Green Book likewise quotes resolution as a width on a wavenumber or frequency scale. Two calculators on this site report every width in all three units. With its default inputs, the Doppler broadening & linewidth calculator finds that hydrogen’s 656.28 nm Balmer-α line from a 10 000 K gas is 46.8 pm wide, which is 32.6 GHz or 1.087 cm⁻¹. In the diffraction grating & spectrometer calculator, a 1200 lines/mm grating at 30° incidence, with a 300 mm focusing mirror and a 50 µm slit, passes 0.138 nm at 500 nm, which is 5.5 cm⁻¹.

Thermal energy and populations

Temperature decides which energy levels are already occupied, and therefore which lines a sample shows. The rule of thumb: levels far above \(k_BT\) are nearly empty, and levels at or below it are well populated. The Boltzmann factor gives the ratio of molecules in an upper and a lower level at temperature \(T\):

\[ \frac{N_u}{N_l} = \frac{g_u}{g_l}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta E}{k_B T}\right), \]

where \(g_u\) and \(g_l\) count the distinct states that share each level. At 298.15 K (25 °C), \(k_BT\) = 207.2 cm⁻¹ = 25.69 meV = 2.479 kJ/mol. For a single vibration treated as a harmonic oscillator with no degeneracy, the fraction of molecules with that vibration excited, in any level above the ground one, is exactly \(\exp(-hc\tilde\nu/k_BT)\).

  • A 2000 cm⁻¹ vibration has \(e^{-2000/207.2} \approx 6\times10^{-5}\) of its molecules vibrationally excited at room temperature. At 1000 K this rises to 5.6 %, and “hot bands”, absorption that starts from an already-excited level, appear in the spectrum.
  • A 500 cm⁻¹ low-frequency mode is 9 % excited at room temperature. Floppy molecules, which have many such modes, have congested room-temperature spectra for this reason.
  • Rotational levels spaced by a few cm⁻¹ are thermally populated over many levels. That is why room-temperature rotational envelopes are broad.
  • A 2 eV electronic excitation has a Boltzmann factor of about 10⁻³⁴ at 298 K. At room temperature, electronically excited states are populated essentially only by absorbing light, by energetic collisions in plasmas and discharges, or by chemical reactions.

Supersonic jet expansions (Levy) and cryogenic ion traps (Rizzo, Stearns, and Boyarkin) cool molecules to rotational temperatures of about 10 K or below, where \(k_BT\) is only a few cm⁻¹. Almost everything is then in the ground vibrational level and the lowest rotational levels, so spectra simplify dramatically. This is the main reason gas-phase spectroscopy goes to the trouble of cooling.

Photon energies and chemical bonds

Comparing photon energies with bond energies explains a lot about laser–material interactions. Most single bonds in organic molecules and polymers, such as C–C, C–H, C–O, and O–H, take roughly 3–5 eV (300–480 kJ/mol) to break (Blanksby and Ellison).

A single deep-UV photon carries enough energy to break a typical bond directly. That is the picture behind “cold” photochemical ablation of polymers with excimer lasers, first reported by Srinivasan and Mayne-Banton in 1982; in practice heating also contributes, and the balance between photochemical and photothermal mechanisms depends on the polymer and the wavelength (Lippert and Dickinson). A 1064 nm photon carries a quarter to a third of a bond energy, and a CO₂ photon a thirtieth to a fortieth. Infrared processing is therefore thermal: energy is absorbed into vibrations and heat before anything breaks, unless intensities are high enough for multiphoton absorption, in which several photons are absorbed at once.

Common pitfalls

  • Air versus vacuum wavelengths. Atomic line tables usually give wavelengths between 200 and 2000 nm in standard air (NIST’s Atomic Spectra Database does so by default), which differs from vacuum by about 0.03 %: 0.175 nm at 633 nm. Balmer-α is 656.28 nm in air and 656.46 nm in vacuum. Wavenumbers always refer to vacuum, so convert an air wavelength to vacuum before taking \(10^7/\lambda\); the energy converter shows both.
  • Averaging in the wrong unit. The mean of two wavelengths is not the wavelength of the mean energy: halfway between 400 and 800 nm in energy is 533 nm, not 600 nm. Take averages and differences in energy units.
  • Ambiguous “per molecule” and “per mole”. Convert kJ/mol to eV with the factor 96.485, not 100. The 3.5 % error from the shortcut is larger than many effects people are trying to measure.
  • Mixing calories. Thermochemistry uses the thermochemical calorie, exactly 4.184 J. The international-table calorie, 4.1868 J, appears in older engineering data.
  • Dropping the factor of 2π. Angular frequency \(\omega = 2\pi\nu\) and \(\hbar\omega = h\nu\). Mixing rad/s and Hz gives a 6.28× error that looks plausible at first glance. The same trap exists for wavenumbers: physicists’ angular wavenumber \(k = 2\pi/\lambda\), in rad/m, is \(2\pi\) times the spectroscopic \(\tilde\nu = 1/\lambda\).
  • “Wavenumbers” as a unit. Write “a band at 1600 cm⁻¹”, not “at 1600 wavenumbers”: wavenumber is the quantity and cm⁻¹ the unit (IUPAC).
