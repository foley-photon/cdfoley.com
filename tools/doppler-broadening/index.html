Doppler Broadening & Linewidth Calculator

How wide is a spectral line from a gas or plasma, and which effect sets that width? Enter the line’s wavelength, the gas temperature, and the mass of the emitting atom to get the Doppler width from thermal motion. Add the lifetime of the excited state, any pressure or Stark broadening, and your spectrometer’s resolution to get the width you would actually measure, the Voigt width. Use it to compare a measured line width with what a temperature predicts, to check whether a spectrometer can resolve a line, or to see whether a laser is narrower than the line it probes. Every width is given in wavelength, frequency, and wavenumber.

Key terms
Linewidth (FWHM)
The width of a spectral line at half its peak height, the full width at half maximum. It can be quoted as a wavelength (pm or nm), a frequency (MHz or GHz), or a wavenumber (cm⁻¹).
Doppler broadening
Atoms moving toward you emit or absorb at a slightly higher frequency, atoms moving away at a lower one. In a gas, thermal motion spreads a line into a bell-shaped (Gaussian) profile that widens with temperature and is narrower for heavier atoms.
Natural linewidth
The width set by how long the excited state lives: the shorter the lifetime, the broader the line. For strong lines it is a few MHz to about 100 MHz, usually far below the Doppler width.
Pressure and Stark broadening
Collisions with other atoms (pressure broadening) and the electric fields of nearby electrons and ions in a plasma (Stark broadening) disturb the emitting atom and widen the line. Both grow with the density of the gas or plasma.
Gaussian, Lorentzian, and Voigt profiles
Line shapes. A Gaussian falls off quickly beyond its half width; a Lorentzian has much broader wings. When both kinds of broadening act, the line is a Voigt profile, a blend of the two.
Instrument function
The shape a perfectly narrow line takes in your spectrometer, set mainly by its slits or pixels. Its width, the instrumental resolution, adds to every measured line width.
Wavenumber
The number of waves per centimeter, 1/λ, in cm⁻¹. Like frequency, it is proportional to photon energy, so a width in cm⁻¹ or GHz is the same energy spread at any wavelength; a width in nm is not.
Air and vacuum wavelength
Light is slightly slower in air, so its wavelength there is about 0.03 % shorter. Many tables list visible lines in air (Hα: 656.28 nm in air, 656.46 nm in vacuum). The calculator expects the vacuum value, but an air value changes the widths by at most 0.03 %.

Inputs

The vacuum wavelength of the line, for example from the NIST Atomic Spectra Database, which lists air wavelengths between 200 and 2000 nm unless you ask for vacuum. An air wavelength changes the widths by at most 0.03 %.

The kinetic temperature of the emitting atoms or ions: about 300 K for a gas or vapor cell at room temperature, thousands of kelvin in flames, arcs, and plasmas. In a plasma, use the gas or ion temperature, which can be far below the electron temperature.

u

Standard atomic weights (CIAAW 2024), averaged over the natural mix of isotopes; argon’s 39.948 u is the value for argon separated from air. For a single isotope, type its mass (⁸⁷Rb: 86.909 u). For a molecule, add the atomic weights (N₂: 28.014 u).

How long the upper level lives: 1 divided by the sum of its transition probabilities (Einstein A values, listed in the NIST database) to all lower levels. Strong allowed lines have 1–100 ns; see the table below. If the lower level also decays, enter the effective lifetime 1/(1/τu + 1/τl). Leave blank to ignore natural broadening.

Collisions (pressure broadening) and, in plasmas, the electric fields of electrons and ions (Stark broadening) add widths that grow with density. Stark broadening usually dominates in discharges and plasmas: hydrogen’s Hβ line is about 1 nm wide at an electron density of 10¹⁶ cm⁻³. Leave blank if negligible.

Your spectrometer’s resolution: the measured FWHM of a line much narrower than it (a low-pressure lamp line or a single-frequency laser), or the bandpass from the diffraction grating calculator. It is treated as a Gaussian and added to the Doppler width in quadrature.

Line profiles (peak-normalized)

How it is calculated

Doppler broadening. Thermal motion along the line of sight shifts each emitter’s frequency by \(\nu_0 v_z/c\). A Maxwell–Boltzmann velocity distribution turns this into a Gaussian line shape with full width at half maximum

\[ \begin{gathered} \Delta\nu_D = \nu_0\sqrt{\frac{8 k_B T \ln 2}{m c^2}} \\[4pt] \frac{\Delta\lambda_D}{\lambda_0} = \frac{\Delta\nu_D}{\nu_0} \approx 7.162\times10^{-7}\sqrt{\frac{T\,[\text{K}]}{M\,[\text{u}]}} \end{gathered} \]

Here \(m = M m_u\), with \(m_u\) the atomic mass constant (1 u), and the factor \(\sqrt{8k_B\ln 2/(m_u c^2)} = 7.1623\times10^{-7}\) follows from the CODATA 2022 constants; NIST’s atomic spectroscopy compendium gives the same formula with 7.16 × 10⁻⁷.

Natural broadening. A level that decays exponentially with lifetime \(\tau\) emits a Lorentzian line with FWHM \(\Delta\nu_N = 1/(2\pi\tau)\), where \(1/\tau\) is the level’s total decay rate, the sum of its Einstein A coefficients. If the lower level also decays, the rates add: \(\Delta\nu_N = (1/\tau_u + 1/\tau_l)/(2\pi)\). Pressure (collisional) and Stark broadening often give approximately Lorentzian profiles too; enter their widths as the additional FWHM. Lorentzian widths add linearly, because two Lorentzians convolve to a Lorentzian whose width is the sum.

Voigt profile. When both kinds of broadening act, the line is the convolution of the Gaussian and the Lorentzian. Gaussian widths add in quadrature, so an instrument width enters as \(f_G = \sqrt{\Delta\lambda_D^2 + \Delta\lambda_\text{inst}^2}\), and \(f_L\) is the sum of the Lorentzian widths. The Voigt FWHM is well approximated by Olivero and Longbothum’s fit:

\[ f_V \approx 0.5346\, f_L + \sqrt{0.2166\, f_L^2 + f_G^2} . \]

Compared with the exact Voigt width, the fit is never off by more than 0.024 % (the largest error is near \(f_L \approx 0.3\,f_G\)), which matches the 0.02 % usually quoted for it. The calculator also computes the Voigt profile itself, by numerical convolution, and reports the FWHM it measures from that profile as a cross-check. Widths convert between units with \(\Delta\lambda = \lambda_0^2\Delta\nu/c\) and \(\Delta\tilde\nu = \Delta\nu/c\), which hold for widths much smaller than \(\lambda_0\). These conversions take \(\lambda_0\) as the vacuum wavelength. If you enter a tabulated air wavelength instead (Hα: 656.28 nm in air, 656.46 nm in vacuum), the results change by at most about 0.03 %, far less than the uncertainty of a temperature or lifetime.

Worked example

Hydrogen Balmer-α (656.28 nm) emitted from a 10 000 K gas: \(\Delta\lambda_D = 656.28\ \text{nm}\times 7.162\times10^{-7}\sqrt{10\,000/1.008} = 46.8\) pm, equal to 32.6 GHz or 1.087 cm⁻¹. A 10 ns lifetime adds only 15.9 MHz (0.023 pm) of natural width, so the line is almost purely Gaussian. (For Hα itself, the upper 3d level lives 15.5 ns and the lower 2p level only 1.60 ns, so the natural width of its strongest components is \((1/15.5\ \text{ns} + 1/1.60\ \text{ns})/2\pi \approx 110\) MHz, still negligible.) Adding 10 pm of Stark broadening as the additional Lorentzian FWHM widens the Voigt FWHM to 52.4 pm. Read the other way, a measured Doppler width of 46.8 pm means 10 000 K, and because the width grows as \(\sqrt{T}\), a width 10 % larger would mean a gas 21 % hotter.

Assumptions and limits

  • Thermal equilibrium. The emitters have a Maxwellian velocity distribution at a single temperature, with no bulk flow and no sight-line averaging over temperature gradients. In a plasma the Doppler width measures the temperature of the emitting atoms or ions, which can be far below the electron temperature.
  • Inhomogeneous vs. homogeneous. Doppler broadening is inhomogeneous; natural, pressure, and Stark broadening are treated as homogeneous Lorentzians. Real pressure- and Stark-broadened profiles can be asymmetric and shifted (van der Waals broadening, for example, shifts a line toward the red by about a third of its width), so treat the Voigt width as an estimate there.
  • No fine, hyperfine, or isotope structure. Unresolved components can make a line appear wider than its Doppler width. In Hα, for example, the two main fine-structure components are about 0.33 cm⁻¹ (14 pm) apart. Summing all seven components, each weighted by its expected intensity (g·A, for statistically populated levels), widens the observed line by about 6 % at 10 000 K and about 70 % at 1000 K.
  • Instrument function. A spectrometer’s slit function is often close to Gaussian or triangular. Enter its FWHM as the instrument width: the calculator treats it as a Gaussian and adds it to \(\Delta\lambda_D\) in quadrature, which is exact for a Gaussian slit function. For a triangular one (equal entrance and exit slits) the quadrature sum can be up to 6 % too small, the worst case being a triangle about three times as wide as the Gaussian. If the instrument function is closer to Lorentzian, enter it in the additional Lorentzian FWHM instead.
  • Other broadening. Transit-time broadening (atoms crossing a narrow laser beam) and power broadening (strong resonant light) are not modeled. Saturated-absorption and two-photon spectroscopy remove most of the Doppler width, leaving the natural and pressure widths.
  • Narrow lines. The first-order Doppler shift and the linear conversions between wavelength, frequency, and wavenumber assume the width is a small fraction of \(\lambda_0\). Above about 10 % of \(\lambda_0\), the results are only approximate and the badge says so.
