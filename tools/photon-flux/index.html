Photon Energy & Flux Calculator

Count the photons in a beam. Enter a wavelength and an average power, or a pulse energy and repetition rate, to get photons per second and per pulse, the photon flux density for a Gaussian beam, and the responsivity and photocurrent of a detector with a given quantum efficiency.

Key terms
Photon energy
The energy of one particle of light, set by its wavelength: 2.33 eV at 532 nm, 1.17 eV at 1064 nm. Shorter wavelengths carry more energy per photon.
Photon rate
How many photons pass per second. A 1 mW green (532 nm) beam carries about 2.7 × 10¹⁵ photons per second.
Photon flux density
Photons per second per unit area, here per cm². It is highest on the beam axis.
Pulse energy and repetition rate
The energy in each pulse and the number of pulses per second. Their product is the average power.
1/e² beam diameter
The diameter at which the intensity has fallen to 13.5 % of its peak. It contains 86.5 % of a Gaussian beam’s power.
Quantum efficiency
The fraction of incoming photons that a detector turns into electrons it counts. 80 % means 8 electrons for every 10 photons.
Responsivity
A detector’s output current per watt of light, in A/W. A perfect detector (100 %) gives 0.43 A/W at 532 nm and 0.86 A/W at 1064 nm.
Photocurrent
The current a photodiode delivers: responsivity times the optical power.

How it is calculated

A photon of vacuum wavelength \(\lambda\) carries energy \(E_\text{ph} = hc/\lambda\). In convenient units that is \(E_\text{ph}\,[\text{eV}] = 1239.842/\lambda\,[\text{nm}]\). The photon rate in a beam of average power \(P\), and the number of photons in a pulse of energy \(E_p\), are

\[ \begin{gathered} \Phi = \frac{P}{E_\text{ph}} = \frac{P\lambda}{hc}, \\[4pt] N_p = \frac{E_p}{E_\text{ph}} = \frac{P}{f\,E_\text{ph}} . \end{gathered} \]

For a Gaussian beam of 1/e² radius \(w\), the on-axis photon flux density is twice the photon rate divided by \(\pi w^2\): \(\phi_0 = 2\Phi/(\pi w^2)\). The “average” shown is \(\Phi/(\pi w^2)\), the usual convention; it is not the mean over the 1/e² circle, which holds only 86.5 % of the photons. The same factor of two applies to the peak photon fluence per pulse.

Detector responsivity and quantum efficiency

A detector with external quantum efficiency \(\eta\) produces \(\eta\) electrons per incident photon. For a camera, EMVA 1288 defines this “total quantum efficiency” over the whole pixel area, so it includes the fill factor and microlenses. The current responsivity of a photodiode is

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathcal{R} = \frac{\eta\, e\, \lambda}{hc} \;\approx\; \eta\,\frac{\lambda\,[\text{nm}]}{1239.842}\ \text{A/W}, \\[4pt] I = \mathcal{R} P . \end{gathered} \]

The relation also runs backwards: a measured responsivity gives \(\eta = \mathcal{R}\,hc/(e\lambda)\), which is how Hamamatsu converts its photosensitivity curves to quantum efficiency. Even an ideal detector (\(\eta = 1\)) has a responsivity that rises linearly with wavelength, because a watt of red light contains more photons than a watt of blue. Silicon photodiodes therefore respond most strongly in the near infrared, roughly 900–1000 nm, where their quantum efficiency is still high. At longer wavelengths silicon absorbs too weakly and the response falls steeply toward the cutoff near 1100 nm, where photons no longer bridge its 1.12 eV band gap (Hamamatsu).

Worked example

A 1 mW, 532 nm beam: \(E_\text{ph} = 2.3305\) eV \(= 3.7339\times10^{-19}\) J, so \(\Phi = 2.678\times10^{15}\) photons/s. At 10 kHz that is \(2.678\times10^{11}\) photons per pulse. Over a 1 mm (1/e²) beam the average flux density is \(3.41\times10^{17}\) photons s⁻¹ cm⁻² and the on-axis value is \(6.82\times10^{17}\). A photodiode with \(\eta = 80\,\%\) has \(\mathcal{R} = 0.343\) A/W and delivers 343 µA.

Assumptions and limits

  • Vacuum wavelength. The photon energy depends on frequency, so use the vacuum wavelength. Air wavelengths differ by about 0.03 %, which is negligible here.
  • Monochromatic light. For broadband sources, integrate \(P_\lambda \lambda/hc\) over the spectrum. Using the center wavelength is a good approximation when the bandwidth is small.
  • Gaussian spatial profile. The peak-to-average factor of 2 holds for a TEM₀₀ beam. Flat-top beams have a factor near 1.
  • Linear detector. Responsivity assumes no saturation, gain, or multiphoton response. Avalanche and photomultiplier detectors multiply the current by their internal gain. In the deep ultraviolet one photon can free more than one electron (EMVA 1288), so a quantum efficiency computed from responsivity can exceed 100 % there even without gain.
