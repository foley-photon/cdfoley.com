Vacuum Conductance & Pump-Down Time Calculator

This calculator answers two questions: how long a chamber takes to pump down, and how much of the pump’s speed actually reaches it through the pumping line. Use it to size a pump or a line, or to see why a pump-down slows near the end. The line’s conductance is recalculated at every pressure, from viscous flow near atmosphere to molecular flow at low pressure; an optional outgassing load and pump base pressure set the lowest pressure the chamber can reach.

Key terms
Pumping speed
The volume of gas a pump removes per second at its inlet, in L/s or m³/h, as rated on its datasheet. Roughing pumps are usually rated in m³/h (1 m³/h is about 0.28 L/s), high vacuum pumps in L/s.
Conductance
How easily gas passes through a pipe, valve, or opening, in the same units as pumping speed. A pipe throttles a pump when its conductance is not much larger than the pump’s speed.
Effective pumping speed
The speed the chamber actually gets once a line, valve, or trap sits between it and the pump. It is always lower than both the pump’s speed and the conductance, and it sets how fast the pressure falls.
Viscous and molecular flow
Near atmosphere gas flows through a pipe like a fluid (viscous flow), and the pipe conducts more the higher the pressure. At low pressure the molecules hit the walls far more often than each other (molecular flow), and the conductance no longer depends on pressure. In between, the flow is transitional.
Knudsen number
The mean free path, the average distance a molecule travels between collisions, divided by the pipe’s bore. Below 0.01 the flow is viscous, above 0.5 molecular. In room-temperature air the mean free path is about 66 µm at 1 mbar and 66 mm at 0.001 mbar.
Gas load
The amount of gas entering the chamber per second, given as pressure times volume per second (mbar·L/s). Outgassing, leaks, and process gas are all gas loads, and the pump has to remove them on top of the gas in the volume.
Outgassing
The slow release of gas, mostly water vapor, from the inside surfaces and materials of a vacuum system. After the first few minutes of pumping it usually sets the pressure.
Ultimate pressure
The lowest pressure a chamber can reach, where the pump removes gas exactly as fast as the gas load brings it in. A pump’s datasheet gives its own ultimate (base) pressure, measured with the inlet closed; the chamber’s is always higher.

Inputs

Chamber and pump

The empty inside volume of the chamber and of anything open to it. A 30 cm cube holds 27 L.

The rated speed from the pump’s datasheet, measured at its inlet (ISO 21360-1). Treated as constant except near the base pressure below. Single-stage rotary-vane pumps slow down sooner, below about 1 mbar (10 mbar with gas ballast), so for a target there use the speed from the pump’s curve.

The lowest pressure the pump reaches with its inlet closed, the “ultimate pressure” on its datasheet (use the total-pressure value if two are given). Near it the pump’s speed falls as S(1 − ppump/p), and the chamber cannot get below it.

Start from atmosphere (1013 mbar) for a full pump-down. The target is the chamber pressure you need, for example the pressure at which a high vacuum pump can take over.

Pumping line

The bore and length of the tube or hose between chamber and pump. Vacuum fittings are sized by nominal width (DN), close to the bore in millimeters: DN 25 is about 24 mm, DN 63 about 70 mm. Set the length to 0 if the pump is mounted directly on the chamber.

How it is calculated

A chamber of volume \(V\) pumped with effective speed \(S_\text{eff}\) by a pump with base pressure \(p_\text{pump}\), against a gas load \(Q\), obeys

\[ \begin{gathered} V\frac{dp}{dt} = -S_\text{eff}(p)\,(p - p_\text{pump}) + Q, \\ t = \int_{p_1}^{p_0} \frac{V\,dp}{S_\text{eff}(p)\,(p - p_\text{pump}) - Q}. \end{gathered} \]

With constant \(S_\text{eff}\), no gas load, and \(p_\text{pump} = 0\) this reduces to the familiar \(t = (V/S_\text{eff})\ln(p_0/p_1)\). The pump removes \(S\,(p_\text{in} - p_\text{pump})\) at its inlet pressure \(p_\text{in}\), the textbook model of a pump that slows near its base pressure. The pressure cannot fall below the ultimate pressure, where pumping and gas load balance:

\[ \begin{gathered} S_\text{eff}\,(p_\text{ult} - p_\text{pump}) = Q, \\ p_\text{ult} = p_\text{pump} + \frac{Q}{S_\text{eff}}. \end{gathered} \]

The calculator integrates the full expression numerically, because the line conductance changes with pressure by orders of magnitude. It integrates in the variable \(\ln(p - p_\text{ult})\), which stays accurate even for a target just above \(p_\text{ult}\). The pump and the line act in series, so their reciprocals add (the same rule as for resistors in parallel):

\[ \frac{1}{S_\text{eff}} = \frac{1}{S} + \frac{1}{C}, \qquad \frac{1}{C} = \frac{1}{C_\text{line}} + \frac{1}{C_\text{other}}. \]

Conductance of a round tube

Molecular flow (mean free path larger than the tube). For a long tube and for a thin orifice of the same diameter,

\[ \begin{gathered} C_\text{tube} = \frac{\pi}{12}\,\bar v\,\frac{d^3}{L}, \qquad C_\text{orifice} = \frac{\bar v}{4}\cdot\frac{\pi d^2}{4}, \\ \bar v = \sqrt{\frac{8RT}{\pi M}}. \end{gathered} \]

For air at 20 °C (\(\bar v\) = 463 m/s) these become the well-known rules of thumb \(C \approx 12.1\,d^3/L\) L/s (with d and L in cm) and 11.6 L/s per cm² of orifice area. Real tubes have an entrance as well as a length. The calculator multiplies the orifice conductance by the tube’s transmission probability \(\alpha\) (Clausing’s problem), using Berman’s closed form (rearranged here to avoid round-off), which agrees with exact numerical solutions to better than 0.1 % at every length:

\[ \begin{gathered} C_\text{mol} = \alpha\,C_\text{orifice}, \quad y = \frac{L}{d}, \\ \alpha = \frac{s}{s + y} - \frac{N^2}{4.5\,(y s - \operatorname{arsinh} y)}, \\ N = \frac{y^2\,(s + 2y - 1)}{(s + 1)(s + y)}, \quad s = \sqrt{1 + y^2}. \end{gathered} \]

It runs from \(\alpha = 1\) for a thin orifice to \(\alpha \to 4d/3L\) for a long tube. For a check by hand, Santeler’s simpler formula stays within 0.7 % of it at any length:

\[ \alpha \approx \left[\,1 + \frac{3L}{4d}\left(1 + \frac{1}{3 + \tfrac{6L}{7d}}\right)\right]^{-1}. \]

The popular series approximation \(\alpha \approx 1/(1 + 3L/4d)\) (Dushman) is up to about 12 % high when L is one to two diameters.

Viscous flow (mean free path much smaller than the tube). Laminar Poiseuille flow gives

\[ C_\text{visc} = \frac{\pi d^4}{128\,\eta L}\,\bar p , \]

where \(\bar p\) is the mean pressure in the line. For air at 20 °C this is about \(136\,d^4\bar p/L\) L/s with d and L in cm and \(\bar p\) in mbar. Conductance in this regime is proportional to pressure, so a line that is wide open at 100 mbar can throttle the pump at 0.1 mbar. The calculator finds \(\bar p\) self-consistently as the mean of the chamber pressure and the pump-inlet pressure \(p_\text{in} = p_\text{pump} + (p - p_\text{pump})\,S_\text{eff}/S\).

Transitional flow. Between the two limits the calculator uses Knudsen’s semi-empirical formula,

\[ \begin{gathered} C_\text{line} = C_\text{visc} + C_\text{mol}\,\frac{1 + a}{1 + 1.235\,a}, \\ a = \frac{d\,\bar p}{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{M}{RT}}. \end{gathered} \]

For air at 20 °C, \(a \approx 190\,d\bar p\) (d in cm, \(\bar p\) in mbar), which gives the familiar long-tube form, in L/s:

\[ C \approx 136\,\frac{d^4\bar p}{L} + 12.1\,\frac{d^3}{L}\cdot\frac{1 + 190\,d\bar p}{1 + 235\,d\bar p}. \]

It has the correct limits on both sides. Against kinetic-theory solutions for long tubes (the BGK model and the Boltzmann equation for hard-sphere molecules, as compiled by Sharipov and Seleznev), it is always slightly high: by at most about 6 %, near Kn ≈ 0.1–0.2, and by about 4 % at the shallow Knudsen minimum near Kn ≈ 1.5, which it places at the right pressure but makes only about half as deep. The plain sum \(C_\text{visc} + C_\text{mol}\), a common shortcut, is up to about 20 % high. The formula was derived for long tubes (L at least about 10 d). For shorter tubes in this range treat the result as an estimate.

With a constant rate the calculator gives a floor on the pressure; real outgassing keeps falling with pumping time.

Worked example

A 50 L chamber pumped by a 35 m³/h (9.72 L/s) rotary-vane pump through 1 m of 25 mm bore line (KF25), down to 0.1 mbar with air at 20 °C:

  • Without the line, \(t = (50/9.72)\ln(1013/0.1) = 47\) s.
  • With the line, the conductance is about 50 000 L/s near atmosphere (laminar) but only 5.0 L/s at 0.1 mbar, where the flow is already transitional (Kn ≈ 0.04 in the line): the laminar term alone gives 3.5 L/s at the mean line pressure of 0.067 mbar and the molecular term adds the rest. There \(S_\text{eff}\) falls to 3.3 L/s, 34 % of the pump’s speed, and the pump-down takes about 58 s.
  • Near atmosphere the flow in the line is turbulent (Re ≈ 3 × 10⁴), so the line actually conducts roughly ten times less than the laminar value. That is still hundreds of times the pump’s speed, so the result is unaffected.
  • In molecular flow the same line conducts only 1.8 L/s. A turbo pump behind this line would lose most of its speed. High vacuum pumps are mounted directly on the chamber for this reason.
  • An unbaked chamber with 5000 cm² of internal surface outgassing at 10⁻⁸ mbar·L/(s·cm²) adds Q = 5 × 10⁻⁵ mbar·L/s. Through this line, that limits the chamber’s ultimate pressure to about 3 × 10⁻⁵ mbar, however large the pump.

Assumptions and limits

  • Pump speed. Constant, except near the pump’s base pressure, where the pump removes \(S\,(p_\text{in} - p_\text{pump})\) as in Leybold’s textbook treatment. Real pump curves can fall off sooner, and at both ends of their range: use the speed near the pressure that dominates the pump-down time, or run the calculation in segments.
  • Outgassing is time-dependent. Desorption of water from metal surfaces typically falls roughly as \(1/t\). A constant \(q\) is a snapshot that sets a floor on pressure, but it does not model the slow tail. Below about 10⁻² to 10⁻³ mbar, outgassing, not volume, usually sets the pump-down time. Use measured or vendor data for your materials and surface condition.
  • Isothermal, quasi-static flow. Fast roughing from atmosphere cools the gas and can condense water.
  • Laminar, subsonic flow in the line. Near atmosphere the flow in a roughing line is often turbulent: the calculator reports the Reynolds number at p₀, which marks laminar flow below about 2300 and turbulent flow above about 4000, and a turbulent line conducts less than the Poiseuille value. A large pump on a narrow line can also choke it: the gas cannot enter faster than about the speed of sound, which caps the throughput near \(0.68\,p\,\pi d^2/4 \cdot \sqrt{RT/M}\) for air and other diatomic gases (0.73 for helium and argon). The calculator flags both but does not model them, so in those cases the real pump-down near atmosphere is slower than calculated.
  • Gas properties. The preset viscosities match NIST data at 20 °C (air to within 0.5 %) and follow a power of the absolute temperature fitted to the same data, within about 3 % from −70 °C to 330 °C. The molecular diameter, used only for the Knudsen number, does not change with temperature.
  • Straight round tubes only. Bends, flexible bellows, and rectangular or annular ducts conduct less. Use vendor data or a Monte Carlo model for them.
  • Elements joined through large volumes. Adding 1/S and 1/C assumes the line and the pump meet through a volume much larger than their openings. When the line bore equals the pump inlet, the sum counts the shared opening twice, so in molecular flow the calculated effective speed is conservative; combining transmission probabilities (Oatley 1957, with Santeler’s 1986 correction) gives about 20 % more for a 100 mm, 20 cm port on a 300 L/s pump.
