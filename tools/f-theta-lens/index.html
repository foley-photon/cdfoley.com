F-Theta Scan Lens Calculator

How large an area can a galvo laser scanner reach with a given f-theta lens, how small is the focused spot, and how finely can the spot be placed? Use it to choose a lens and scan head for laser marking, engraving, cutting, or drilling. It also reports two numbers that often limit real processes: the usable depth of focus, and how far the mark moves when the part sits above or below the focal plane.

Key terms
Galvo scanner
Two small mirrors, each turned by a fast galvanometer motor, that steer the laser beam in x and y before it enters the lens.
F-theta lens
A scan lens designed so the spot moves the same distance for every degree the beam is turned, and stays in focus on a flat plane. Focal lengths of about 100–250 mm are common; a longer lens gives a larger field and a larger spot.
Optical angle
How far the beam itself is turned. A mirror that rotates by 10° turns the beam by 20°, so the optical angle is twice the mechanical (mirror) angle.
Scan field
The square area on the work surface that the spot can reach. Its side is twice the focal length times the optical half-angle in radians.
Spot diameter (1/e²)
The diameter of the focused beam where the intensity falls to 13.5 % of its center value. Marking lenses typically focus to a few tens of micrometers.
Beam quality, M²
How close the beam is to an ideal Gaussian beam, for which M² = 1. The spot grows in proportion to M², so take the value from the laser datasheet.
Depth of focus
The height range over which the spot stays within the size tolerance you set, half above and half below best focus.
Telecentric lens
A scan lens that sends the beam straight down onto the work everywhere in the field, so a height error blurs the spot but does not move it.

Effective focal length from the lens datasheet, for example 100, 160, or 254 mm. It is not the working distance.

How it is calculated

A standard focusing lens places the spot at \(y = f\tan\theta\), which is non-linear in scan angle, and it focuses onto a curved surface. An f-theta lens is designed with controlled barrel distortion so that

\[ y = f\,\theta, \]

where \(\theta\) is the optical scan angle in radians. Spot position is then linear in mirror angle. The design also corrects field curvature, so the focal surface is nearly flat. With two galvo mirrors each deflecting the beam by up to ±θ, the field is a square of side \(2f\theta\). The corners need a combined field angle near \(\sqrt{2}\,\theta\), so check that the lens is specified for the full diagonal. A datasheet that quotes a single scan angle may mean this corner angle (see the example lenses below).

The spot size and depth of focus follow Gaussian-beam optics for a collimated beam focused by the lens:

\[ \begin{gathered} d_F = \frac{4M^2\lambda f}{\pi D}, \quad z_R = \frac{\pi d_F^2}{4M^2\lambda}, \\[8pt] \mathrm{DOF} = 2z_R\sqrt{(1+\varepsilon)^2-1}, \end{gathered} \]

where \(\varepsilon\) is the allowed fractional growth of the spot diameter (the DOF tolerance, so 5 % is 0.05). DOF is the total range, half on each side of best focus. These formulas assume the beam is not clipped by the galvo mirrors. Scanner apertures are small, so truncation is common. Use the focused spot tool with the scanner aperture as \(D_t\) to include it.

Galvo positioning resolution is the full optical scan range divided by the number of command steps: \(\Delta y = f \cdot 2\theta / 2^{N}\) for an \(N\)-bit command. This assumes the full command range spans ±θ. If the scanner’s range is wider than the field you use, each step is larger in proportion.

Telecentricity and height errors

In a non-telecentric f-theta lens the beam reaches the work surface at an angle close to the scan angle; the exact landing angle depends on the lens design and on where the mirrors sit. A part that sits \(\Delta z\) above or below the focal plane is therefore marked at a position shifted by about \(\Delta z\tan\theta\). It is also out of focus. At a 20° field edge, a 1 mm height error moves the mark by about 0.36 mm. The corners are \(\sqrt{2}\) times farther from the axis, and the landing slope grows in proportion to that distance, so the shift there is \(\sqrt{2}\) times larger: \(\sqrt{2}\tan 20^\circ = 0.51\) mm per millimeter of height error. That slope is a landing angle of 27.2°, a little less than the 28.3° corner field angle (\(\sqrt{2}\,\theta\)) in the results, because the f-theta mapping scales the angle itself with distance from the axis, while the landing geometry scales its tangent.

Telecentric f-theta lenses keep the chief ray nearly perpendicular to the work surface across the field. This removes most of the height-dependent position error and makes hole walls and scribe edges consistent from center to edge. The cost: the last lens element must be larger than the scan field, so these lenses are larger and more expensive, and they are usually limited to smaller fields.

Worked example

The default inputs: a 163 mm f-theta lens with galvos rotating ±10° mechanically (±20° optical), a 10 mm beam at 1064 nm, and M² = 1.1.

  • Field: \(2 \times 163 \times 0.349 = 113.8\) mm square (160.9 mm diagonal). A plain \(f\tan\theta\) lens would put the edge spot 2.43 mm too far out.
  • Spot: \(4 \times 1.1 \times 1.064\,\mu\text{m} \times 163 / (\pi \times 10) = 24.3\) µm. \(z_R = 0.40\) mm, so the 5 % depth of focus is only 0.25 mm in total (±0.13 mm about best focus).
  • A 16-bit galvo addresses 1.74 µm steps on the work surface (10.7 µrad optical).

Assumptions and limits

  • Ideal f-theta mapping. Real lenses deviate slightly from \(y = f\theta\) (the datasheet’s f-theta distortion: 0.1–1.3 % for the lenses in the table). Scanner software corrects the residual, and the field distortion that comes from the two mirrors turning about separate axes, with a field-correction (calibration) table.
  • Scan angle. F-theta lenses typically accept up to about ±20° optical per axis, about ±28° at the corners (see the table). Above 30° the tool shows a warning: such values usually mean an optical angle was marked as mechanical, or a full angle was entered as the half-angle.
  • Spot size at field center, untruncated. Aberrations and field curvature usually enlarge the spot toward the edge. Vendors specify the spot size and telecentricity across the field. With a non-telecentric lens the beam also lands obliquely, so on a flat part the spot is stretched by about \(1/\cos\theta\) in the radial direction (6 % at 20°, more at the corners). Datasheet spot sizes can include clipping: the 100 mm lens in the table is quoted at 16 µm for a 12 mm beam at 1064 nm. That matches \(1.83\,\lambda f/D\), the vendor’s formula for a beam cut off at its 1/e² diameter, while this tool gives the unclipped \(4\lambda f/(\pi D) = 11.3\) µm. The focused spot tool computes the clipped case.
  • Working distance is not \(f\). The back focal distance from the last lens surface depends on the lens design. Use the lens datasheet.
  • Digital resolution is not accuracy. Galvo drift, servo noise, thermal effects, and calibration limit absolute accuracy, which is usually much coarser than the command resolution, especially for 18–20-bit controllers. One compact 16-bit scan head, for example, lists an 11 µrad step and 2 µrad repeatability, but up to 100 µrad of offset drift over 8 hours and gain and offset errors of up to 5 mrad before calibration. With a 163 mm lens those are 16 µm and 0.8 mm on the work surface.
