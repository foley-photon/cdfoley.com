Diffraction Grating & Spectrometer Calculator

Where does a diffraction grating send each wavelength, and how finely can a spectrometer built around it separate wavelengths? Enter the grating’s groove density, the wavelength, and the geometry (a fixed angle of incidence, a Littrow mount, or a Czerny–Turner spectrometer) to get the diffraction angle, the dispersion at the detector, the bandpass for your slits, and the resolving power. Use it to choose a grating, to check that a wavelength reaches the detector, or to estimate a spectrometer’s resolution.

Key terms
Groove density
The number of grooves (lines) per millimeter, printed on the grating or its data sheet, for example 600, 1200, or 2400 lines/mm. More grooves spread the spectrum wider but reach a shorter longest wavelength. The groove spacing d is its inverse: 833 nm at 1200 lines/mm.
Diffraction order
A grating sends each wavelength into several directions, numbered m = 0, ±1, ±2, and so on. Order 0 is a plain mirror reflection with no separation of wavelengths; first order is the usual working order. Higher orders spread the spectrum more, but their spectra overlap.
Angles of incidence and diffraction
α and β, measured from the grating normal, the line perpendicular to the grating surface. An angle on the same side of the normal as the incoming light counts as positive, as in Figure 1 below.
Littrow and Czerny–Turner
Two common layouts. In Littrow, the grating sends the chosen wavelength straight back toward the source, as in tunable lasers. In a Czerny–Turner spectrometer, two concave mirrors bring the light to the grating and focus it on the exit slit or detector; the grating turns to select the wavelength while the angle between the beams, the deviation angle, stays fixed.
Dispersion
How far apart two wavelengths end up. The reciprocal linear dispersion, in nm/mm, is how many nanometers fall on each millimeter of the exit slit or detector; the smaller it is, the finer the detail.
Bandpass
The range of wavelengths that passes the exit slit at once, as a full width at half maximum. It is set by the slit widths and the dispersion and usually limits a spectrometer’s resolution.
Resolving power
λ/Δλ for the finest detail the grating itself could separate: the diffraction order times the number of illuminated grooves.
Free spectral range
Light at 500 nm in first order leaves the grating in the same direction as 250 nm in second order. The free spectral range is the band free of such overlap; order-sorting filters block the light that would overlap.

Inputs

Fixed incidence: the light meets the grating at a set angle, as in a spectrograph or a bench setup. Littrow: the grating sends the wavelength back toward the source, as in a tunable laser. Constant deviation: the grating turns between fixed entrance and exit arms, as in most scanning monochromators and Czerny–Turner spectrometers.

Diffraction angle vs. wavelength

How it is calculated

For a plane reflection grating with groove spacing \(d\) (the inverse of the groove density), light incident at angle \(\alpha\) is diffracted into angles \(\beta\) that satisfy the grating equation

\[ m\lambda = d\,(\sin\alpha + \sin\beta) . \]

Sign convention. Both angles are measured from the grating normal, and two angles have the same sign when they lie on the same side of it (Palmer’s convention). With the incoming side taken as positive, the zero order is the mirror reflection, \(\beta = -\alpha\); positive orders lie on the incoming side of it and negative orders on the other side (Figure 1). An order exists only if \(|\sin\beta| \le 1\); otherwise it is evanescent and carries no power away from the grating. Other books use other sign conventions, so check them before comparing angles between sources.

Here \(\lambda\) is the wavelength in the medium around the grating, normally air, as on spectrometer scales and in HORIBA’s tutorial. The vacuum wavelength is about 0.03 % longer; at the default settings it would move \(\beta\) by about 0.01°.

Mountings

  • Littrow: the light returns along its incoming path, \(\alpha = \beta\), so \(\sin\alpha = m\lambda/2d\). Tunable lasers use it, and echelle gratings work close to it.
  • Constant deviation (Czerny–Turner): the angle between the incident and diffracted beams, \(D_V = \alpha - \beta\), is fixed by the instrument, and the grating rotates to scan (Figure 2). With scan angle \(\phi = (\alpha+\beta)/2\), the equation becomes \(m\lambda = 2d\cos(D_V/2)\sin\phi\), so \(\alpha = \phi + D_V/2\) and \(\beta = \phi - D_V/2\) (Palmer writes \(2K\) for \(D_V\)). Which beam makes the larger angle with the normal depends on which way the grating turns. Enter \(D_V\) as negative when the diffracted beam does (\(\beta > \alpha\)); that is the orientation of HORIBA’s tables, which define \(D_V = \beta - \alpha\). The orientation changes the dispersion, which follows \(\cos\beta\): at 500 nm with 1200 lines/mm and a 300 mm focal length, \(D_V = +30^\circ\) gives 2.77 nm/mm and \(D_V = -30^\circ\) gives 2.33 nm/mm.

Dispersion, resolution, and bandpass

\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{d\beta}{d\lambda} = \frac{m}{d\cos\beta}, \\[4pt] \frac{d\lambda}{dx} = \frac{d\cos\beta}{|m|\,L_B}, \\[4pt] R = \frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = |m|N = \frac{W\,|\sin\alpha + \sin\beta|}{\lambda} \le \frac{2W}{\lambda} . \end{gathered} \]

The reciprocal linear dispersion \(d\lambda/dx\) (nm per mm at the focal plane) turns a width at the exit into a band of wavelengths. The grating also changes the width of the entrance-slit image: it is magnified by \(\cos\alpha/\cos\beta\), times \(L_B/L_A\) when the collimating focal length \(L_A\) differs from \(L_B\). The bandpass is set by the wider of that image and the exit slit:

\[ B \approx \frac{d\lambda}{dx}\,\max\!\left(w_\text{exit},\ w\,\frac{\cos\alpha}{\cos\beta}\,\frac{L_B}{L_A}\right) . \]

The calculator takes \(L_A = L_B\) and equal slits, \(w_\text{exit} = w\), so \(B = w\,d\max(\cos\alpha,\cos\beta)/(|m|L_B)\): both orientations of a constant-deviation mount give the same bandpass, although their dispersions differ. With a detector array instead of an exit slit, \(B\) is about the slit-image width times \(d\lambda/dx\). The resolving power \(R = |m|N\) is the theoretical limit set by the number of illuminated grooves (Rayleigh criterion), and no geometry can exceed \(2W/\lambda\); practical spectrometers are usually slit- or pixel-limited well before reaching it. The results give the bandpass and the diffraction-limited width \(\lambda/R\) in wavelength, frequency, and wavenumber, with \(\Delta\nu = c\,\Delta\lambda/\lambda^2\) and \(\Delta\tilde\nu = \Delta\lambda/\lambda^2\) (about 0.03 % high when \(\lambda\) is an air wavelength). The free spectral range \(\lambda/|m|\) is the band before the next order overlaps, so a first-order spectrum spanning more than a factor of two in wavelength needs order-sorting filters, unless the overlapping light is absorbed, as air absorbs it below about 190 nm.

Worked example

Take a 1200 lines/mm grating (\(d = 833.3\) nm) at \(\alpha = 30^\circ\), first order, 500 nm. Then \(\sin\beta = 0.6 - 0.5 = 0.1\), so \(\beta = 5.74^\circ\). The angular dispersion is 1.206 mrad/nm, and with a 300 mm focusing mirror the reciprocal linear dispersion is 2.76 nm/mm. The grating shrinks the image of a 50 µm entrance slit to 43.5 µm (\(\cos 30^\circ/\cos 5.74^\circ = 0.870\)), so with 50 µm slits the exit slit sets the bandpass: about 0.14 nm (5.5 cm⁻¹). Fifty millimeters of illuminated grating gives \(R = 60\,000\), or 8.3 pm (10 GHz) at 500 nm, so the slits, not the grating, limit the resolution. In a Czerny–Turner with \(D_V = 30^\circ\), the same wavelength needs \(\alpha = 33.09^\circ\) and \(\beta = 3.09^\circ\). With \(D_V = -30^\circ\) the two angles swap: the reciprocal linear dispersion changes from 2.77 to 2.33 nm/mm, while the bandpass stays 0.139 nm.

Assumptions and limits

  • Plane grating in the principal plane. No conical (off-plane) diffraction. Concave and holographic aberration-corrected gratings follow the same equation at their center, but their focal-plane properties differ.
  • Geometric bandpass. The bandpass counts only the slit widths and the slit image. Aberrations and diffraction at the optics widen it further, and the diffracted wavefront must be good to about λ/10 for the grating to reach its theoretical resolving power (Palmer). The calculator assumes equal collimating and focusing focal lengths; otherwise scale the slit image by \(L_B/L_A\).
  • Detector normal to the diffracted beam. \(d\lambda/dx\) is for a focal plane perpendicular to the central diffracted ray. A detector tilted by \(\gamma\) from that plane spreads the spectrum by \(1/\cos\gamma\), so \(d\lambda/dx = d\cos\beta\cos\gamma/(|m|\,L_B)\).
  • Efficiency is not modeled. Which orders are bright depends on blaze angle, groove profile, polarization, and coating; use the manufacturer’s efficiency curves. As a rule of thumb, a blazed grating is most efficient where its groove facets reflect the incident beam into the diffracted one, \(\alpha + \beta = 2\theta_B\). Catalogs quote the blaze wavelength for first-order Littrow, \(\lambda_B = 2d\sin\theta_B\), so a grating blazed at the Littrow angle in the results peaks near \(\lambda\) when used in Littrow. In a constant-deviation mount the peak moves to \(\lambda_B\cos(D_V/2)\), and efficiency generally falls the farther a mount is from Littrow. First-order efficiency drops to about half its peak at roughly two thirds of the blaze wavelength and at 1.5 to 1.8 times it (rules of thumb from Palmer and HORIBA).
  • Anomalies. Wherever an order starts or stops propagating, at the wavelength where its beam would graze the grating, the power in the other orders changes abruptly (a Rayleigh anomaly). Efficiency curves can also show sharp resonance anomalies. Both show up mainly for light polarized perpendicular to the grooves.
