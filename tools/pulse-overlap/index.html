Laser Pulse & Hatch Overlap Calculator

When a pulsed laser is scanned across a part, for marking, scribing, cleaning, or milling, how far apart do the pulses land, how much do neighboring spots overlap, and how much light does each point receive? Enter the spot size, scan speed, repetition rate, and line spacing to find out, or set a target overlap to get the scan speed you need.

Key terms
Spot diameter (1/e²)
The size of the focused laser spot on the part, measured where the fluence has fallen to 13.5 % of its central value. The default is 30 µm.
Scan speed
How fast the beam moves across the part, set in the marking software; also called marking speed. Galvanometer scanners with a 100 mm lens reach about 10 m/s.
Repetition rate
How many pulses the laser fires each second. Together with the scan speed it sets how far apart the pulses land.
Pulse pitch
The distance between the centers of two consecutive pulses: the scan speed divided by the repetition rate. 1 m/s at 100 kHz gives 10 µm.
Pulse overlap
How much of a spot’s diameter the next spot covers again. At 0 % the spots just touch; a negative value means gaps between them.
Hatch spacing
The distance between neighboring scan lines when an area is filled with parallel lines; also called line pitch.
Pulses per spot
How many pulses land within one spot diameter along the line. Many papers call this the effective pulse number.
Fluence-equivalent pulse number
All the energy a point receives from the overlapping pulses, counted as a number of full-strength pulses at the beam center. It is smaller than the pulses per spot because the edge of a Gaussian spot is weak.

Inputs

The 1/e² diameter at the work surface: from a beam profiler, the focused-spot calculator, or a crater-size fit in the ablation tool.

The marking speed set in the scanner software.

Pulses per second, set on the laser or in the marking software (also called pulse frequency or PRF).

The spacing between neighboring lines when filling an area. For a single line, leave any value: only the area results use it.

%

Both optional. The target overlap gives the scan speed required. The area gives the hatch-fill time, excluding jumps and turnarounds.

How it is calculated

A pulsed laser moving at speed \(v\) and repetition rate \(f_\text{rep}\) places consecutive pulses a pitch \(\Delta x\) apart. Adjacent scan lines sit a hatch \(h\) apart:

\[ \Delta x = \frac{v}{f_\text{rep}}, \qquad \text{OL}_\text{pulse} = 1 - \frac{\Delta x}{d}, \qquad \text{OL}_\text{line} = 1 - \frac{h}{d}, \qquad N_\text{spot} = \frac{d}{\Delta x}. \]

Overlap is defined against the 1/e² diameter \(d = 2w_0\), as \((2w_0 - \Delta x)/2w_0\) (Račiukaitis et al., 2009). Negative overlap means there are gaps between spots. The scan speed for a target overlap is \(v = d\,f_\text{rep}\,(1 - \text{OL})\).

Fluence-equivalent pulse number for a Gaussian beam

“Pulses per spot” counts circles, but a Gaussian beam delivers most of its fluence near the center. Summing the Gaussian fluence of every pulse that passes a point on the scan line gives the accumulated dose in units of the peak fluence \(F_0\):

\[ \sum_n e^{-2(x - n\Delta x)^2/w_0^2} \;\approx\; \frac{1}{\Delta x}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-2x^2/w_0^2}\,dx \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\,\frac{w_0}{\Delta x} \equiv N_\text{eff,1D}. \]

For a hatched area the same integral runs in two dimensions: \(N_\text{eff,2D} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}\dfrac{w_0^2}{\Delta x\,h}\). Then \(N_\text{eff,2D}F_0 = E_p/(\Delta x\,h)\) exactly: the accumulated fluence is the pulse energy per unit area of the pattern. Summing the discrete pulses numerically confirms both expressions. When \(\Delta x \le w_0\), the point-to-point ripple in the line sum stays within about ±1.5 %. With \(h \le w_0\) as well, the area sum stays within about ±3 %. At larger pitch or hatch the dose varies from point to point and \(N_\text{eff}\) is only an average.

Papers count pulses per point in different ways. Many call the number of pulses within the 1/e² diameter, \(d/\Delta x\), the effective pulse number (Skoulas et al., 2017); the fluence-equivalent \(N_\text{eff,1D}\) here is \(\sqrt{\pi/2}/2 \approx 0.63\) times that count. An incubation law such as \(F_\text{th}(N) = F_\text{th}(1)N^{S-1}\) is fitted with one particular count, so use the same definition as the source of your \(S\). The accumulated fluence \(N_\text{eff}F_0\) is a useful dose metric for comparing scan strategies.

Worked example

A 30 µm spot scanned at 1 m/s with 100 kHz pulses and a 15 µm hatch:

\[ \Delta x = \frac{1000\ \text{mm/s}}{100\ \text{kHz}} = 10\ \mu\text{m}, \quad \text{OL} = 1 - \tfrac{10}{30} = 66.7\,\%, \quad N_\text{eff,1D} = 1.2533\times\tfrac{15}{10} = 1.88. \]

That is 3 pulses per spot diameter. The line overlap is 50 %, so \(N_\text{eff,2D} = 2.36\). The coverage rate is 15 mm²/s, which fills 1 cm² in 6.7 s before jump and acceleration overhead. Reaching 90 % pulse overlap at the same repetition rate means slowing to 300 mm/s.

Choosing overlap in practice

  • Craters must touch. Separate craters merge into a line only when the pitch is smaller than the ablated diameter \(D_\text{th} = w_0\sqrt{2\ln(F_0/F_\text{th})}\) (see the fluence tool). At \(F_0 = 2F_\text{th}\) that takes more than 41 % overlap; at \(F_0 = e^2F_\text{th}\) any positive overlap will do.
  • Continuous scribes and milled surfaces commonly use about 50–90 % overlap, chosen by experiment for edge quality and a smooth floor. Surface structuring with picosecond pulses gave the lowest roughness at 50–75 % (Neuenschwander et al., 2014), and trenches in alumina had a flat floor at about 83–89 % by this page’s definition (Furmanski et al., 2007). The edge ripple period equals Δx.
  • Very high overlap (> 90–95 %) raises the pulse count per point and, at high repetition rates, causes heat accumulation: below a critical scan speed, ablated metal turns from smooth and reflective to rough and dark (Bauer et al., 2015; Weber et al., 2014). Check the thermal diffusion tool.
  • Fast scanners spread the heat. Galvanometer scanners with a 100 mm lens reach about 10 m/s; polygon scanners reach about 100 m/s, so a laser firing millions of pulses per second can keep a moderate overlap and build depth in many fast passes (Neuenschwander et al., 2014).
  • Galvo acceleration raises the effective overlap at line ends unless the controller accelerates outside the marked region with the laser off (“skywriting”) or fires pulses by position rather than by time.

Assumptions and limits

  • Constant scan speed and repetition rate, straight parallel lines, and one pulse per trigger (no burst mode).
  • Overlap is geometric, measured against the 1/e² diameter. Some groups define it against the ablated crater diameter instead, which depends on fluence, and Furmanski et al. use the relative fluence of the next pulse at the previous pulse’s center. Check the definition before copying an overlap from a paper.
  • \(N_\text{eff}\) assumes a TEM₀₀ Gaussian focal spot. For a top-hat beam, each point on the center of the scan line receives the full fluence from \(d/\Delta x\) pulses, so pulses per spot is already the effective count there.
