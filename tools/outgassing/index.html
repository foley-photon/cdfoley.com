Outgassing Calculator & NASA Outgassing Database Search

Two tools for choosing what goes inside a vacuum system. The first searches all 13,582 results in NASA’s outgassing database, the standard screening test for spacecraft materials (ASTM E595), and marks the ones within the usual limits of 1.0 % total mass loss and 0.10 % condensable material. The second estimates how much gas the chamber walls, seals, and parts give off per second, how that falls with pumping time, and what pressure it holds the chamber at. The two answer different questions: a material can pass the NASA screen and still be the largest gas source in a high-vacuum chamber.

Key terms
Outgassing
The slow release of gas from materials in vacuum: mostly water vapor at first, then solvents, uncured resin fractions, and plasticizers from polymers, and hydrogen from metals once the water is gone.
ASTM E595
A screening test: a small sample is held at 125 °C in vacuum for 24 h, and the mass it loses and the mass that condenses on a nearby plate at 25 °C are weighed. It ranks materials; it does not measure how fast they outgas at room temperature.
TML (total mass loss)
The mass the sample lost in the E595 test, as a percentage of its starting mass. Water counts toward it.
CVCM (collected volatile condensable materials)
The mass that condensed on the 25 °C collector, as a percentage of the sample’s starting mass. It stands for the material that can deposit as a film on optics, detectors, and other cool surfaces.
WVR and RML
Water vapor regained: the mass the sample takes back up in 24 h of humid air after the test. TML minus WVR, the mass lost other than water, is called the recovered mass loss (RML) and is what the European space standard screens on.
Specific outgassing rate
Gas released per second per unit area of surface, in mbar·L/(s·cm²) or Pa·m/s. It depends strongly on the pumping time, so a value means little without it.
Gas load
The total gas entering the chamber per second (mbar·L/s): the sum over every surface of its outgassing rate times its area, plus any leaks and process gas.
Base pressure
The pressure at which the pump removes gas as fast as the gas load brings it in. While outgassing falls with time, so does the base pressure.

Test results

Loading the NASA table…

TML, CVCM, and WVR in percent of the starting mass; red values exceed your limit. Cure: the cure or post-cure steps before the test, in order, as NASA lists them (a vacuum written as 1.5×10^? lost its exponent in NASA’s own data; in many 2019 and later entries the first step reads 24 h at 125 °C in vacuum, the same exposure as the E595 test itself).

What ASTM E595 tells you, and what it does not. It is a 24 h test at 125 °C that compares how much volatile material different products hold, and how much of it can recondense on a 25 °C surface. It does not give an outgassing rate at room temperature, how that rate falls with pumping time, which gases come off, or anything that condenses only below 25 °C. Water, the main gas load in an unbaked system, is counted in TML and returns from humid air. Most Viton tests in the database pass the NASA limits, yet after an hour of pumping, unbaked Viton outgasses more than 300 times as much per square centimeter as cleaned stainless steel. Use E595 data to rule materials out for contamination, and outgassing rates (Part 2) to size the vacuum.

Part 2

Gas load and base pressure from outgassing rates

List the surfaces inside the chamber, pick a typical rate for each or enter a measured one, and set the pumping speed the chamber actually gets. The rates fall as a power of the pumping time, so the pressure keeps creeping down for days.

Inputs

Surfaces inside the chamber

Up to 8 surfaces. Set one to “Not used” to leave it out.

Rates are in mbar·L/(s·cm²) after 1 h of pumping at room temperature; the exponent sets how fast they fall: q(t) = q₁·(t/1 h)−n. Cleaned metals fall about as 1/t (n = 1), polymers about as 1/√t (n ≈ 0.5), and a baked surface releasing hydrogen hardly at all (n = 0). Count only the area that faces the vacuum: for an O-ring, the strip between the flanges.

Pumping

Time since the pump-down from atmosphere began. The model holds from a few minutes on, once the air in the volume is gone; before that, use the pump-down calculator.

The high vacuum pump’s speed for water vapor, reduced by the port, valve, and any line in front of it. The pump-down calculator gives it from the pump’s speed and the line.

The pump’s own ultimate pressure adds to the result. With a target, the calculator finds the pumping time to reach it and the speed that would hold it at the time above.

Pressure vs. pumping time

The total pressure and each surface’s share of it (its gas load divided by the pumping speed), on log scales. A straight line falls as a power of time, steeper for a larger exponent, so the metal walls fade while the polymers persist.

How it is calculated

Database search. The page loads NASA’s table once (13,582 rows, 1.4 MB, about 360 kB as sent compressed) after the rest of the page has loaded, and filters it in the browser. A test is within the limits when

\[ \text{TML} \le \text{TML}_\text{max} \quad\text{and}\quad \text{CVCM} \le \text{CVCM}_\text{max}, \]

both as published (to 0.01 %). On the ESA basis, the mass loss is \(\text{RML} = \text{TML} - \text{WVR}\), and both comparisons are strict (<), as ECSS-Q-ST-70-02C writes them. A test missing TML or CVCM is shown as incomplete and never counted as passing.

Gas load. Each surface \(i\) of area \(A_i\) outgasses at a rate that falls as a power of the pumping time \(t\), the empirical law found for nearly all unbaked materials:

\[ q_i(t) = q_{1,i}\left(\frac{t}{1\ \text{h}}\right)^{-n_i}, \qquad Q(t) = \sum_i q_i(t)\,A_i . \]

For a rate \(q_\text{ref}\) quoted at another time \(t_\text{ref}\), the rate after 1 h on the same law is \(q_1 = q_\text{ref}\,(t_\text{ref}/1\ \text{h})^{n}\); several presets below were converted this way. A constant hydrogen rate from baked metal is \(n = 0\).

Pressure. Hours into a pump-down, the air in the volume is long gone (it is pumped with the time constant \(V/S_\text{eff}\), a fraction of a second for a small chamber on a turbo pump), so the pressure follows the gas load, as in the pump-down equation with \(dp/dt\) negligible:

\[ p(t) = p_\text{pump} + \frac{Q(t)}{S_\text{eff}} . \]

Every term of \(Q(t)\) falls or stays constant, so the time to reach a target pressure is the single root of \(Q(t) = S_\text{eff}\,(p_\text{target} - p_\text{pump})\), found by bisection in \(\ln t\). The speed that holds the target at time \(t\) is \(Q(t)/(p_\text{target} - p_\text{pump})\). Units: 1 mbar·L/(s·cm²) = 1000 Pa·m/s, and 1 Torr·L/(s·cm²) = 1333.2 Pa·m/s.

The cleaned-metal rule and the literature averages differ by a factor of about 100 for stainless steel. The averages are for surfaces with no stated treatment and their scatter is as large as the values (Leybold’s standard value for stainless steel at 1 h is 9 × 10⁻⁸), so use the CERN rule for parts cleaned for vacuum and the averages as a pessimistic case. Thin films such as Kapton deplete their water within hours: after the bend, the rate falls much faster than 1/√t (Baglin gives 5 × 10⁻¹⁰ after 100 h for 0.1 mm), so the preset overestimates it at long times. ISO/TS 20177:2018 sets out how outgassing rates should be measured and reported; quote the pumping time with any rate you measure.

Worked example

The defaults: a chamber with 5400 cm² of cleaned, unbaked stainless steel walls (a 30 cm cube), 20 cm² of Viton O-ring surface facing the vacuum, and 50 cm² of PEEK insulators, pumped at an effective 200 L/s.

  • After 10 h the steel gives 3 × 10⁻⁹/10 × 5400 = 1.62 × 10⁻⁶ mbar·L/s, the Viton 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ × 10−0.7 × 20 = 4.39 × 10⁻⁶, and the PEEK 4.7 × 10⁻⁷ × 10−0.5 × 50 = 7.43 × 10⁻⁶ mbar·L/s.
  • The total, Q = 1.34 × 10⁻⁵ mbar·L/s, holds the chamber at 1.34 × 10⁻⁵/200 = 6.7 × 10⁻⁸ mbar. The 70 cm² of polymer, 1.3 % of the area, supplies 88 % of the gas.
  • To reach 10⁻⁸ mbar takes 253 h, about 10.5 days. The steel alone would get there in 8.1 h: 3 × 10⁻⁹ × 5400/(200 × 10⁻⁸) = 8.1. Holding 10⁻⁸ mbar at 10 h would take 1340 L/s, a much larger pump; replacing the PEEK with metal parts and baking the seals is cheaper.
  • In Part 1, a search for PEEK finds 25 tests, 24 within the NASA limits. PEEK is a sound low-contamination choice, and still the largest gas source here. The two criteria measure different things.

Assumptions and limits

  • Power-law rates. \(q \propto t^{-n}\) is an empirical fit that holds over a few decades of time. Water from metals follows 1/t from minutes to weeks; diffusion from polymers follows 1/√t until the part is depleted, after which it falls faster (thin films within hours). The calculator does not model that bend, so at long times it overestimates thin polymer parts.
  • Room temperature. Outgassing rises steeply with temperature; a heated stage or a lamp near a polymer part changes everything. Bake-out lowers the later rates by orders of magnitude, and the baked presets assume it has been done.
  • Each surface independent. Readsorption on the other walls, permeation of air and water through elastomer seals (about 10⁻⁷ mbar·L/s of water through a 6 cm Viton O-ring with a 5 mm cord, once it reaches steady state after about two months), leaks, and virtual leaks (trapped volumes behind screws and welds) are not included.
  • One pumping speed. Pumps have different speeds for different gases, and a turbo pump’s speed and compression are lowest for hydrogen. Use the speed for the gas that dominates, usually water.
  • E595 numbers are not rates. TML and CVCM come from a 125 °C test and cannot be converted into a room-temperature rate. NASA’s guide notes that formulations change and recommends testing each lot for critical uses. Manufacturer codes are shown as NASA lists them; the current NASA site does not publish the code list.
