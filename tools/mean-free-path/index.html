Mean Free Path & Knudsen Number Calculator

How far a molecule travels between collisions, and what that means for flow in your chamber. The calculator also gives the number density, mean speed, how often molecules hit a surface, and how long a clean surface stays clean.

Key terms
Mean free path
The average distance a gas molecule travels before it hits another molecule. In nitrogen at room temperature it is about 65 µm at 1 mbar and 65 mm at 0.001 mbar: ten times lower pressure, ten times longer path.
Knudsen number
The mean free path divided by the size of the pipe, gap, or chamber the gas is in (D here). It tells you which kind of flow you have.
Viscous and molecular flow
In viscous flow (Knudsen number below 0.01) molecules mostly hit each other and the gas flows like a fluid, as in a roughing line near atmosphere. In molecular flow (above 0.5) they mostly hit the walls and move independently, as in a high vacuum chamber. In between, the flow is transitional.
Collision diameter
The effective size of a molecule in a collision, treating it as a hard ball: about 0.37 nm for nitrogen. The values here are worked out from each gas’s viscosity.
Number density
How many molecules are in each cubic centimeter: about 2.5 × 10¹⁹ at atmospheric pressure and room temperature, and still about 25 billion at 10⁻⁶ mbar.
Impingement rate
How many molecules strike each square centimeter of a surface every second: about 3 × 10¹⁴ for nitrogen at 10⁻⁶ mbar and room temperature.
Monolayer time
How long a freshly cleaned surface takes to be covered by one layer of molecules if every molecule that hits it sticks: about 3 ms at 10⁻³ mbar, 3 s at 10⁻⁶ mbar, and 10 hours at 10⁻¹⁰ mbar.
Sticking coefficient
The fraction of the molecules hitting a surface that stay on it, from 0 to 1. Nitrogen, hydrogen, and the noble gases hardly stick to room-temperature metal; water, carbon monoxide, and carbon dioxide stick far more readily.

Mean free path vs. pressure

How it is calculated

Treating molecules as hard spheres of diameter \(d\) in an ideal gas at temperature \(T\), the mean free path is

\[ \begin{gathered} \lambda = \frac{k_B T}{\sqrt{2}\,\pi d^2\, p} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\, n\, \pi d^2}, \\[8pt] n = \frac{p}{k_B T}. \end{gathered} \]

The \(\sqrt2\) accounts for the motion of the collision partners: in a Maxwell–Boltzmann gas the mean speed of two molecules relative to each other is \(\sqrt2\) times the mean speed. At a fixed temperature the product \(\lambda p\) is constant. For N₂ at 20 °C it is 6.48 mm·Pa, or about 6.5 × 10⁻³ cm·mbar. So \(\lambda \approx 6.5\) mm at 1 Pa, and it scales as \(1/p\): about 6.5 cm at 10⁻³ mbar and 65 m at 10⁻⁶ mbar.

The other kinetic quantities follow from the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, with molecular mass \(m = M/N_A\):

\[ \begin{gathered} \bar v = \sqrt{\frac{8 k_B T}{\pi m}}, \qquad z = \frac{\bar v}{\lambda}, \\[8pt] \Phi = \frac{n \bar v}{4} = \frac{p}{\sqrt{2\pi m k_B T}}, \\[8pt] \tau_\text{mono} = \frac{n_s}{s\,\Phi}. \end{gathered} \]

Here \(z\) is how often one molecule collides with others, \(\Phi\) is the number of molecules striking a unit area of wall per second, \(n_s \approx 10^{15}\) cm⁻² is the number of adsorption sites in a monolayer, and \(s\) is the sticking coefficient. The Boltzmann constant \(k_B\) and the Avogadro constant \(N_A\) are exact in the SI.

Knudsen number and flow regime

The Knudsen number \(\mathrm{Kn} = \lambda/D\) compares the mean free path with the size of the system. It decides whether gas behaves as a fluid or as independent molecules bouncing between walls:

KnRegimeWhat it means
< 0.01Viscous (continuum)Collisions between molecules dominate. Conductance depends on pressure and viscosity.
0.01–0.5Transitional (Knudsen)Gas–gas and gas–wall collisions both matter.
> 0.5MolecularMolecules mostly hit walls. Conductance is independent of pressure.

These boundaries are common in vacuum-industry literature. Some texts put the start of molecular flow at Kn = 1. The change between regimes is gradual, not sharp. The chart above shades the molecular and viscous regimes for the D you enter.

The diameters are Kennard’s equivalent hard-sphere diameters (1938, table on p. 149). He calculated them from the viscosity \(\eta\) of each gas at 15 °C, using Chapman and Enskog’s result for hard spheres, \(\eta = 0.499\,\rho \bar v \lambda\) with gas density \(\rho = nm\), which gives

\[ d = \sqrt{\frac{0.499\, m \bar v}{\sqrt2\,\pi\,\eta}}. \]

The calculator uses them because most vacuum texts tabulate these values, so its λ matches the literature, and because they still match current data: recomputed from NIST viscosities at 15 °C, eight of the nine agree within 0.5 %, and water vapor within 0.8 %. The same formula gives a diameter for a gas that is not listed: for N₂, \(\eta = 17.33\ \mu\text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}\) at 15 °C gives \(d = 3.75\) Å.

Do not enter a Lennard-Jones σ instead (Svehla’s table, for example, gives N₂ 3.80 Å, He 2.55 Å, and H₂O 2.64 Å). It is a parameter of a soft intermolecular potential and gives the viscosity only together with temperature-dependent collision integrals; in the hard-sphere formula it puts λ off by up to a factor of three (for water vapor). Water vapor is also the least certain entry in the table: Kennard derived it from a measurement on saturated vapor, and because H₂O is strongly polar its effective diameter changes fastest with temperature.

Worked example

N₂ at 10⁻³ mbar (0.1 Pa) and 20 °C, in a 100 mm diameter tube (the default inputs):

  • \(\lambda = 6.48\ \text{mm}\cdot\text{Pa} / 0.1\ \text{Pa} = 64.8\ \text{mm}\), so Kn = 0.65 and the flow is molecular. In this tube it turns viscous above about 0.065 mbar (Kn = 0.01).
  • \(n = 0.1/(1.381\times10^{-23}\times293.15) = 2.47\times10^{19}\ \text{m}^{-3} = 2.47\times10^{13}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\). Even at “high vacuum” there are tens of trillions of molecules in every cubic centimeter.
  • \(\bar v = 471\) m/s, so each molecule collides with another about \(\bar v/\lambda \approx 7300\) times per second, and the wall is hit at \(\Phi = 2.9\times10^{17}\) cm⁻² s⁻¹. With \(s = 1\), a clean surface is covered in about 3.4 ms. At 10⁻⁶ mbar the same estimate gives 3.4 s, which is why surface-science work needs ultra-high vacuum.

Assumptions and limits

  • Ideal, dilute gas with hard-sphere collisions and a single species. The calculator flags results where λ is under 10 molecular diameters (above about 17 atm for N₂ at 20 °C). Mixtures need a collision diameter and reduced mass for each pair of species; dry air is treated as one gas with an effective diameter, as in Kennard’s table.
  • Temperature. The diameters belong to 15 °C. Real molecules also attract each other, which deflects slow molecules more than fast ones, so the effective cross-section shrinks as the temperature rises; a fixed diameter misses this (Bird, 1983). With NIST viscosity data at the actual temperature, Kennard’s formula gives a mean free path of N₂ that is 40 % shorter than the fixed-diameter value at 77 K, 11 % shorter at 200 K, 9 % longer at 400 K, and 29 % longer at 1000 K; at 20 °C the two differ by 0.4 %. Gases with stronger attraction deviate more: at 400 K by 15 % for CO₂ and Xe and by 23 % for water vapor. For the listed gases, within the temperature range of the viscosity data, the calculator shows the viscosity-based value under the result whenever the two differ by more than 5 %. For a custom gas, or outside that range, it flags temperatures outside 200–400 K.
  • With \(s = 1\) the monolayer time is a lower bound: the fastest a clean surface can be covered. N₂, H₂, CH₄, and the noble gases have negligible sticking coefficients on room-temperature metal, so they take far longer; H₂O, CO, CO₂, O₂, and heavier hydrocarbons stick much more readily (Chiggiato). Sticking also falls as the surface fills: in Langmuir’s model only empty sites adsorb, so \(n_s/(s\Phi)\) is the time to \(1 - 1/e \approx 63\) % coverage, not a complete monolayer. The site density of 10¹⁵ cm⁻² is an order of magnitude.
