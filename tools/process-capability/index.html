Process Capability (Cpk) Calculator

This Cpk calculator answers a simple question: does the process make parts inside the specification, and with how much room to spare? Paste your measurements in the order they were made, or enter a mean and standard deviations, together with the specification limits. You get Cp, Cpk, Pp, Ppk, and Cpm, the expected and observed parts per million (PPM) out of specification, a normality check, and charts of your data against the limits.

Key terms
Specification limits
The lowest and highest acceptable values of a characteristic (LSL and USL), from the drawing or the customer’s specification. Some characteristics, such as flatness or burr height, have only an upper limit.
Target
The ideal value, usually the middle of the specification (the nominal).
Subgroup
A few parts, typically 4 or 5, made and measured close together in time under the same conditions. The variation inside a subgroup shows what the process does in the short term.
σ within and σ overall
Two estimates of the standard deviation σ, the typical spread of the values. σ within comes from the variation inside subgroups, or between consecutive values when parts are measured one at a time. σ overall is the ordinary standard deviation of all the values, so it also includes drifts and shifts during the study.
Cp and Cpk
Cp compares the width of the specification with the width of the process (6 σ within) and ignores where the process is centered. Cpk uses the distance from the mean to the nearer limit, so it drops when the process runs off center. A Cpk of 1.33 means the nearer limit is 4 σ from the mean.
Pp and Ppk
The same two ratios calculated with σ overall. They describe what the process actually delivered during the study.
PPM
Parts per million: out-of-specification parts per million parts made; 1000 PPM is 0.1 %. Expected PPM comes from a normal-distribution model, observed PPM from counting your data.
Stable process
A process in statistical control: its values vary randomly around a steady mean with a steady spread, without trends or jumps. A control chart shows whether it is. Capability numbers predict future output only for a stable process.

Normal probability plot

Each point is one measurement, placed by its rank on a scale on which a normal distribution is a straight line: the dashed line is the normal fit with σ overall. Points that bend away from it at the ends mean the tails are not normal, and the expected PPM is then unreliable. Where the line crosses LSL, the scale reads the expected percentage below it; at USL, 100 % minus the reading is the percentage above.

How the capability indices are calculated

Capability indices compare the width of the specification with the spread of the process, measured in standard deviations σ. Cp and Pp compare only the widths. Cpk and Ppk also account for where the process is centered, through the distance from the mean \(\bar x\) to the nearer limit (Kane, 1986):

\[ \begin{gathered} C_p = \frac{USL - LSL}{6\,\sigma_w} \\[6pt] C_{pk} = \min\!\left(\frac{USL - \bar{x}}{3\,\sigma_w},\ \frac{\bar{x} - LSL}{3\,\sigma_w}\right) \end{gathered} \]

Pp and Ppk use the same expressions with the overall standard deviation \(\sigma_o\) in place of \(\sigma_w\). With a one-sided specification, only CPU (or CPL) exists, and Cpk equals it; Cp and Pp are undefined. Because Cpk mixes spread and centering, the same Cpk can come from a centered process with a wide spread or an off-center process with a narrow one: compare it with Cp to tell them apart (Boyles, 1991).

The two standard deviations come from different estimators. Within subgroups of size \(n\), the calculator offers three:

\[ \sigma_w = \frac{\bar R}{d_2(n)} \quad\text{or}\quad \frac{\bar s}{c_4(n)} \quad\text{or}\quad \frac{s_p}{c_4(d+1)} \]

For individual values (\(n = 1\)) and for all the data:

\[ \sigma_w = \frac{\overline{MR}}{1.128}, \qquad \sigma_o = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{N-1}} \]

Here \(\bar R\) is the average subgroup range, \(\bar s\) the average subgroup standard deviation, \(s_p\) the pooled standard deviation of the \(k\) subgroups, with \(d = k(n-1)\) degrees of freedom, and \(\overline{MR}\) the average moving range of consecutive values. \(d_2\) and \(c_4\) correct the bias of the range and of the standard deviation for normal data: \(d_2(n)\) is the expected range of \(n\) values in units of σ (2.326 for \(n = 5\)), and \(c_4(m) = \sqrt{2/(m-1)}\,\Gamma(m/2)/\Gamma\big((m-1)/2\big)\) is the expected sample standard deviation of \(m\) values in units of σ (0.9400 for \(m = 5\)). The AIAG SPC manual estimates σ within with \(\bar R/d_2\) or \(\bar s/c_4\). Minitab’s capability analysis uses \(s_p/c_4(d+1)\) by default, so its Cpk can differ slightly from the default here; choose the same estimator to compare.

The Taguchi index \(C_{pm}\) also penalizes distance from the target \(T\), which must lie within the limits:

\[ C_{pm} = \frac{\min(USL - T,\ T - LSL)}{3\sqrt{\sigma_o^2 + (\bar x - T)^2}} \]

With the target at the midpoint of the limits, the numerator is \((USL - LSL)/2\), which gives the usual form of Chan, Cheng, and Spiring (1988) and Boyles (1991). With the target off the midpoint, the nearer limit counts. With a one-sided specification, only the given limit enters, as in the one-sided version of Chan, Cheng, and Spiring; Minitab uses the same rules. The root-mean-square distance from the target is estimated from \(\sigma_o\) and \(\bar x\), as in the NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook; Minitab uses \(\sqrt{\sum (x_i - T)^2/N}\), which differs by a term of order \(1/N\).

Expected nonconforming parts per million come from the normal model, \(\text{PPM}_{<LSL} = 10^6\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{LSL - \bar x}{\sigma}\right)\) and \(\text{PPM}_{>USL} = 10^6\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{\bar x - USL}{\sigma}\right)\). Z.bench, \(Z_\text{bench} = -\Phi^{-1}(p_\text{total})\), is the one-sided z-value that would give the same total fraction out of specification. The 95 % interval for Ppk uses Bissell’s (1990) approximation of its standard error, the same one Minitab and the NIST handbook use:

\[ \hat P_{pk} \pm 1.96\,\sqrt{\frac{1}{9N} + \frac{\hat P_{pk}^2}{2(N-1)}} \]

The normality check is the Anderson–Darling test with the mean and σ estimated from the data: its statistic \(A^2\) is multiplied by \(1 + 0.75/N + 2.25/N^2\), and the p-value comes from the approximation given by Stephens (1986). It needs at least 8 values. The probability plot places the value of rank \(i\) among \(N\), counted from the smallest, at the normal quantile of Filliben’s order-statistic median, \((i - 0.3175)/(N + 0.365)\), with \(0.5^{1/N}\) and \(1 - 0.5^{1/N}\) for the two extremes (NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook, §1.3.3.21).

Cpk versus Ppk

The AIAG SPC manual separates inherent variation, from common causes only and estimated within subgroups, from total variation, which also includes the drifts and shifts between subgroups and is estimated by the standard deviation of all the readings. Cpk uses the first: it shows what the process could do if the differences between subgroups were removed. Ppk uses the second: it describes what the customer actually received during the study.

Wheeler (2013) reads the four indices together. Cp is the best case: the process run predictably and on center. Ppk describes the process as it actually ran. For a stable, centered process, all four estimate nearly the same number. When Ppk is clearly below Cpk, the mean or the spread changed between subgroups (tool wear, lot changes, warm-up, set-up differences), and the gap shows what removing those causes is worth. That is a stability problem to fix before quoting capability. Wheeler also warns against calling Pp and Ppk “long-term capability”: an unstable process has no single long-term value to estimate.

Names differ between standards. This calculator follows the AIAG SPC manual (2nd ed., 2005), the AIAG PPAP manual, and Minitab. ISO 3534-2 and the ISO 22514 series tie the name to stability instead: a capability index describes a process shown to be in statistical control, and a performance index one that has not been shown to be. The new AIAG & VDA SPC manual (1st ed., 2026) is aligned with ISO 22514 and ISO 3534, according to VDA. In its public review draft, Cp/Cpk and Pp/Ppk are calculated the same way, from the overall variation, and the within-subgroup indices are renamed Cw and Cwk, for analysis rather than for reports.

Worked example

The default data are 100 measurements of a laser-scribe line width, in 20 subgroups of 5, with specification 50 ± 6 µm and target 50 µm. The mean is 50.93 µm. The average range is R̄ = 2.992 µm, so σw = 2.992/2.326 = 1.286 µm. The overall standard deviation is 1.293 µm.

\[ \begin{gathered} C_p = \frac{56 - 44}{6 \times 1.286} = 1.56 \\[6pt] C_{pk} = \frac{56 - 50.93}{3 \times 1.286} = 1.31 \end{gathered} \]

The spread alone would support Cpk ≈ 1.56, but the mean sits 0.93 µm above target, so Cpk = 1.31 is just short of 1.33. About 40 PPM is expected above the USL. Re-centering the process at 50.0 µm, for example with a laser-power or focus offset, would raise Cpk to 1.56 and cut the expected nonconforming rate to about 3 PPM, without any reduction in variation. Cpm = 1.26 replaces σ with the root-mean-square distance from the target, \(\sqrt{1.293^2 + 0.93^2} = 1.59\) µm.

Ppk (1.31) is close to Cpk, and an x̄–R chart of the same data in the control chart tool shows no points outside its limits and no run-rule signals, so the process looks stable over the study. The normality test gives p = 0.49 and the probability plot is straight, so the normal-model PPM is reasonable. Two warnings about precision remain. The 95 % interval for Ppk, 1.11–1.50, shows that 100 values pin the index down only to about ±0.2. And with s̄/c₄ or the pooled standard deviation instead of \(\bar R/d_2\), σ within is 1.361 or 1.339 µm and Cpk 1.24 or 1.26: with 20 subgroups, the estimators differ by a few percent from sampling alone.

Assumptions and limits

  • Stability comes first. A capability index predicts future output only if the process is in statistical control; for an unstable process it describes only the past. Plot a control chart before trusting the numbers. ISO 22514-2 sorts process behavior into eight time-dependent distribution models; only in models A1 and A2 is the process in statistical control.
  • Normality. The expected PPM, Z.bench, and the link between any index and a defect rate assume a normal distribution, and that model is least certain in the tails beyond about 3σ, exactly where the PPM is calculated and where few of your data fall to check it. Check the normality test and the probability plot. With several thousand values, or a gauge so coarse that many values repeat, the test also flags departures too small to matter; judge those from the plot. Characteristics bounded at zero, such as flatness, roundness, runout, or burr height, are skewed by nature. For them, fit a suitable distribution (a folded normal for form and position tolerances, for example) or transform the data (Box–Cox, Johnson), and use the quantile method of ISO 22514-2 and the AIAG & VDA draft, where \(X_{q}\) is the value below which \(q\) % of the fitted distribution lies:
    \[ \begin{gathered} P_p = \frac{USL - LSL}{X_{99.865} - X_{0.135}} \\[6pt] P_{pk} = \min\!\left(\frac{USL - X_{50}}{X_{99.865} - X_{50}},\ \frac{X_{50} - LSL}{X_{50} - X_{0.135}}\right) \end{gathered} \]
    For a normal distribution these reduce to the formulas above. Reading the quantiles straight from the data instead of from a fitted distribution needs about 2000 values or more (AIAG & VDA draft). The draft also describes converting the fitted fraction beyond each limit into an equivalent normal index (Bothe’s method). This calculator does not fit non-normal distributions.
  • Sample size. Capability estimates are noisy: 100 values pin Ppk down only to about ±0.2. Use at least 100 values; the AIAG & VDA draft asks for 125, in 25 subgroups of 5, collected over a period long enough for the usual sources of variation (shifts, tool changes, material lots) to act. Report the confidence interval, not just the point estimate.
  • Measurement system. Observed variance is the sum of process and gauge variance, \(\sigma^2_\text{obs} = \sigma^2_\text{process} + \sigma^2_\text{gauge}\), so a poor gauge makes the process look less capable than it is, and coarse resolution makes \(\bar R\) unreliable. The AIAG MSA manual treats %GRR under 10 % as acceptable, 10–30 % as possibly acceptable depending on the application, and over 30 % as unacceptable, with at least five distinct categories (ndc ≥ 5). ISO 22514-7 gives an alternative based on measurement uncertainty.
  • Rational subgroups. σ within is only meaningful if each subgroup was collected close together in time under the same conditions. Subgroups that span a shift change inflate \(\bar R\), which lowers Cpk toward Ppk and hides the between-subgroup instability that the comparison is meant to reveal.
  • Other indices. Many variants exist, such as Cpmk, indices for non-normal data, and multivariate indices; de-Felipe and Benedito (2017) and Yum (2023) review them.
