Low-E Coating Sheet Resistance & Emissivity Calculator

Sheet resistance is a fast, routine production check of a low-e coating: it follows the silver (or the transparent conducting oxide) that gives the coating its low emissivity. Enter a sheet resistance in ohms per square to estimate the coating’s emissivity, the conductor thickness, how strongly the coating blocks radio signals, and what it does to the U-factor of a double-glazed window. A four-point-probe converter, typical values for each coating family, and measurement notes follow.

Key terms
Low-e coating
A thin, transparent coating that reflects the long-wave infrared a warm room radiates while letting most visible light through. Most are built around one, two or three silver layers; the durable “hard-coat” type is fluorine-doped tin oxide.
Sheet resistance
The electrical resistance of a square of thin film, measured from one edge to the opposite edge. It is the same for a square of any size, hence the unit ohms per square (Ω/sq). Less resistance means more conducting material: silver low-e coatings are typically 1–6 Ω/sq.
Emissivity
How strongly a surface radiates heat compared with a perfect radiator (a black body), on a scale from 0 to 1. Uncoated glass is about 0.84; silver low-e coatings are about 0.01–0.08.
Normal and hemispherical emissivity
Normal emissivity is measured looking straight at the surface; hemispherical emissivity averages over all directions and is what heat-transfer calculations use. For low-e coatings the hemispherical value is about 10–30 % higher: 20–30 % for silver coatings and about 10–15 % for tin-oxide hard coats. European standards call their version the corrected emissivity.
U-factor
The heat flow through a window per square meter and per degree of indoor–outdoor temperature difference, in W/m²·K (or Btu/h·ft²·°F). Lower is better; a low-e coating and argon fill together roughly halve it for a double-glazed unit.
Shielding effectiveness
How much a barrier weakens a radio wave, in decibels: 30 dB cuts the power a thousandfold. The silver in low-e glass makes it a good radio shield, which is why phones can lose signal behind it.
Four-point probe and eddy-current gauge
The two common ways to measure sheet resistance. A four-point probe touches the film with four pins in a row; an eddy-current gauge senses the film through a coil without touching it.

How a low-e coating works

A silver low-e coating is a stack of nanometer-scale layers on the glass (figure). The silver does the work: its free electrons reflect the long-wave infrared that room-temperature surfaces radiate, so the coated surface also radiates little heat itself, while a layer about 10 nm thick, with oxide layers around it, still lets most visible light through. The oxides cancel the silver’s visible reflection and set the color, a zinc-oxide seed layer helps the silver grow as a dense, well-ordered film, and a thin blocker protects it while the top oxide is deposited. Double- and triple-silver coatings repeat the silver unit two or three times. Because the silver carries the current, its sheet resistance measures how much silver there is and how well it grew, and with it the emissivity.

How it is calculated

Sheet resistance is the resistance of a square of film between opposite edges, whatever the size of the square: \(R_s = \rho/t\) for a film of resistivity \(\rho\) and thickness \(t\). Room-temperature surfaces radiate in the thermal infrared, about 96 % of it between 5 and 50 µm. A metal film a few tens of nanometers thick is far thinner than these wavelengths, so to a good approximation it acts as a conducting sheet: an electric field \(E\) drives a current \(E/R_s\) per unit width. Glass is opaque at these wavelengths, so whatever the coated face does not reflect is absorbed, and by Kirchhoff’s law the absorbed fraction is the emissivity. For a plane wave at normal incidence this gives the normal emissivity of the coated face

\[ \varepsilon_n = \frac{4(y + n_g)}{(1 + y + n_g)^2}, \qquad y = \frac{Z_0}{R_s}, \]

where \(Z_0 = \mu_0 c = 376.73\ \Omega\) is the impedance of free space and \(n_g = 2\) is an effective infrared index of the glass. With no coating (\(y = 0\)) the formula gives \(\varepsilon_n = 8/9 = 0.889\), and 0.839 averaged over all directions, matching the 0.893 and 0.839 computed from the measured optical constants of window glass. At the low sheet resistance of low-e coatings the glass hardly matters, and the expression reduces to the widely used rule

\[ \varepsilon_n \approx \frac{4R_s}{Z_0} \approx 0.0106\,R_s \quad (R_s \text{ in } \Omega/\text{sq}). \]

Halving the sheet resistance halves the emissivity. For a thick metal the classic counterpart is the Hagen–Rubens relation, \(\varepsilon_n \approx \sqrt{8\varepsilon_0\omega\rho}\), which depends on the bulk resistivity and on the frequency \(\omega\) of the radiation. For a thin film only the sheet resistance counts: while \(R_s \ll Z_0\) the result is the same at every thermal wavelength and, to first order, whatever the electrons’ scattering time.

Heat radiation between window panes depends on the hemispherical emissivity, averaged over all directions. Standards obtain it from \(\varepsilon_n\) with empirical correlations. NFRC 301, used for North American ratings, applies the correlation of Rubin, Arasteh and Hartmann, which this page uses for \(\varepsilon_h\) and the U-factors:

\[ \begin{aligned} \varepsilon_h = {} & 1.3217\,\varepsilon_n - 1.8766\,\varepsilon_n^2 \\ & + 4.6586\,\varepsilon_n^3 - 5.8349\,\varepsilon_n^4 \\ & + 2.7406\,\varepsilon_n^5. \end{aligned} \]

European practice (EN 12898, EN 673) instead multiplies \(\varepsilon_n\) by a factor tabulated in ISO 10292: 1.22 at \(\varepsilon_n\) = 0.03, 1.18 at 0.05, 1.14 at 0.1, 1.10 at 0.2, 1.06 at 0.3, 1.03 at 0.4, 1.00 at 0.5, 0.98 at 0.6, 0.96 at 0.7, 0.95 at 0.8 and 0.94 at 0.89, interpolated linearly and, below 0.03, extrapolated linearly as the table allows. EN 12898:2019 replaced the table with a polynomial fitted to it. This “corrected emissivity” comes out 3–4 % below the NFRC value for \(\varepsilon_n\) from 0.01 to 0.1. For an ideal thin metal sheet the ratio \(\varepsilon_h/\varepsilon_n\) approaches 4/3 as \(\varepsilon_n \to 0\), because obliquely incident p-polarized radiation is absorbed more strongly. A full thin-film calculation for silver films 6–40 nm thick (see Assumptions and limits) gives 1.21–1.29 for \(\varepsilon_n\) from 0.09 to 0.01, within 2 % of the NFRC correlation.

The conductor thickness is \(t = \rho/R_s\) for the resistivity chosen above. The silver layers of a double- or triple-silver stack conduct in parallel, so for them it is the total silver thickness.

A thin sheet of resistance \(R_s\) across a plane radio wave in free space passes a fraction \(2/(2 + Z_0/R_s)\) of the field, so its shielding effectiveness is

\[ SE = 20\log_{10}\!\left(1 + \frac{Z_0}{2R_s}\right)\ \text{dB}, \]

the thin-film formula used for transparent conductive shields (Colaneri and Shacklette 1992). A four-point probe with equally spaced pins in a line, on a sheet much larger than the pin spacing, gives \(R_s = (\pi/\ln 2)\,V/I = 4.532\,V/I\) for a current \(I\) through the outer pins and a voltage \(V\) across the inner pins. On a finite sample the geometry factor is Smits’s correction \(C\) divided by 4.532.

The U-factors come from this site’s glazing engine: the ISO 15099 center-of-glass model at NFRC 100 winter conditions (−18 °C outside with a 5.5 m/s wind, 21 °C inside), for a unit 1 m tall of two 6 mm clear panes (glass conductivity 1 W/m·K) around a 12.7 mm gap of air or of 90 % argon and 10 % air, with \(\varepsilon_h\) on surface #2 and 0.84 on the other three glass surfaces.

Radio signals and low-e glass

The same sheet conductance reflects radio waves. For a plane wave the coating alone gives a shielding effectiveness of \(20\log_{10}(1 + Z_0/2R_s)\): about 30–36 dB for single-silver low-e at 3–6 Ω/sq, rising to 41–46 dB for triple-silver at 0.9–1.6 Ω/sq. The glass under the coating changes the total with frequency. For a 6 mm pane it is within about 0.3 dB of the formula at 0.9 GHz, but about 2 dB lower at 2.4 GHz and 6–7 dB lower at 5.5 GHz, where the pane is close to a quarter wavelength thick (calculated with the glass properties of ITU-R P.2040). That is why low-e windows weaken cell and Wi-Fi signals, and why some projects laser-pattern the coating into a frequency-selective surface that passes radio while still reflecting heat (Alcain et al. 2022).

How sheet resistance is measured

  • Four-point probe. A current passes through the outer two pins and the voltage is read across the inner two, which removes contact resistance. It is the usual laboratory method for low-e stacks and transparent conductors, and it suits exposed conductors such as pyrolytic tin oxide. On a soft-coat silver stack the pins can scratch the coating.
  • Non-contact eddy current. A coil induces currents in the film and senses the loss. It reads through the top oxide layers without touching the coating, which suits soft-coat silver stacks, handheld checks, and inline gauges that map sheet resistance across the width of a coater.
  • Emissometers. Portable emissometers compare the thermal radiation of the sample with high- and low-emissivity standards (ASTM C1371) and can be calibrated to read hemispherical emissivity. They are useful for finding which surface is coated and for calibrating the sheet-resistance-to-emissivity relation.
  • Infrared spectrometry. Reference values come from the infrared reflectance spectrum: EN 12898 averages the reflectance at 30 wavelengths from 5.5 to 50 µm chosen for a 283 K black body (ISO 20589 covers the same determination internationally), and NFRC 301 integrates at least 5–25 µm for 300 K.

Sheet resistance and color tell you different things. Sheet resistance follows the silver, while color follows the dielectric layer thicknesses through thin-film interference. Mapping both across a lite checks the silver and the dielectric layers together. The color tool explains how to measure low-e color, and the glazing calculator turns emissivity into U-factor and SHGC for a full window.

Worked example

For the default coating, \(R_s = 2.5\ \Omega\)/sq, so \(y = 376.73/2.5 = 150.69\) and

\[ \varepsilon_n = \frac{4\,(150.69 + 2)}{(1 + 150.69 + 2)^2} = 0.0259, \]

a little below the simple rule’s \(4 \times 2.5/376.73 = 0.0265\). The NFRC 301 correlation gives \(\varepsilon_h = 0.033\), and the ISO 10292 factor, 1.228, a corrected emissivity of 0.0318. With 4 µΩ·cm silver the thickness is \(4\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\text{m} / 2.5\ \Omega = 16\ \text{nm}\), and the radio attenuation is \(20\log_{10}(1 + 376.73/5) = 37.7\ \text{dB}\). In the double-glazed unit the U-factor is 1.38 W/m²·K with argon and 1.65 W/m²·K with air, against 2.68 W/m²·K uncoated. The four-point-probe default, 1.10 mV at 2.0 mA, gives \(4.532 \times 0.55\ \Omega = 2.493\ \Omega\)/sq.

Assumptions and limits

  • Thin-film relation. It treats the coating as an infinitely thin conducting sheet on glass with an effective infrared index of 2. A full thin-film calculation for an ideal free-electron silver film (Drude model with the measured product of resistivity and scattering time of silver films, and the optical constants of window glass) puts εn about 3–9 % above the line for 10–25 nm of silver, and more for thinner or thicker films.
  • Real stacks. Infrared-absorbing oxide and blocker layers, rough interfaces and imperfect films raise the emissivity further, most visibly below about 2 Ω/sq, where the silver itself absorbs little; measured double- and triple-silver stacks can sit well above the line there. Calibrate the relation against emissometer or spectrometer measurements of your own coating.
  • Doped oxides. ITO and fluorine-doped tin-oxide films are typically a few hundred nanometers thick, not thin compared with the depth thermal infrared penetrates, and their electrons scatter about as fast as the radiation oscillates. Their emissivity therefore levels off at a value set by carrier density and mobility instead of falling in proportion to sheet resistance. A free-electron model of a measured commercial tin-oxide coating, using its measured carrier density and mobility, gives about 0.15 at 13 Ω/sq, where the line gives 0.12.
  • Hemispherical values. Both conversions are empirical correlations. NFRC 301 weights the spectrum for a 300 K black body and EN 12898 for 283 K, and EN 12898 rounds εn to two decimals before converting; the thin-film estimate here depends on neither.
  • Thickness. The resistivity of thin silver depends on its own thickness and growth (about 3.5–6.5 µΩ·cm at 10–20 nm), so the thickness is an estimate.
  • Radio attenuation. Plane wave at normal incidence on the coating alone. Glass under the coating lowers it at higher frequencies (see above), insulating units add a second pane and reflections between the panes, and oblique incidence, frames and openings let more signal through, so real building losses differ.
  • U-factors. Center of glass only, for the fixed unit described above; frames and spacers raise whole-window values. European Ug values follow EN 673, with different boundary conditions, and are not directly comparable.
