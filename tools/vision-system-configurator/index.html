Machine Vision System Configurator

Describe the job: field of view, working distance, the smallest feature you need to see, and how fast the part moves. The configurator picks a standard lens for your sensor, draws the setup, and checks resolution, depth of field, motion blur, interface bandwidth, lens format, and lighting. The table at the bottom ranks 392 area-scan sensors from ten makers for the same job, with filters and a link to each sensor’s source.

Key terms
Field of view (FOV)
The width and height of the area one image must cover: the part, plus a margin for where it can land.
Working distance
How far the lens is from the part (the lens calculator calls this the object distance). The calculation measures it from the lens’s front principal plane, an optical reference point, so it differs somewhat from the working distance lens makers quote from the front of the lens barrel.
Smallest feature
The smallest defect or detail the system must find or measure, such as a scratch width, a particle, or the gap between two edges.
Pixels across the feature
How many pixels the smallest feature spans in the image. Two is the theoretical minimum; about 3 to 5 detect a feature reliably, and measuring it takes 10 or more.
Depth of field
The range of heights that stays acceptably sharp. It grows with a higher f-number (a smaller aperture) or a lower magnification.
Exposure or strobe time
How long the sensor collects light for each image. A short exposure, or a brief strobe flash, freezes a moving part.
Interface bandwidth
How many megabytes per second the camera’s connection to the computer (GigE, USB3, CoaXPress, Camera Link) can carry. It caps the frame rate of a large sensor.
Lens format
The largest sensor a lens is designed for, set by the diameter of the sharp image circle it projects. It must be at least the sensor diagonal, or the image corners go dark and soft.

How it is calculated

The lens is a thin lens with its principal plane at the working distance \(s\) from the part. The sensor of width \(w\) and height \(h\) must see a field of at least \(W \times H\), so the magnification cannot exceed

\[ \begin{gathered} m_\text{max} = \min\!\left(\frac{w}{W}, \frac{h}{H}\right), \\[6pt] f_\text{ideal} = \frac{m_\text{max}\, s}{1 + m_\text{max}}. \end{gathered} \]

In automatic mode it picks the longest standard focal length at or below \(f_\text{ideal}\) (4, 5, 6, 8, 12, 16, 25, 35, 50, 75, or 100 mm), so the field is always covered. The actual magnification is then \(m = f/(s - f)\), each pixel of pitch \(p\) covers \(p/m\) on the part, and the smallest feature spans \(d\,m/p\) pixels.

Depth of field, diffraction, and motion

Depth of field uses the exact thin-lens limits with the acceptable blur \(c\) (in pixels) and hyperfocal distance \(H = f^2/(Nc) + f\), the same model as the lens selection calculator. The height check passes only when both ends of the range, \(s \pm \Delta z/2\), lie between the near and far limits; the far side of the depth of field is always the longer one. The Airy disk diameter at the sensor is \(2.44\,\lambda N(1+m)\), and the ideal-lens MTF at the sensor Nyquist frequency \(1/(2p)\) shows whether the optics or the pixels limit resolution.

Motion blur in pixels is the distance the part moves during the exposure divided by the pixel size on the part, \(v\,t_\text{exp}\,m/p\). Keep it under one pixel for measurement and under about two for detection. Rolling-shutter sensors also skew moving parts, because rows expose at different times.

Bandwidth and lens format

The data rate is pixels per frame × bytes per pixel × frame rate. Packed 10- and 12-bit pixel formats carry 1.25 and 1.5 bytes per pixel. The interface limits are typical sustained image payloads, listed below. The sensor maker’s full-resolution frame rate is checked too, but a camera can be slower than its sensor, so check the camera datasheet as well.

USB3 Vision’s 5 Gb/s link carries 500 MB/s after 8b/10b coding, and A3 gives 350–400 MB/s sustained (passive copper cables are specified to 3 m, though 5 m cables often work). Camera Link Base and Full are 2.04 and 5.44 Gb/s at an 85 MHz pixel clock, their maximum. CoaXPress runs 6.25 Gb/s (CXP-6) or 12.5 Gb/s (CXP-12) per cable, also 8b/10b coded, keeps at least 95 % of the coded rate for image data, and can combine cables (four CXP-12 cables: about 4800 MB/s). Real throughput also depends on the host computer, so leave some margin.

A lens must project an image circle at least as large as the sensor diagonal. Format names such as 2/3″ are a legacy of television camera tubes, not a dimension: a 2/3″ sensor is about 11 mm across its diagonal, not 17 mm. The configurator suggests the smallest standard lens format that covers the sensor and the usual mount for that format. C-mount covers sensors up to about 1.1″ (17.6 mm diagonal), or 1.2″ (19.3 mm) with lenses rated for it; larger sensors need TFL (M35), TFL-II (M48, designed for APS-H, about 35 mm), M42, F-mount, M58, or M72 lenses. No standard format is listed above the 35 mm full-frame circle (Ø 43.3 mm): a larger sensor gets a warning to use custom or large-format optics whose image circle is at least the sensor diagonal. Format names that are not inch types (APS-C, APS-H, Super 35 mm, 35 mm full frame, medium format 645) are the makers’ own; the check always compares the computed diagonal with the lens circle.

Lighting

Lighting decides contrast, and contrast decides whether a feature that spans enough pixels is actually detectable. The suggestions follow standard practice: backlights for silhouettes and gauging, coaxial light for flat reflective parts, dome light for curved shiny parts, low-angle dark field for scratches and particles, and patterned backgrounds for transparent parts. The machine vision optics article covers the geometry in more depth.

Worked example

The defaults: a 120 × 90 mm field at a 300 mm working distance, a 0.2 mm smallest feature that should span 3 px, and a 2/3″ Sony IMX250 (2448 × 2048 pixels of 3.45 µm, an 8.446 × 7.066 mm sensor).

  • The width sets the limit: mmax = 8.446/120 = 0.0704 (the height would allow 7.066/90 = 0.0785), so fideal = 0.0704 × 300/1.0704 = 19.7 mm, and automatic mode picks the 16 mm lens.
  • Then m = 16/(300 − 16) = 0.0563, the field is 149.9 × 125.4 mm, each pixel covers 61.2 µm, and the 0.2 mm feature spans 3.27 px, above the target of 3.
  • At f/4 with an acceptable blur of 2 px (6.9 µm), the part stays in focus from 291.1 to 309.5 mm, a depth of field of 18.4 mm, which covers the 2 mm height variation (299 to 301 mm).
  • The working f-number is 4 × 1.0563 = 4.23, so the Airy disk at 525 nm is 5.41 µm (1.57 px), and the ideal-lens MTF at Nyquist is 60 %: the pixels, not the optics, limit the finest detail.
  • At 100 mm/s, a 100 µs exposure smears the part by 10 µm, 0.16 px. Mono8 at 10 fps is 2448 × 2048 × 1 byte × 10 = 50.1 MB/s, well inside GigE’s 115 MB/s, which allows up to 22.9 fps.
  • The 11.0 mm diagonal needs a lens rated for 2/3″ (Ø 11 mm) or larger: a C-mount lens.

Assumptions and limits

  • Thin-lens, paraxial model. Real lenses have separated principal planes and a minimum object distance, so confirm the working distance with the lens maker’s data or a test image.
  • Telecentric lenses do not follow \(m = f/(s - f)\). For gauging with height variation, the perspective check suggests when a telecentric lens is worth it.
  • In a color camera’s Bayer filter, green covers every other pixel and red and blue each cover every second row and column, so fine color detail is sampled at about half the monochrome pixel density in each direction. Use monochrome unless the decision depends on color.
  • Sensor data come from each maker’s datasheet, flyer, product page, or press release, or for a few older parts a distributor’s or camera maker’s copy (each linked in the table). Each array is the maker’s recommended recording size where one is stated (what most cameras output by default), otherwise the effective or active array; camera makers may crop further. The maximum frame rate is the maker’s full-resolution figure at the bit depth shown, an upper bound for any camera. “EOL” means the maker or an authorized distributor lists the part as discontinued, and “status unknown” means neither says either way. The year is the release year or the earliest dated maker document, so a part may be older.
  • Global-shutter sensors and interline CCDs expose every pixel at the same time. Rolling-shutter sensors expose row by row, so moving parts skew. Full-frame CCDs have no electronic shutter: the pixels keep collecting light while the image is read out, so they need a mechanical shutter or a strobe in the dark. Interline CCDs with frame readout read two interlaced fields one after the other and need the same for a full-resolution image.
  • Sensitivity and noise are not modeled. To compare cameras for dim scenes or short strobes, use their EMVA 1288 data: quantum efficiency (the share of photons that become signal), saturation capacity, and dynamic range.
