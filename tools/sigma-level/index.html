Sigma Level, DPMO & Yield Calculator

Six Sigma rates how often a process makes defects on one scale, the sigma level: the higher the level, the fewer the defects (3.4 per million opportunities at 6σ). Type a defect rate, a yield, or a sigma level and the other values follow, with or without the conventional 1.5σ shift. Below that, turn inspection counts into defects per million opportunities (DPMO), defects per unit, and first-pass yield, and see how the yields of several process steps multiply into the rolled throughput yield.

Key terms
Defect
Any single failure to meet a requirement, such as a missing hole, a scratch, or a wrong label. A unit with one or more defects is a defective unit; one unit can carry several defects.
Opportunity
One place or feature on a unit where a defect could occur and that you inspect, such as a solder joint or a measured dimension. How many to count per unit is a definition you choose: write it down and keep it fixed.
DPMO
Defects per million opportunities: the defects found divided by the opportunities inspected, times one million. The Six Sigma goal is 3.4 DPMO.
Yield
The share that comes out right. In the converter it is per opportunity (100 % minus the defect rate). First-pass yield is per unit: the share of units that pass the first time, without rework.
Sigma level
A defect rate restated as a distance on the bell curve of process variation, counted in standard deviations (σ, the usual measure of spread). Higher is better: 66 807 DPMO at 3σ, 6 210 at 4σ, and 3.4 at 6σ in the usual convention.
1.5σ shift
A convention from Motorola in the 1980s: it allows for the process average drifting toward a limit over time, so a quoted sigma level is 1.5 higher than the plain z-value of its defect rate. Whether to apply it is debated (see below).
Defects per unit (DPU)
The average number of defects found on each unit. A DPU of 0.03 means about 3 defects per 100 units.
Rolled throughput yield (RTY)
The chance that a unit gets through every step of a process the first time, with no rework. It is the step yields multiplied together, so five steps at 99 % give about 95 %.

How sigma level is calculated

A sigma level restates a defect rate as a z-value: a distance along the standard normal (bell) curve. If a fraction \(p\) of opportunities is defective, the long-term z-value is the point with that much area beyond it, and the Six Sigma convention adds 1.5:

\[ \begin{gathered} Z_\text{LT} = -\Phi^{-1}(p) \\[4pt] \sigma_\text{level} = Z_\text{LT} + 1.5 \ \ \text{(with the shift)} \\[4pt] \sigma_\text{level} = Z_\text{LT} \ \ \text{(no shift)} \end{gathered} \]

\(\Phi\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function and \(\Phi^{-1}\) its inverse. DPMO is \(10^6 p\), the defect rate is \(100p\) %, and the yield per opportunity is \(100(1-p)\) %; in the other direction, \(p = \Phi(1.5 - \sigma_\text{level})\) with the shift. The conversion is one-sided: every defect counts in the tail beyond one specification limit. ISO 13053-1 builds its sigma table this way (clauses 5.2 and 5.3, Table 2 and Annex A), as do most Six Sigma tables, so that 6σ gives 3.4 DPMO. Some textbook tables, such as Figure 1.12 in Montgomery, also count the small tail beyond the far limit; that matters only below about 3σ (697 672 instead of 691 462 DPMO at 1σ).

The line under the converter adds two comparisons. Ppk ≈ \(Z_\text{LT}/3\) is the performance index of a normally distributed process whose defects all lie beyond one limit (see the capability calculator). A centered process with limits at \(\pm\sigma_\text{level}\) would make \(2\Phi(-\sigma_\text{level})\times10^6\) parts per million, the last column of the table below.

The 1.5σ shift: where it comes from

Six Sigma began at Motorola in the 1980s. Engineer Bill Smith proposed a 50 % design margin for key product characteristics, which puts the specification limits six standard deviations from the target instead of three; from 1987, Motorola’s designers worked to ±6σ limits (Smith, 1993). Mikel Harry, whose team put the idea into practice, recalled that Smith believed the process average typically shifts by about 1.5σ from target over time (Harry, 2004), and Motorola’s 1988 guide to tolerancing by Harry and Stewart built a 1.5σ shift of the mean into its tolerance stack-ups. Statistical tolerancing already widened root-sum-square stack-ups by a factor of 1.5 to allow for drifting component means (Gilson, 1951, and Bender, 1962, both cited by de Treville et al., 2023; Evans, 1975, reviews such allowances), and de Treville et al. suggest that Harry’s number came from that allowance, from a confidence interval on the estimated mean, or from both. Harry later described the 1.5σ not as a literal movement of the mean but as an “equivalent mean shift” that allows for long-term random sampling error.

ISO 13053-1 adopts the convention and explains it as an allowance for changes in the process mean between the short and the long term. A process quoted at 6σ is therefore expected to deliver, over the long term, the defect rate of a one-sided tail 4.5σ out: 3.4 DPMO. Without the shift, 6σ would mean 0.001 DPMO one-sided, or 0.002 two-sided.

The 1.5σ shift: the debate

  • For. Monitoring catches small shifts slowly. An X̄ chart with subgroups of four and 3σ limits needs on average about 2 subgroups to signal a 1.5σ shift, 6 for a 1σ shift, and 44 for a 0.5σ shift, so even a monitored process runs off center part of the time. Bothe (2002) uses this sensitivity of control charts as a statistical reason for the allowance. Montgomery calls the Six Sigma process “one way to model” the disturbances that move real processes: probably not exactly right, but “a useful way to think about process performance.”
  • Against. Montgomery also notes an apparent inconsistency: performance can be predicted only for a stable process, and a mean that drifts may move more than the “allowed” 1.5σ. Wheeler (2003) observes that early documents allude to a research study behind the figure but never cite one, that shifts of 3σ and more are common in production, and that without monitoring there is no limit to them, so 1.5σ is a best case rather than a worst case; he also doubts parts-per-million figures taken this far into the tails of an assumed normal model. De Treville et al. (2023) argue that a literal 1.5σ shift contradicts statistical control: when the mean moves, the cause should be found and the process recentered. Coskun and Ialongo (2020) conclude that the shift should not be applied as a universal constant, only where there is evidence that the process in question drifts.

The 1.5σ is a convention, not a law of nature. Processes that are recalibrated often or held on target by feedback control can drift much less; processes that nobody watches can drift much more. For engineering decisions it is clearer to quote measured long-term performance directly, as Ppk or observed parts per million, and to say which convention any sigma level uses.

DPU, DPO, first-pass yield, and rolled throughput yield

\[ \begin{gathered} DPU = \frac{D}{U}, \qquad DPO = \frac{D}{U \cdot O} \\[8pt] DPMO = 10^6\,DPO \\[4pt] FPY \approx e^{-DPU} \end{gathered} \]

If defects land on units at random, the number of defects on a unit follows a Poisson distribution with mean DPU, and the chance that a unit has none is \(e^{-DPU}\). This is an approximation: if each of the \(O\) opportunities on a unit fails independently with probability DPO, the exact chance is \((1-DPO)^O\). The two agree closely when DPO is small, the usual case (96.88 % for both in the example below). With one opportunity per unit, each defect is on a different unit and the first-pass yield is exactly \(1 - DPU\).

If the observed first-pass yield is clearly higher than the Poisson estimate, defects are clustering on fewer units than chance would give, which points to a specific cause such as a bad batch or a station that damages some parts. The calculator calls the difference clear when it exceeds two standard deviations of the number of defect-free units under random placement, \(\sqrt{U e^{-DPU}\left(1-(1+DPU)\,e^{-DPU}\right)}\). Semiconductor yield models describe clustered defects with the negative binomial yield \(Y = (1 + DPU/\alpha)^{-\alpha}\), which always lies above \(e^{-DPU}\) and approaches it as the cluster parameter \(\alpha\) grows (Stapper, 1973; Cunningham, 1990).

\[ \begin{gathered} RTY = \prod_{i=1}^{n} FPY_i \approx e^{-\sum_i DPU_i} \\[4pt] Y_\text{norm} = RTY^{1/n} \\[4pt] TDPU = -\ln RTY \end{gathered} \]

The rolled throughput yield of a process with \(n\) steps is the product of the steps’ first-pass yields: the chance that a unit passes every step without rework, repair, or adjustment (ISO 13053-1, clause 5.4). A final yield that counts reworked units as good hides those losses; in the standard’s example, a final yield of 97.0 % against an RTY of 89.7 %. The normalized yield is the geometric mean of the step yields, and the total defects per unit, TDPU, is the number of defects per unit across all steps that the RTY implies under the Poisson model (Harry, 2004).

Zero defects

With no defects in \(n = U \cdot O\) opportunities, the defect rate is small, not zero. The calculator then reports a lower bound on the sigma level, from the exact one-sided 95 % upper confidence limit of a binomial proportion (Clopper and Pearson, 1934):

\[ p_{95} = 1 - 0.05^{1/n} \approx \frac{3}{n} \]

\(3/n\) is the “rule of three” (Hanley and Lippman-Hand, 1983), close to the exact value for \(n\) above about 30. For 1200 units with 12 opportunities each, \(p_{95} = 2.08\times10^{-4}\), or 208 DPMO, so the sigma level is at least 5.03 with the shift (3.53 without). The bound treats the opportunities as independent trials.

Worked example

An inspection finds 38 defects on 1200 units, with 12 defect opportunities per unit. That gives DPU = 0.0317, DPO = 0.00264, and DPMO = 2639. This corresponds to Z = 2.79 long-term, or a 4.29σ sigma level with the shift. The Poisson first-pass yield, \(e^{-0.0317} = 96.88\,\%\), agrees with the observed 1163/1200 = 96.92 %: the difference is 0.4 defect-free units, about half a standard deviation (0.76), so the defects look randomly spread rather than clustered. Five steps with first-pass yields of 98.5, 99.2, 97.8, 99.6, and 98.9 % give RTY = 94.13 %, a normalized yield of 98.80 % per step, and TDPU = 0.0605. About one unit in seventeen needs rework somewhere, even though no single step looks worse than 97.8 %.

Assumptions and limits

  • Opportunity counts are a judgment call. Counting more opportunities per unit lowers DPMO and raises the sigma level without any change to the product. In Wheeler’s example, 2.5 blemishes per 100 yards of cloth is 8 333 DPMO with one opportunity per foot but 694 DPMO with one per inch (3.9σ against 4.7σ). ISO 13053-2 counts the critical-to-quality characteristics of a unit: a pizza delivery that can be late, cold, or the wrong pizza has three, so 36 defects in 50 000 deliveries is 240 DPMO. Define opportunities once, document the definition, and keep it fixed when comparing over time.
  • Normal tails. Converting a defect rate to a z-value assumes normally distributed variation, and at high sigma levels it relies on the far tails of the normal curve, where real processes rarely follow it. For attribute defects (missing features, contamination) the sigma level is only a common scale, not a physical distance to a limit.
  • One-sided convention. Every defect is assigned to one tail. For a centered process with two-sided limits, use the two-sided column in the table or the capability calculator.
  • Random defects. The Poisson first-pass yield and the clustering check assume that defects land on units independently. With one opportunity per unit there is nothing to compare: each defect fails a different unit.
  • Independent steps. The product of the step yields is the chance of passing every step when each step’s first-pass yield is counted on the units that came through the earlier steps without rework, or when the steps fail independently, as ISO 13053-1 assumes. When the units that fail one step are also more likely to fail later ones (a weak part, a marginal setup), failures pile up on the same units and the true RTY is higher than the product of separately measured yields; when failures tend to exclude each other, it is lower. Track units through the line to measure RTY directly when the difference matters.
  • Numerical range. The six digits shown are correct to within one unit in the last digit. With the shift, sigma levels from about −6.8 to 39 convert; beyond them the defect rate rounds to 100 % or falls below 10−308, the smallest normal double-precision number.
