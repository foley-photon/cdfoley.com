Laser Fluence & Irradiance Calculator

Fluence (J/cm²) and irradiance (W/cm²) control how a laser pulse interacts with a material. This calculator gives the peak (on-axis) fluence and irradiance, and the average fluence, for Gaussian and top-hat beams. How it is calculated

A Gaussian beam with 1/e² radius \(w_0\) and pulse energy \(E_p\) has the fluence distribution

\[ F(r) = F_0\, e^{-2r^2/w_0^2}, \qquad F_0 = \frac{2E_p}{\pi w_0^2}. \]

The peak irradiance follows from the peak power, \(I_0 = 2P_\text{peak}/(\pi w_0^2)\), where \(P_\text{peak} = kE_p/\tau\) with \(k = 1\), 0.9394, or 0.8814 for rectangular, Gaussian, or sech² pulses (see pulse energy & peak power). For a top-hat beam of diameter \(d\), the fluence is uniform: \(F = E_p/(\pi d^2/4)\).

Peak or average? The factor of two

Two conventions are in common use, and they differ by exactly a factor of two for a Gaussian beam:

  • Peak (on-axis) fluence \(F_0 = 2E_p/(\pi w_0^2)\). This is the value at the center of the beam. Damage thresholds (ISO 21254 uses the effective area \(A_\text{eff} = \pi w_0^2/2\)), ablation thresholds from the Liu method, and most ultrafast-ablation papers use it.
  • Average fluence \(E_p/(\pi w_0^2)\): the energy divided by the area of the 1/e² circle. It is a common shortcut, but it equals \(F_0/2\), and only 86.5 % of the energy actually falls inside that circle.

Comparing an average fluence with a threshold quoted as a peak value understates your fluence by a factor of two. Your fluence-to-threshold ratio then looks half as large as it really is: an ablation process seems closer to threshold than it is, and an optic seems to have twice its real margin below the damage threshold. Before comparing a process window against literature or a vendor specification, check which convention each source uses.

Threshold diameter

Where \(F(r)\) exceeds a threshold \(F_\text{th}\), the material is modified or ablated. Setting \(F(r) = F_\text{th}\) gives

\[ D_\text{th} = w_0\sqrt{2\ln\!\left(\frac{F_0}{F_\text{th}}\right)}, \]
\[ \frac{E_\text{inside}}{E_p} = 1 - \frac{F_\text{th}}{F_0}. \]

The feature grows only slowly with fluence, because \(D_\text{th}^2\) is proportional to \(\ln(F_0/F_\text{th})\). Doubling the pulse energy at \(F_0/F_\text{th} = 5\) widens it by only about 20 %. Fitting D² against ln Ep is the Liu method for measuring \(w_0\) and \(F_\text{th}\), implemented in the ablation threshold & depth tool.

Worked example

A 20 µJ, 10 ns Gaussian pulse focused to a 30 µm (1/e²) spot:

\[ F_0 = \frac{2 \times 20\ \mu\text{J}}{\pi\,(15\ \mu\text{m})^2} = 5.66\ \text{J/cm}^2, \]
\[ I_0 = \frac{0.9394\,F_0}{10\ \text{ns}} = 5.3\times10^{8}\ \text{W/cm}^2. \]

The average fluence \(E_p/(\pi w_0^2)\) is 2.83 J/cm². With a 1 J/cm² threshold, the modified diameter is 27.9 µm, and 82 % of the pulse energy lands inside it.

Assumptions and limits

  • The spot diameter is the 1/e² diameter at the work surface. Defocus increases it: see Gaussian beam propagation. For a Gaussian spot measured as a FWHM diameter, multiply by 1.699 to get the 1/e² diameter.
  • Fluence is incident fluence. Absorbed fluence is lower by the surface reflectance, which for metals in the near-IR is roughly 60–98 %.
  • A real top-hat has soft edges, and a real Gaussian may have M² > 1 or ellipticity. Measure the profile (ISO 11146) when accuracy matters.
  • At high irradiance, plasma shielding and nonlinear absorption change how much energy reaches the surface. Those effects are not modeled.
  • The threshold diameter applies to the pulse count the threshold was measured for. With overlapping pulses, incubation lowers the threshold, so enter \(F_\text{th}\) measured for the same number of pulses per spot (see ablation threshold & depth).
