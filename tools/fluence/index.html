Laser Fluence & Irradiance Calculator

Fluence (energy per unit area, in J/cm²) and irradiance (power per unit area, in W/cm²) decide what a laser pulse does to a surface: whether it marks it, removes material, or damages it. Enter the pulse energy and spot size to get the peak (on-axis) fluence and irradiance, and the average fluence, for Gaussian and top-hat beams. Add a threshold fluence to see how far above it you are and how wide the affected region is.

Key terms
Fluence
Energy delivered per unit area of the surface, in J/cm². For a single pulse it decides whether the material is modified, removed, or damaged.
Irradiance
Power per unit area, in W/cm²; also called intensity. The value here is at the top of the pulse and the center of the beam.
Spot diameter (1/e²)
The usual way to state a laser spot size: the diameter at which the fluence has fallen to 13.5 % (1/e²) of its central value. About 86.5 % of a Gaussian beam’s energy falls inside it.
Gaussian and top-hat beams
A Gaussian beam is strongest at the center and fades smoothly outward, like a bell curve; many lasers focus to a near-Gaussian spot. A top-hat beam, made with beam-shaping optics, is uniform across the spot and drops sharply at its edge.
Peak fluence
The fluence at the center of the beam, where it is highest. For a Gaussian beam it is twice the pulse energy divided by the area of the 1/e² circle. Damage standards and crater-size (Liu) ablation measurements quote thresholds this way.
Average fluence
The pulse energy divided by the area of the 1/e² circle. A common shortcut; for a Gaussian beam it is exactly half the peak fluence.
Threshold fluence
The lowest fluence at which a material is modified or removed (the ablation threshold) or an optic is damaged (the damage threshold). It depends on the material, the wavelength, the pulse duration, and the number of pulses.
Effective area
The area that would hold the whole pulse energy if it were spread evenly at the peak fluence. For a Gaussian beam it is half the area of the 1/e² circle.

Inputs

The reading of a pulse-energy meter. If you know the average power and repetition rate instead, choose that option above.

The diameter at the work surface: from a beam profiler, the focused-spot calculator, or a crater-size fit in the ablation tool. For a Gaussian spot quoted as a FWHM diameter, multiply by 1.699 to get the 1/e² diameter.

The pulse duration (FWHM) and shape set only the peak power and irradiance; the fluence does not depend on them.

How it is calculated

A Gaussian beam with 1/e² radius \(w_0\) and pulse energy \(E_p\) has the fluence distribution

\[ F(r) = F_0\, e^{-2r^2/w_0^2}, \qquad F_0 = \frac{2E_p}{\pi w_0^2}. \]

The peak irradiance follows from the peak power, \(I_0 = 2P_\text{peak}/(\pi w_0^2)\), where \(P_\text{peak} = kE_p/\tau\) with \(k = 1\), 0.9394, or 0.8814 for rectangular, Gaussian, or sech² pulses (see pulse energy & peak power). For a top-hat beam of diameter \(d\), the fluence is uniform: \(F = E_p/(\pi d^2/4)\).

Peak or average? The factor of two

Two conventions are in common use, and they differ by exactly a factor of two for a Gaussian beam:

  • Peak (on-axis) fluence \(F_0 = 2E_p/(\pi w_0^2)\). This is the value at the center of the beam. Laser-damage standards use it: ISO 21254 divides the pulse energy by an effective area \(A_\text{eff} = E_p/F_0\), which is \(\pi w_0^2/2\) for a Gaussian beam. Ablation thresholds from crater-size (Liu) fits, the usual method in ultrafast-ablation papers, are peak values too.
  • Average fluence \(E_p/(\pi w_0^2)\): the energy divided by the area of the 1/e² circle. It is a common shortcut, but it equals \(F_0/2\), and only 86.5 % of the energy actually falls inside that circle.

Comparing an average fluence with a threshold quoted as a peak value understates your fluence by a factor of two. Your fluence-to-threshold ratio then looks half as large as it really is: an ablation process seems closer to threshold than it is, and an optic seems to have twice its real margin below the damage threshold. Published damage thresholds that used the average convention without saying so are a known source of this error. Before comparing a process window against literature or a vendor specification, check which convention each source uses.

Threshold diameter

Where \(F(r)\) exceeds a threshold \(F_\text{th}\), the material is modified or ablated. Setting \(F(r) = F_\text{th}\) gives

\[ D_\text{th} = w_0\sqrt{2\ln\!\left(\frac{F_0}{F_\text{th}}\right)}, \]
\[ \frac{E_\text{inside}}{E_p} = 1 - \frac{F_\text{th}}{F_0}. \]

The feature grows only slowly with fluence, because \(D_\text{th}^2\) is proportional to \(\ln(F_0/F_\text{th})\). Doubling the pulse energy at \(F_0/F_\text{th} = 5\) widens it by only about 20 %. Fitting D² against ln Ep is the Liu method for measuring \(w_0\) and \(F_\text{th}\), implemented in the ablation threshold & depth tool.

Repeated pulses on the same spot lower the threshold (incubation), here by a factor of 5 to 13. Thresholds also depend on the surface finish, the wavelength, and the pulse duration, so treat these as starting points and measure your own material.

Worked example

A 20 µJ, 10 ns Gaussian pulse focused to a 30 µm (1/e²) spot:

\[ F_0 = \frac{2 \times 20\ \mu\text{J}}{\pi\,(15\ \mu\text{m})^2} = 5.66\ \text{J/cm}^2, \]
\[ I_0 = \frac{0.9394\,F_0}{10\ \text{ns}} = 5.3\times10^{8}\ \text{W/cm}^2. \]

The average fluence \(E_p/(\pi w_0^2)\) is 2.83 J/cm². With a 1 J/cm² threshold, the modified diameter is 27.9 µm, and 82 % of the pulse energy lands inside it.

Assumptions and limits

  • The spot diameter is the 1/e² diameter at the work surface. Defocus increases it: see Gaussian beam propagation. For a Gaussian beam the 1/e² diameter equals the second-moment (D4σ) diameter of ISO 11146-1. For a Gaussian spot measured as a FWHM diameter, multiply by 1.699 to get the 1/e² diameter.
  • Fluence is incident fluence. An opaque surface absorbs only the fraction 1 − R, where R is its reflectance. Computed from published optical constants, R for clean, polished metals at 1064 nm runs from about 58 % (chromium) and 63 % (iron) through 78 % (nickel) and 95 % (aluminum) to 97–99.7 % (copper, gold, silver). Roughness and contamination raise the absorption.
  • A real top-hat has soft edges, and a real Gaussian may have M² > 1 or ellipticity. When accuracy matters, measure the beam widths (ISO 11146-1) and, for shaped beams, the fluence distribution (ISO 13694).
  • Pulses are separate in time. In average-power mode the calculator stops when the wings of neighboring pulses would add more than 1 % to the peak irradiance: above τ·frep ≈ 0.72 for Gaussian pulses and 0.53 for sech² (1 for rectangular).
  • At high irradiance, plasma shielding and nonlinear absorption change how much energy reaches the surface. Those effects are not modeled.
  • The threshold diameter applies to the pulse count the threshold was measured for. With overlapping pulses, incubation lowers the threshold, so enter \(F_\text{th}\) measured for the same number of pulses per spot (see ablation threshold & depth).
