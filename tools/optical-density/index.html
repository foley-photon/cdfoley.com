Optical Density (OD) & ND Filter Calculator

How much does a filter dim a laser beam? Convert between the ways that dimming (attenuation) is specified: optical density, transmittance, decibels, and percent blocked. Then stack neutral-density filters to bring a laser down to a power that a camera, power meter, or spectrometer can take.

Key terms
Optical density (OD)
A logarithmic measure of how strongly a filter dims light. OD 1 passes 10 %, OD 2 passes 1 %, and OD 3 passes 0.1 %.
Transmittance (T)
The fraction of the incoming power that gets through, from 0 to 1.
Decibel (dB)
Ten times the OD: OD 3 is 30 dB. Common in fiber optics and electronics.
Neutral-density (ND) filter
A gray filter that dims all colors in its working range by about the same amount. Absorptive ND filters are tinted glass; reflective ones carry a thin metal coating.
Absorbance
The spectroscopy name for the same logarithm when the light is lost by absorption in a sample.
Damage threshold
The power or energy per unit area a filter can take without cracking, burning, or changing.
MPE
Maximum permissible exposure: the most laser light the eye or skin may receive, set by the laser-safety standards. It decides the OD that safety eyewear needs.

How it is calculated

Optical density is the base-10 logarithm of the attenuation. Decibels use ten times the same logarithm:

\[ \mathrm{OD} = -\log_{10} T = \log_{10}\frac{P_\text{in}}{P_\text{out}}, \qquad \mathrm{dB} = 10\,\mathrm{OD}, \qquad T = 10^{-\mathrm{OD}}. \]

Because these are logarithms, filters in series add: \(\mathrm{OD}_\text{total} = \sum \mathrm{OD}_i\), equivalent to multiplying their transmittances. The OD needed to bring a beam from \(P_\text{in}\) down to \(P_\text{target}\) is \(\log_{10}(P_\text{in}/P_\text{target})\).

Laser-safety eyewear uses the same logarithm with exposures in place of powers: \(\mathrm{OD} = \log_{10}(H_0/\mathrm{MPE})\), where \(H_0\) is the worst-case exposure at the eye and MPE the maximum permissible exposure, both in the same units (W/cm² or J/cm²) and for the same wavelength and exposure time. The MPE values come from ANSI Z136.1 or IEC 60825-1. Eyewear must also survive the beam: ANSI Z136.1 asks for a filter damage threshold over a stated exposure time (typically 10 s), and the EN 207 scale numbers (LB) combine a minimum OD with a direct-hit test of 5 s for continuous lasers or 50 pulses for pulsed ones. This tool does not calculate MPEs; choose eyewear with your laser safety officer.

OD is not always absorbance

For an absorptive ND filter (tinted glass), the OD is the internal absorbance of the glass plus a small surface-reflection term:

\[ \mathrm{OD} = \mathrm{OD}_\text{int} - \log_{10}P, \qquad \mathrm{OD}_\text{int} = \frac{\alpha d}{\ln 10}, \qquad P \approx \frac{2n}{n^2+1}. \]

The internal part grows in proportion to the thickness \(d\) (the Beer–Lambert law, with absorption coefficient \(\alpha\)), which is how one glass gives a range of ODs. The reflection factor \(P\) of two uncoated surfaces is 0.92 at \(n = 1.5\), about OD 0.035. A reflective ND filter (a thin metal or dielectric coating) reflects much of the light; a metal coating such as Inconel also absorbs part of it. Its OD describes transmission only. The “missing” power goes partly back toward the source, where it can disturb the laser or create ghost beams. In spectroscopy, absorbance \(A = -\log_{10}(I/I_0)\) is the same logarithm, measured against a reference so that it leaves out reflection at the cell walls.

Worked example

The default inputs: a 2 W beam needs to be at or below 1 mW for a camera. The required OD is \(\log_{10}(2\,\text{W}/1\,\text{mW}) = 3.30\). Stacking OD 1 + OD 2 + OD 0.5 = OD 3.5 transmits \(2\,\text{W}\times10^{-3.5} = 0.63\) mW, which meets the target. The stack absorbs or reflects 1.999 W, and the first filter in the beam (OD 1) takes 1.8 W of that. That is a lot for an ND filter: check its damage threshold for your beam size, or pick off a small fraction of the beam first.

Assumptions and limits

  • OD depends on wavelength. The OD of absorptive ND glass can change by a factor of two or more between the visible and the near-infrared: 1 mm of SCHOTT NG9 glass has an internal OD of 1.38 at 550 nm, 0.92 at 1060 nm, and 0.49 at 1550 nm. Metal-coated reflective filters are flatter, but only over their stated range. Use the vendor’s curve at your wavelength rather than the nominal value, and calibrate a filter in your own setup when the number matters.
  • Stacking reflective filters creates multiple reflections between them. Light bouncing between two parallel filters adds to the transmitted beam: with reflectances \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) facing each other, the pair transmits \(T_1T_2/(1 - R_1R_2)\) instead of \(T_1T_2\), so two filters that each reflect 50 % pass a third more than the summed OD predicts. With laser light the gap can act as a resonator. Tilt the filters by a few degrees so back-reflections leave the beam path, and don’t let them return into the laser.
  • High power. ND filters are generally not high-power attenuators. An absorptive filter at the front of a stack absorbs most of the power and can crack or thermally lens, and a metal-coated reflective filter also absorbs part of the light, in a very thin film. For watts, pick off a small fraction with a beam sampler or wedge, send the rest into a beam dump, and keep every filter below its damage threshold.
  • Ultrashort pulses. At the peak intensities of picosecond and femtosecond pulses, glass, polymer, and interference filters can show nonlinear effects such as saturable absorption that lower their OD; ANSI Z136.1 warns of this for eyewear filters. Attenuate where the beam is large, and ask the vendor for data at your pulse duration.
  • High OD is hard to verify. At OD 5 a filter passes only 10⁻⁵ of the light, so a little light that scatters or leaks around it can exceed what goes through. Block every path around the filters before trusting a measured high OD.
