How it is calculated

Two lenses separated by the sum of their focal lengths form an afocal telescope: collimated light in, collimated light out. With \(f_1\) the input lens and \(f_2\) the output lens (signed, positive for converging),

\[ \begin{gathered} M = -\frac{f_2}{f_1}, \quad L = f_1 + f_2, \\[4pt] D_\text{out} = |M|\,D_\text{in}, \quad \Theta_\text{out} = \frac{\Theta_\text{in}}{|M|}. \end{gathered} \]

A Galilean expander pairs a negative input lens with a positive output lens. \(M\) is positive (upright beam) and the tube is shorter than \(f_2\). A Keplerian expander uses two positive lenses. \(M\) is negative (the beam is inverted) and there is a real focus between the lenses.

The product of diameter and divergence is conserved, so expansion does not change beam quality. It only redistributes it. If you leave the input divergence blank, the calculator uses \(\Theta = 4M^2\lambda/(\pi D_\text{in})\), which is M² times the diffraction limit. If you enter a measured divergence, it takes precedence over the M² input, and the calculator reports the implied M² so you can check the datasheet for consistency.

Effect on the focused spot

A focusing lens maps the far field to its focal plane, so the spot diameter is the focal length times the full divergence of the beam entering the lens:

\[ \begin{gathered} d_F = f_F\,\Theta = \frac{4M^2\lambda f_F}{\pi D} \\[4pt] \Longrightarrow\quad d_{F,\text{expanded}} = \frac{d_{F,\text{direct}}}{|M|}. \end{gathered} \]

A 3× expander therefore gives a spot one-third the size, nine times the peak fluence, and one-ninth the depth of focus (\(z_R \propto d_F^2\)). Expanding the beam is an alternative to a shorter focal length lens, but check that the expanded beam still fits through the scanner mirrors and focusing optics without clipping. The focused spot tool includes aperture truncation.

Galilean or Keplerian?

  • Galilean has no internal focus, so it is preferred for pulsed lasers with high peak power: there is no risk of air breakdown or of damaging an optic placed near the focus. It is also shorter: for the same magnification and output lens, the lens separation is \(f_2 - |f_1|\) instead of \(f_2 + |f_1|\).
  • Keplerian has an accessible internal focus. A pinhole placed there acts as a spatial filter that strips high-spatial-frequency noise from the beam. The spot is about \(f_1\Theta_\text{in}\), and a pinhole of roughly 1.5 times the 1/e² focus diameter passes about 99 % of a TEM₀₀ beam. At high peak power the internal focus can ionize air, so evacuate the filter or use a Galilean design instead.

Worked example

A 3 mm laser beam with a 1.2 mrad full-angle divergence (implied M² ≈ 2.66 at 1064 nm) passes through a Galilean expander with \(f_1 = -50\) mm and \(f_2 = 150\) mm:

  • \(|M| = 3\) and \(L = 100\) mm. The output is 9 mm in diameter with 0.40 mrad divergence.
  • With a 100 mm focusing lens the spot shrinks from \(100\,\text{mm}\times 1.2\,\text{mrad} = 120\) µm to 40 µm. The Rayleigh range at focus becomes 0.44 mm.
  • A Keplerian version (\(f_1 = +50\) mm) needs \(L = 200\) mm and has a 60 µm internal focus. A 90 µm pinhole would make a suitable spatial filter.

Assumptions and limits

  • Thin, aberration-free lenses. Real expanders have a lens spacing that differs slightly from \(f_1 + f_2\) because of principal-plane positions, and they are usually adjustable to set collimation. Use the vendor's mechanical data.
  • Collimated input. If the input beam's waist is far from the expander, the output waist moves too. The divergence ratio still holds, but the location of the minimum spot does not. The M² and divergence relations also treat \(D_\text{in}\) as the waist diameter. If the expander is more than a small fraction of the input Rayleigh range (about \(D_\text{in}/\Theta_\text{in}\)) from the laser's waist, the implied M² comes out too high and the M²-based divergence too low. See Gaussian beam propagation.
  • No clipping. Both lenses and every downstream aperture must be at least about 1.6× the local 1/e² beam diameter (π times the 1/e² radius, which passes 99.3 % of the power). See beam clipping.
  • Focused-spot estimate assumes an untruncated beam with no lens aberrations, paraxial focusing (the results flag a convergence half-angle above 0.15 rad), and the focal plane close to the beam waist.
