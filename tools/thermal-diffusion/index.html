Thermal Diffusion Length & Heat Accumulation Calculator

How far does heat spread during a laser pulse, and how fast can you pulse before heat builds up from one pulse to the next? This tool estimates both, and checks whether the heat stays within the depth where the light is absorbed. Use it to choose a pulse duration and repetition rate that keep the heat-affected zone small. Includes room-temperature diffusivities for common metals, glasses, and polymers.

Key terms
Thermal diffusivity
How quickly a temperature change spreads through a material, in mm²/s: the thermal conductivity divided by density and specific heat. Copper is about 117 mm²/s, stainless steel about 4, glass under 1, and water 0.15.
Thermal diffusion length
Roughly how far heat spreads during the pulse. It grows with the square root of time, so a pulse 100 times longer spreads heat only 10 times farther.
Optical penetration depth
The depth at which the light being absorbed has fallen to 1/e (37 %) of its value at the surface. It is about 8–21 nm for metals in the near infrared, and far longer in clear glass.
Thermal confinement
The heat stays where the light was absorbed for the whole pulse, because the diffusion length is shorter than the penetration depth. It favors clean ablation.
Heat-affected zone
The material around the processed spot that got hot enough to change: melt, recast, discoloration, or cracks.
Heat accumulation
Leftover heat from earlier pulses raising the temperature, when pulses arrive faster than heat can leave the spot.
Repetition rate
Pulses per second, in kHz or MHz. Its inverse is the time between pulses: 100 kHz means 10 µs.
Spot diameter (1/e²)
The diameter of the focused spot on the part, measured where the intensity has fallen to 13.5 % of its peak.

How it is calculated

Heat released at a surface spreads with the diffusion equation. After a time \(t\), the temperature profile from an instantaneous planar source falls as \(\exp(-x^2/4Dt)\), so it drops to 1/e at

\[ L_\text{th} = 2\sqrt{D\tau}, \qquad D = \frac{k}{\rho\, c_p}, \]

where \(k\) is the thermal conductivity, \(\rho\) the density, and \(c_p\) the specific heat (Carslaw and Jaeger). Authors differ on the prefactor: \(\sqrt{D\tau}\), \(\sqrt{2D\tau}\), and \(2\sqrt{D\tau}\) all appear. ISO 21254-1, for example, uses \(\sqrt{2D\tau}\) as the “thermal transit distance” in laser-damage testing. The calculator shows two of them, \(2\sqrt{D\tau}\) and \(\sqrt{D\tau}\), so check which one a source uses before comparing numbers. Diffusion length scales only with \(\sqrt{\tau}\). Going from 10 ns to 10 ps shrinks it by a factor of about 32, not 1000. The ratio \(L_\text{th}/w_0\) shows where the heat goes during the pulse: mainly into the depth when it is much less than 1, and also sideways out of the spot when it approaches 1.

Thermal confinement

If \(L_\text{th} \lesssim \delta\), the optical penetration depth, heat stays in the volume where it was absorbed during the pulse. The pulse is thermally confined. Energy density then builds up efficiently and ablation is relatively clean, with a small heat-affected zone. If \(L_\text{th} \gg \delta\), heat leaks into the bulk during the pulse. More energy is needed to reach ablation, and melt and recast appear. For metals, \(\delta\) is about 8–21 nm at 1064 nm (table below), so confinement needs pulses shorter than about 30 ps in stainless steel and shorter than 1 ps in copper or aluminum, where electron heat transport takes over (see the limits below).

Heat accumulation between pulses

After each pulse, heat must spread out of the irradiated spot before the next pulse arrives. Spreading over the beam radius takes about

\[ t_w \approx \frac{w_0^2}{4D} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_\text{acc} \approx \frac{4D}{w_0^2}. \]

Above roughly \(f_\text{acc}\), residual heat from earlier pulses raises the baseline temperature. The process then behaves more like a quasi-CW heat source: the heat-affected zone grows, melt and burr formation increase, and the effective threshold drops. Using the full diameter instead of the radius gives a 4× lower, more conservative onset. The badge compares the pulse period \(T = 1/f\) with \(t_w\): it reads “likely” when \(T < t_w\), “borderline” when \(T\) is between \(t_w\) and \(4t_w\), and “dissipates” above that. The transition is gradual because residual heat decays slowly, so treat \(f_\text{acc}\) as an order-of-magnitude guide; how much the build-up matters also depends on the pulse energy and average power. Weber et al. give an analytical treatment of heat accumulation and of the largest average power usable at a given repetition rate. Measured examples include femtosecond waveguide writing in borosilicate glass, where cumulative heating enlarged the modified zone above about 200 kHz (Eaton et al.), and scanned ultrashort-pulse ablation of metals, where it caused a sharp change from a smooth to a rough, dark surface (Bauer et al.). Pulse overlap raises the number of pulses per point and makes accumulation worse (see the overlap tool).

In materials that are transparent at the laser wavelength, linear absorption is weak and δ is far longer than any thermal length. Ultrashort pulses are then absorbed nonlinearly, near the focus, which this simple picture does not cover.

Worked example

Stainless steel 304 (D = 3.95 mm²/s) with 10 ns pulses: \(L_\text{th} = 2\sqrt{3.95\ \text{mm}^2/\text{s}\times 10\ \text{ns}} \approx 0.40\ \mu\text{m}\). That is about 20× the optical penetration depth of a metal in the near infrared (δ ≈ 20 nm), so the process is not thermally confined. The same steel with 10 ps pulses gives 12.6 nm, which is comparable to δ. For a 30 µm spot, \(t_w = (15\ \mu\text{m})^2/(4D) = 14\ \mu\text{s}\), so heat accumulation sets in around 70 kHz. At the default 100 kHz the pulse period is 0.70 tw, so expect accumulation, more so with overlapping pulses. In fused silica the same spot accumulates heat from about 15 kHz. In copper the onset is about 2 MHz.

Assumptions and limits

  • Classical (Fourier) diffusion with constant properties. Phase changes, latent heat, and temperature-dependent properties are not included.
  • For metals at pulse durations below a few picoseconds, electrons and the lattice are not in equilibrium. Heat initially spreads by hot-electron transport, which the two-temperature model describes (Anisimov et al.). The classical \(L_\text{th}\) then underestimates the early heat penetration.
  • The heat is assumed to be deposited at the surface of a thick (semi-infinite) part. The accumulation onset compares single-spot timescales. Scanned processes also depend on overlap, line spacing, and part geometry. Thin sheets and small features accumulate heat faster than a semi-infinite solid.
