Glazing Performance Calculator: U-Factor, SHGC & VT

How well does a window’s glass insulate, how much solar heat does it let in, and how much daylight? Build a single, double, triple, or vacuum glazing unit pane by pane (clear or low-iron glass, low-e coatings on any surface, and air, argon, krypton, xenon, or vacuum gaps) and get its center-of-glass U-factor, solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC), and visible transmittance (VT), calculated the way NFRC rates them, plus reflectance, color, surface temperatures, and spectra. Use it to compare glazing options, see what a coating or a gas fill changes, or evaluate the measured spectra of a specific product.

Key terms
U-factor
The heat that flows through each square meter of glazing for each degree of temperature difference between inside and outside, in W/m²·K (US units: Btu/h·ft²·°F; 1 Btu/h·ft²·°F = 5.678 W/m²·K). Lower insulates better. The R-value is its inverse.
Center of glass
The middle of the glazing, away from the edge seal and the frame. These results are center-of-glass values; the whole-window U-factor on an NFRC label also counts the frame and the edges and comes out higher.
SHGC
Solar heat gain coefficient: the fraction of the sunlight striking the glass that ends up as heat indoors, transmitted directly or absorbed in the panes and released inward. It runs from 0 to 1: low values keep rooms cool, high values help passive solar heating.
Visible transmittance (VT)
The fraction of daylight that passes through, weighted by the eye’s sensitivity. Clear double glazing transmits about 80 %.
Low-e coating
A thin, transparent coating of doped tin oxide or silver that reflects the long-wave infrared radiated by room-temperature surfaces. Its emissivity, from 0 to 1, says how strongly a surface radiates heat: 0.84 for bare glass, about 0.15 for a tin-oxide hard coat, and about 0.015–0.04 for silver coatings.
Surface numbers
Glass surfaces are counted from outdoors: a double unit has surfaces #1 (outdoor face) to #4 (indoor face), so a coating on #2 is on the inner face of the outer pane, inside the sealed gap.
Gas fill
Argon, krypton, or xenon conducts heat less well than air, so a filled gap insulates better. Here a gas fill is 90 % gas and 10 % air.
Vacuum insulating glass (VIG)
Two panes a fraction of a millimeter apart with the air pumped out, held apart by a grid of small pillars and sealed at the edges. Heat crosses only by radiation and through the pillars, so a VIG can insulate about as well as triple glazing in a unit under 1 cm thick, about a third of the thickness of a double-glazed unit.

Glazing system

Height of the glass. It sets the convection in the gaps and on the indoor surface, so U changes slightly with it. LBNL WINDOW reports glazing systems at 1 m, the NFRC default; whole-window ratings use the product’s own height.

    How it is calculated

    The calculator works the way LBNL WINDOW does for a glazing system. It finds the spectral transmittance and reflectance of each pane, combines the panes optically, and then solves the heat balance of every glass surface under the NFRC conditions.

    Light and solar radiation

    Each pane is described by its spectral transmittance \(T(\lambda)\) and its front and back reflectance \(R_f(\lambda)\) and \(R_b(\lambda)\) from 300 to 2500 nm, in 5 nm steps. The panes are combined with the net-radiation (multiple-reflection) method, which follows light bouncing between every pair of surfaces. For two panes,

    \[ \begin{gathered} T = \frac{T_1 T_2}{1 - R_{b,1} R_{f,2}}, \\ R_f = R_{f,1} + \frac{T_1^2 R_{f,2}}{1 - R_{b,1} R_{f,2}}, \end{gathered} \]

    and the same bookkeeping gives the fraction of sunlight absorbed in each pane. The visible and solar values are weighted averages over the spectrum:

    \[ \begin{gathered} \tau_v = \frac{\sum D_{65}(\lambda)\,V(\lambda)\,T(\lambda)}{\sum D_{65}(\lambda)\,V(\lambda)}, \\ \tau_s = \frac{\sum E(\lambda)\,T(\lambda)}{\sum E(\lambda)} \end{gathered} \]

    Here \(D_{65}\) is the CIE daylight illuminant and \(V(\lambda)\) the eye’s daylight (photopic) sensitivity, summed over 380–780 nm. \(E(\lambda)\) is the AM1.5 direct-normal solar spectrum that NFRC 300 specifies (ISO 9845-1:1992, Table 1, column 2, the values of ASTM E891), summed over 300–2500 nm; about half of its energy is visible light. Reflectances are weighted the same way. The UV transmittance is the solar-weighted average from 310 to 380 nm, calculated the way LBNL WINDOW does it. Color is CIELAB under D65 with the CIE 1931 2° observer.

    Heat transfer and U-factor

    The thermal model is the ISO 15099 center-of-glass method, which NFRC 100 prescribes and LBNL WINDOW implements. Every glass surface has its own temperature. The model solves the heat balance of all surfaces together and repeats it with updated, temperature-dependent coefficients until it converges.

    Show the heat-transfer equations: gaps, glass surfaces, and vacuum glazing

    Across each gap, heat flows by radiation between the two facing surfaces,

    \[ h_r = \frac{\sigma\,(T_1^2 + T_2^2)(T_1 + T_2)}{1/\varepsilon_1 + 1/\varepsilon_2 - 1}, \]

    and by conduction and convection of the gas, \(h_c = \mathrm{Nu}\,k/d\), for a gap of width \(d\) and height \(H\). The Nusselt number Nu depends on the Rayleigh number of the gap:

    \[ \begin{gathered} \mathrm{Ra} = \frac{\rho^2 d^3 g\, c_p\, \Delta T}{\mu\, k\, T_m}, \\ \mathrm{Nu} = \max\!\left(\mathrm{Nu}_1,\ 0.242\left(\frac{\mathrm{Ra}\,d}{H}\right)^{0.272}\right) \end{gathered} \]

    \(\mathrm{Nu}_1\) is Wright’s (1996) correlation for vertical window cavities: \(0.0673838\,\mathrm{Ra}^{1/3}\) above \(\mathrm{Ra} = 5\times10^4\), \(0.028154\,\mathrm{Ra}^{0.4134}\) from \(10^4\) to \(5\times10^4\), and \(1 + 1.7596678\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{Ra}^{2.2984755}\) below \(10^4\). The second term, from ElSherbiny, Raithby, and Hollands (1982), matters only in short cavities. At low Ra, Nu approaches 1: the gas only conducts, and \(h_c = k/d\). The gas properties vary linearly with temperature and are taken at the mean gap temperature \(T_m\). The 90 % fills contain 10 % air, mixed by the ISO 15099 rules (Wilke’s rule for viscosity, Mason and Saxena’s for thermal conductivity). Glass conducts with \(k = 1.0\) W/m·K.

    On the outdoor surface, wind convection is \(h_c = 4 + 4v = 26\) W/m²·K at the NFRC wind speed of 5.5 m/s, and the glass radiates to surroundings at the outdoor air temperature. On the indoor surface, natural convection follows the ISO 15099 correlation for a vertical plate as tall as the glazing, \(\mathrm{Nu}_H = 0.56\,\mathrm{Ra}_H^{1/4}\), with a turbulent branch above \(\mathrm{Ra}_H \approx 1.1\times10^{11}\) and air properties at \(T_i + (T_s - T_i)/4\). The glass also exchanges radiation with a room at the indoor air temperature. NFRC 100 sets the winter conditions: −18 °C outside with 5.5 m/s wind, 21 °C inside, and no sun. At the converged temperatures, the layers act as thermal resistances in series:

    \[ \frac{1}{U} = \frac{1}{h_o} + \sum_j \frac{t_j}{k_j} + \sum_k \frac{1}{h_{r,k} + h_{c,k}} + \frac{1}{h_i} \]

    Here \(h_o\) and \(h_i\) are the outdoor and indoor surface coefficients, convection plus radiation. The table under the results lists these resistances for your glazing. The R-value is \(1/U\); in US units, \(U\) in Btu/h·ft²·°F is \(U/5.678263\).

    Vacuum glazing

    A vacuum gap has no gas conduction or convection. Heat crosses it by radiation, with the same \(h_r\), and through the support pillars. Each pillar of radius \(a\) touches the glass on a small circle, and the heat it carries must spread out from that contact into each sheet: a spreading resistance of \(1/(4ka)\) per sheet (Collins et al., 1991). A square array of pillars with spacing \(\lambda\) therefore conducts

    \[ C_p = \frac{2\,k\,a}{\lambda^2} \]

    per unit area of glass with conductivity \(k\) (Collins and Fischer-Cripps, 1991). For example, 0.5 mm pillars 25 mm apart conduct 0.80 W/m²·K. The formula assumes pillars that conduct much better than glass, such as metal ones. A pillar of conductivity \(k_p\) and height \(h\) adds its own resistance, \(C_p = 2ka/[\lambda^2(1 + 2kh/(k_p \pi a))]\), as LBNL WINDOW models it. For a steel pillar 0.2 mm high and 0.5 mm across (\(k_p \approx 16\) W/m·K), that lowers \(C_p\) by about 3 %; for a glass pillar (\(k_p \approx 1\) W/m·K), by about a third.

    Residual gas is neglected. At 0.1 Pa the mean free path of air molecules, about 66 mm (see the mean free path calculator), is far larger than the gap, so the gas conducts as free molecules, \(C_g = \alpha\,\frac{\gamma + 1}{\gamma - 1}\sqrt{\frac{R}{8\pi M T}}\;p\) (Corruccini, 1959), where \(\alpha\) is the combined accommodation coefficient and \(\gamma\) the heat-capacity ratio. With the accommodation coefficients LBNL uses (0.5 at each surface), air at 0.1 Pa adds about 0.04 W/m²·K, ten times less at 0.01 Pa.

    Solar heat gain

    SHGC is the solar transmittance plus the fraction of the absorbed sunlight that flows inward. The calculator solves the heat balance twice under the NFRC 200 summer conditions: 32 °C outside with 2.75 m/s wind (so \(h_c = 15\) W/m²·K) and 24 °C inside. The first solution includes 783 W/m² of sunlight at normal incidence absorbed in the panes, the second has none, and the difference in heat flow into the room is the inward-flowing part:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{SHGC} &= \tau_s + \frac{q_\text{in}(I_s) - q_\text{in}(0)}{I_s} \\ &= \tau_s + \sum_i N_i\,\alpha_{s,i} \end{aligned} \]

    Here \(\alpha_{s,i}\) is the solar absorptance of pane \(i\) and \(N_i\) its inward-flowing fraction. Each pane’s absorbed heat is split equally between its two surfaces. In the default unit, about 7 % of the heat absorbed in the outer pane flows inward, and 84 % of the heat absorbed in the inner pane.

    Generic glass and low-e coatings

    Generic panes use a soda-lime glass model: a refractive index near 1.52 and an absorption spectrum set by iron (Rubin, 1985). Ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) absorbs in a broad band centered near 1050 nm whose tail reaches into the red. That band cuts near-infrared transmission and gives clear glass its blue-green tint, plainest at the edge. Ferric iron (Fe³⁺) absorbs mainly in the ultraviolet (Shelby, 2015). Low-iron glass has less iron, more of it ferric. For 6 mm clear float, the model gives 89 % visible and 80 % solar transmittance, against 88 % and 77 % for LBNL’s generic 5.7 mm clear glass. For 6 mm low-iron glass it gives 91 % and 90 %, against 91 % and 89 % for the 5 mm low-iron glass of the EnergyPlus dataset.

    The four generic coatings stand for the main low-e families: a pyrolytic (hard-coat) doped tin oxide with an emissivity of 0.15, and sputtered single-, double-, and triple-silver stacks with emissivities of 0.04, 0.025, and 0.015. These, like all emissivities on this page, are hemispherical values as NFRC 301 defines them; a European “corrected emissivity” (EN 12898) runs 3–4 % lower for low-e coatings, and the low-e coating calculator gives both from a sheet resistance. LBNL’s documentation gives about 0.15 as typical of hard coats (0.1 or less for newer ones), 0.04 of sputtered coatings, and 0.02 for the best available. More silver layers make a steeper rise in near-infrared reflectance, so less solar heat passes for the same daylight. The emissivity of the coated surface sets the radiation across the gap. Real products vary, so use measured spectra for a specific product.

    Surface numbering. Surfaces are numbered from the outside: a double unit has surfaces #1 (outdoor face) to #4 (indoor face). Moving a coating from #2 to #3 leaves the U-factor unchanged but raises SHGC, because the sunlight the coating absorbs then heats the inner pane, whose heat flows mostly indoors. For the default double-silver unit, SHGC is 0.37 with the coating on #2 and 0.42 on #3. So #3 is the usual choice where solar gain is welcome, and #2 where it is not.

    Worked example

    The default unit: two 6 mm clear panes, the double-silver coating (ε = 0.025) on #2, and a 12.7 mm gap of 90 % argon, 1 m tall. Under NFRC 100 winter conditions the surfaces settle at −16.2, −15.9, 13.1, and 13.5 °C (#1 to #4).

    • Outdoor surface: \(h_o = 26.0 + 3.20 = 29.2\) W/m²·K, so \(R_o = 0.0342\) m²·K/W.
    • Glass: \(t/k = 0.006/1.0 = 0.0060\) m²·K/W per pane.
    • Gap: at the mean gap temperature of −1.4 °C the argon–air mixture conducts \(k = 0.0170\) W/m·K. With 29.0 K across the gap, \(\mathrm{Ra} = 1.04\times10^4\) and \(\mathrm{Nu} = 1.29\), so \(h_c = 1.29 \times 0.0170/0.0127 = 1.72\) W/m²·K. Radiation adds only \(h_r = 0.11\) W/m²·K because of the coating; bare glass would give 3.3 at the same temperatures. \(R_\text{gap} = 1/1.837 = 0.5445\) m²·K/W.
    • Indoor surface: natural convection \(h_c = 2.41\) (\(\mathrm{Ra}_H = 8.0\times10^8\)) plus radiation \(h_r = 4.67\) gives \(h_i = 7.07\) W/m²·K, so \(R_i = 0.1414\) m²·K/W.

    In total, \(R = 0.0342 + 0.0060 + 0.5445 + 0.0060 + 0.1414 = 0.7321\) m²·K/W, so \(U = 1.37\) W/m²·K (0.241 Btu/h·ft²·°F, R-4.16). The gap holds 74 % of the resistance and takes 29 K of the 39 K difference. Under NFRC 200 summer conditions, τs = 0.333, and the panes absorb 27.6 % and 2.1 % of the sunlight. Their inward-flowing heat adds 0.035, so SHGC = 0.368. VT is 73.8 %, and VT/SHGC = 2.0.

    Assumptions and limits

    • Center of glass only. Whole-window ratings add the frame and the edges of the glazing, and the better the glass, the more they matter. In LBNL’s example windows of 1.2 × 1.5 m, a low-e double glazing of 1.65 W/m²·K at the center rated 1.77 in a wood frame and 2.12 in a thermally broken aluminum frame; a triple glazing of 0.60 rated 0.87 and 1.26 (Curcija and Hart, 2018). The edges are also the coldest part of the indoor glass, so condensation starts there.
    • Vacuum glazing. The pillar formula assumes metal pillars on a square grid in glass much thicker than the pillar radius, and residual gas is neglected (see above). The edge seal, often fused solder glass, conducts heat around the vacuum and is not part of the center-of-glass value. In tests of a prototype in a wood casement frame at −18 °C outside and 21 °C inside, the indoor glass at the sightline was at 3.6 °C where the edge sat flush and 8.7 °C where it was recessed 12.7 mm into the sash, against 16.5 °C at the center (Simko et al., 1995). In LBNL’s example windows, a vacuum glazing of 0.52 W/m²·K at the center rated 0.82–0.86 in a wood frame (Curcija and Hart, 2018). EN 673:2024 and ISO 9050:2026 exclude vacuum glazing; EN 673 refers to EN 674 or ISO 19916-1 for its U value.
    • NFRC conditions and spectrum. European Ug values (EN 673) use other standardized boundary conditions and gas data, and g-values (EN 410, ISO 9050) weight solar quantities with a global (direct plus diffuse) spectrum, so they differ from NFRC numbers for the same glass. ASTM G173, which replaced E891 as the reference spectrum, has more visible light: with it, SHGC would come out 0.01–0.03 higher (0.023 for the default unit) and solar transmittance 1.5–2.5 points higher for the silver coatings here.
    • Color. CIELAB here uses the CIE 1931 2° observer. LBNL WINDOW and most glass datasheets quote D65 with the 1964 10° observer (ASTM E308), which can differ by up to about one unit in a* or b* for coated glass.
    • Generic layers. They stand for product families, not products: the glass is within about 3 points of typical solar transmittance, and the coatings show what each family can do. For product selection or code compliance, use measured spectra or NFRC-certified ratings.
    • Imported spectra. Data are resampled to the 5 nm grid by linear interpolation, as NFRC 300 specifies, and the first and last values are held outside the measured range. The emissivities come from the file header and must be hemispherical values (NFRC 301), as IGDB files give them; LBNL WINDOW can recompute them from far-infrared data in the file, which can shift U slightly. For color, CIE 15 recommends higher-order interpolation of data coarser than 5 nm, so a* and b* from such data can be off by a few tenths.
    • Geometry and optics. Layers are specular (non-scattering) and the light arrives at normal incidence, as NFRC rates SHGC and VT. Laminated interlayers, frits, films, and shades are not modeled. The glazing is vertical; tilt changes the convection. Gas fills are ideal mixtures at atmospheric pressure, and the panes stay flat, although real units bow with temperature and pressure (LBNL WINDOW can model that).
    • Gap convection. Wright’s correlation was fitted to cavities more than 40 times taller than wide, with Ra up to 10⁶. In shorter glazings the ElSherbiny term takes over.
    • Dew point. The condensation note uses the Magnus formula for 21 °C room air, \(T_d = b\gamma/(a - \gamma)\) with \(\gamma = \ln(\mathrm{RH}) + aT/(b + T)\), \(a = 17.62\), and \(b = 243.12\) °C. Here it is within 0.02 °C of the IAPWS saturation curve.
    • Validation. With the same layers, the results match LBNL’s Windows-CalcEngine (pyWinCalc 3.3.1) for 27 glazing systems and five LBNL product files: U within 0.01 %, SHGC and solar and visible transmittance within 0.001, and surface temperatures within 0.003 K.
