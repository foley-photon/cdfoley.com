Machine Vision Lens Calculator

Choose a camera sensor and a lens to see what the camera will see: how large an area it covers (the field of view), how small a defect it can resolve, and how much height variation stays sharp (the depth of field). The page also shows whether the lens or the pixels limit the finest detail, and the lower panel finds the focal length you need for a given field of view and distance.

Key terms
Field of view (FOV)
The width and height of the area on the part that one image covers. Spreading the same pixels over a larger field makes each pixel cover more of the part.
Object distance
How far the part is from the lens (the vision system configurator calls it the working distance). Here it is measured from the lens’s front principal plane, an optical reference point, so it differs somewhat from the working distance that lens makers quote from the front of the lens barrel.
Focal length
The lens’s main specification, printed on the barrel (25 mm, for example). From the same distance, a longer focal length sees a smaller area in more detail.
Magnification
The size of the image on the sensor divided by the true size on the part. Most machine vision setups work well below 1; the default setup here is 0.14.
Pixel pitch
The spacing between neighboring pixels on the sensor, listed in the camera datasheet as the pixel size, in micrometers (µm).
f-number
The aperture setting on the lens, written f/2.8, f/8, and so on. A larger number means a smaller opening: more depth of field, but less light and more blur from diffraction.
Depth of field
How far the part can move toward or away from the camera and still look sharp. “Sharp” means the blur stays within the acceptable blur you set, in pixels.
MTF at Nyquist
The share of contrast the lens keeps on the finest pattern the pixels can record (one dark and one light line per two pixels). Near 0 % that detail is lost, however many pixels it spans.

How it is calculated

The lens is modeled as a thin lens. For an object at distance \(s\) from the front principal plane of a lens of focal length \(f\), the magnification (image size over object size) and the field of view are

\[ \begin{gathered} m = \frac{f}{s - f}, \quad \text{FOV} = \frac{\text{sensor size}}{m}, \\[6pt] \text{pixel on object} = \frac{p}{m}, \end{gathered} \]

where \(p\) is the pixel pitch. To resolve a defect reliably it should span several pixels (typically 3–5), so the smallest detectable defect is that many object-side pixels.

Depth of field

The depth of field is the range of object distances over which the geometric blur at the sensor stays under the acceptable circle of confusion \(c\) (a diameter, set here as a number of pixels). For a thin lens with the aperture stop at the lens, the near and far limits follow exactly from the hyperfocal distance \(H = f^2/(Nc) + f\):

\[ \begin{gathered} s_\text{near} = \frac{s(H - f)}{H + s - 2f}, \\[8pt] s_\text{far} = \frac{s(H - f)}{H - s}, \\[8pt] \text{DOF} = s_\text{far} - s_\text{near}. \end{gathered} \]

For close-up work (\(s \ll H\)) this reduces to the familiar approximation

\[ \mathrm{DOF} \approx \frac{2\,N\,c\,(1 + m)}{m^2}. \]

A common mistake. The expression \(2Nc(1+m)\) is the image-side depth of focus: how far the sensor can move. Object-side depth of field carries the extra factor \(1/m^2\). At m = 0.14 the two differ by a factor of about 50. Using the wrong one makes a lens look far less forgiving than it is.

Closing the aperture (larger N) increases the depth of field in proportion to N in this approximation, but it also enlarges the diffraction spot in proportion to N and cuts the light on the sensor in proportion to \(1/N^2\): each full stop (N × √2) halves it. That trade-off is the central compromise in machine vision optics.

Diffraction vs. pixel size

At the sensor, light from a point forms an Airy disk whose diameter to the first dark ring is \(2.44\,\lambda N_w\), where \(N_w = N(1+m)\) is the working f-number (for a lens with equal entrance and exit pupils). In incoherent light, a diffraction-limited lens with a circular aperture transmits no detail finer than the cutoff frequency \(1/(\lambda N_w)\), in cycles per millimeter when \(\lambda\) is in millimeters. The sensor can only represent detail up to its Nyquist frequency \(1/(2p)\), one cycle per two pixels.

The ratio alone is not the whole story. An ideal lens’s MTF falls continuously to zero at the cutoff,

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathrm{MTF}(\nu) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\bigl[\arccos\nu - \nu\sqrt{1-\nu^2}\bigr] \\[6pt] \nu = \frac{f_\text{Nyquist}}{f_\text{cutoff}}, \end{gathered} \]

so a ratio of exactly 1 still means zero contrast at Nyquist. A ratio of 1.2 gives about 8 %, and 1.5 gives about 22 %.

  • Cutoff ÷ Nyquist ≥ 2: sensor-limited. The optics keep useful contrast (39 % or more) at the pixel Nyquist frequency. Smaller pixels or more magnification would help, and fine periodic patterns can alias.
  • Between 1 and 2: balanced. Both the optics and the pixels limit resolution. Contrast at Nyquist is low, so small defects lose contrast before they lose pixels.
  • Below 1: diffraction-limited. The pixels oversample the blur. Opening the aperture (smaller N), if depth of field allows, adds real resolution. Adding pixels does not.

Real lenses fall short of the diffraction limit, most of all at wide apertures, where aberrations dominate, so these MTF values are upper bounds. Check the lens maker’s MTF curve at your sensor’s Nyquist frequency.

Worked example

A 2/3″ IMX250 camera (3.45 µm pixels, 2448 × 2048) with a 25 mm lens at f/2.8, 200 mm from the part:

  • m = 25/175 = 0.1429, so the 8.446 × 7.066 mm sensor sees a 59.12 × 49.46 mm field. Each pixel covers 24.15 µm, so a 3-pixel defect is 72.5 µm.
  • With c = 2 px (6.9 µm), H = 32.37 m and the part stays in focus from 198.9 to 201.1 mm: a depth of field of 2.16 mm. The image-side formula would wrongly give 0.044 mm.
  • Nw = 3.2, so the Airy disk at 550 nm is 4.29 µm, 1.24 pixels. The cutoff-to-Nyquist ratio is 3.92 (ideal-lens MTF at Nyquist 68 %), so this setup is sensor-limited. To find smaller defects, increase magnification or use smaller pixels. Do not stop down.

For a 100 mm field at 300 mm with the same sensor, the lower panel gives f = 23.4 mm. A standard 16 mm lens gives a 150 mm field. A 25 mm lens needs the part about 321 mm away.

Assumptions and limits

  • Thin-lens, paraxial model with a pupil magnification of 1. Real multi-element lenses have separated principal planes and asymmetric pupils, so treat distances as approximate and confirm with the lens vendor’s calculator or a test image.
  • Every lens has a minimum object distance, listed in its datasheet. Closer work needs a spacer ring (extension tube) or a macro lens. The “extension” result is the extra lens-to-sensor distance needed beyond infinity focus.
  • Telecentric lenses do not follow \(m = f/(s-f)\). Their magnification is fixed and their depth of field is set by the object-side NA.
  • Depth of field is geometric and ignores diffraction. When the Airy disk is comparable to \(c\) or larger, the usable depth is smaller than shown.
  • Pixel figures assume a monochrome sensor. In a color camera’s Bayer filter, green covers every other pixel and red and blue each cover every second row and column, so fine color detail is sampled at about half the monochrome pixel density in each direction. Allow up to about twice as many pixels across a defect, or use a monochrome camera.
  • Detectability also depends on contrast, lighting, noise, and the algorithm. The pixel-count rule is a starting point, not a guarantee.
