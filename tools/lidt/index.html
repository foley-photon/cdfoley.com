LIDT Calculator: Laser Damage Threshold Scaling

Will this optic survive my laser? A datasheet gives an optic’s laser-induced damage threshold for one test laser. This tool scales that value to your pulse duration and wavelength, compares it with the fluence (or, for a CW laser, the linear power density) your beam delivers, and reports the safety margin.

Key terms
LIDT
Laser-induced damage threshold: the highest fluence (pulsed lasers) or power density (CW lasers) at which the optic is expected not to be damaged. It is found by testing many spots and extrapolating to zero chance of damage, and it holds only for the test’s wavelength, pulse duration, and beam size.
Fluence
Pulse energy per unit area, in J/cm². A Gaussian beam’s peak fluence, on its axis, is twice the pulse energy divided by the 1/e² beam area.
Peak or average
ISO 21254 and Thorlabs compare peak fluence. Some datasheets divide the energy by the 1/e² area instead, which gives half the peak value. Mixing the two halves or doubles your margin.
Linear power density
For CW lasers: power divided by the beam diameter, in W/cm. A CW beam damages by heating, which depends on power per beam width, so a beam twice as wide tolerates twice the power.
1/e² beam diameter
The diameter at which the intensity has fallen to 13.5 % of its peak. It contains 86.5 % of a Gaussian beam’s power.
1-on-1 and S-on-1
Test methods: each test spot gets one pulse (1-on-1) or a train of S pulses (S-on-1). Many pulses usually cause damage at a lower fluence.
Scaling rule
A rule of thumb that estimates the threshold at another pulse duration or wavelength. Vendors use different rules, and data measured at your conditions are always better.
Margin and safety factor
The margin is the scaled LIDT divided by what your beam delivers. The safety factor is the margin you require, commonly 2 to 3.

How LIDT is specified

Damage thresholds are measured by exposing many sites on a sample at a range of fluences, recording which sites are damaged, and extrapolating the damage probability to zero. ISO 21254-1 defines the threshold as the highest fluence (or power density) “for which the extrapolated probability of damage is zero.” In a 1-on-1 test each site sees a single pulse. In an S-on-1 test each site sees a train of S pulses, which usually gives a lower threshold (ISO 21254-2). Some values are certification levels instead: the optic survived that level, and its true threshold may be higher. Thorlabs marks the CW values of its broadband dielectric mirrors this way.

ISO 21254-1 defines the effective area of a beam as the pulse energy divided by the peak fluence. For a Gaussian beam of 1/e² radius \(w\) that area is \(\pi w^2/2\), so a threshold quoted per ISO 21254 is a peak (on-axis) fluence. Thorlabs likewise compares its values with a beam’s maximum fluence, twice the energy divided by the 1/e² area. Some datasheets instead divide the energy by the 1/e² area, giving a number half as large. When the convention is unclear, treat the specification as a peak value. That is the conservative choice.

How it is calculated

For a pulsed beam with pulse energy \(E_p\) and 1/e² radius \(w\) at the optic, the operating fluence and the scaled threshold are

\[ \begin{gathered} F_\text{pk} = \frac{2E_p}{\pi w^2}, \qquad F_\text{avg} = \frac{E_p}{\pi w^2}, \\[4pt] F_\text{LIDT} = F_\text{spec}\,\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{\tau_\text{spec}}}\;f_\lambda , \end{gathered} \]

where the wavelength factor \(f_\lambda\) is \(\sqrt{\lambda/\lambda_\text{spec}}\) (√λ rule), \(\lambda/\lambda_\text{spec}\) (linear rule), or the smaller of the two (conservative). The margin is \(F_\text{LIDT}\) divided by the operating fluence in the same convention as the specification, and the design limit is \(F_\text{LIDT}\) divided by the safety factor. The peak irradiance shown for reference is \(2(E_p/\tau)/(\pi w^2)\), which treats the pulse as flat-topped in time; a Gaussian pulse of FWHM \(\tau\) peaks at \(0.94\,E_p/\tau\). For CW beams the calculator compares linear power densities, as described below.

Scaling with pulse duration

With nanosecond pulses, damage in dielectric coatings and substrates usually starts at absorbing defects and inclusions, which heat up (Bloembergen 1973). While the pulse lasts, the heat spreads over a depth that grows as \(\sqrt{D\tau}\), where \(D\) is the thermal diffusivity (see the thermal diffusion tool). The fluence needed to reach a critical temperature therefore grows as

\[ F_\text{LIDT}(\tau) \approx F_\text{spec}\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{\tau_\text{spec}}}. \]

This is a central estimate, not a law: measured exponents range from about 0.3 to 0.6, depending on the material and on what was measured (Edmund Optics). Vendors give ranges for it. Thorlabs and Edmund Optics apply it from 1 to 100 ns, and Sill Optics from about 30 ps to many milliseconds. Edmund also limits its calculator to a factor of 3 in pulse duration, and this page warns beyond three decades.

The rule fails for ultrashort pulses. Stuart et al. found the \(\sqrt{\tau}\) dependence in fused silica and calcium fluoride for pulses longer than about 50–100 ps. Below about 10 ps, damage is no longer thermal: multiphoton and avalanche ionization create a plasma that ablates the surface, and the threshold falls much more slowly than \(\sqrt{\tau}\) (Stuart et al. 1995, 1996; Du et al. 1994). Thorlabs’ own data show the size of the error. A broadband mirror coating rated 1 J/cm² at 810 nm with 10 ns pulses is rated 0.205 J/cm² at 800 nm with 99 fs pulses, while \(\sqrt{\tau}\) scaling from the nanosecond value predicts 0.003 J/cm², more than 60 times too low. Do not scale a nanosecond specification into the femtosecond regime. Ask the vendor for ultrafast data instead.

Scaling with wavelength

Thresholds usually fall at shorter wavelengths. Each photon carries more energy, so fewer photons are needed to lift an electron across a coating material’s band gap, directly or through defect states, and absorption rises. Carr et al. saw the threshold of DKDP crystals change in steps at the photon energies where the number of photons needed changes. A coating’s standing-wave field pattern is also designed for one wavelength. There is no general physical law, only vendor rules of thumb. Thorlabs scales pulsed thresholds with \(\sqrt{\lambda}\). Sill Optics (“half the wavelength results in the half LIDT value”) and Edmund Optics scale linearly with \(\lambda\), and Edmund applies its rule only within about ±5 % of the test wavelength. Moving to a shorter wavelength, the linear rule predicts the lower threshold. Moving to a longer one, the square-root rule does. The conservative option uses whichever is lower. Coatings are designed for specific wavelengths, so measured data at your wavelength always beats scaling: the typical values below include a coating rated 1 J/cm² at 810 nm but 0.5 J/cm² at 1064 nm (with different test beams), the opposite of what both rules predict.

CW lasers: linear power density

For CW and long-pulse lasers, damage is thermal and set by the steady-state temperature rise. For a round beam absorbed at the surface of a thick substrate, the peak rise equals the absorbed irradiance integrated along a line through the beam center, a quantity in W/cm, divided by twice the thermal conductivity \(k\). For a Gaussian beam of 1/e² radius \(w\) this gives \(\Delta T_\text{max} = P_\text{abs}/(\sqrt{2\pi}\,k\,w)\) (Lax 1977): the rise scales with power divided by beam size, not area. CW thresholds are therefore quoted as a linear power density in W/cm (ISO 21254-1), which Thorlabs computes as the power divided by the 1/e² beam diameter. Thorlabs compares the specification with the beam’s maximum linear power density, which it takes as twice \(P/d\) for a Gaussian beam. The line integral through the center of a Gaussian beam is \(\sqrt{2/\pi}\,P/w\), about \(1.6\,P/d\), so \(2P/d\) is conservative. The calculator uses the same factor and Thorlabs’ linear wavelength rule:

\[ \text{LPD}_\text{max} \approx \frac{2P}{d_{1/e^2}}\quad[\text{W/cm}], \qquad \text{LPD}_\text{LIDT}(\lambda) \approx \text{LPD}_\text{spec}\,\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_\text{spec}}. \]

So a beam twice as large tolerates twice the power, not four times as much. Thorlabs calls the wavelength rule a general trend only: CW damage follows absorption in the coating and substrate, which need not scale with wavelength. ISO 21254-1 treats a pulse as “long,” and so judged like CW, when the heat spreads during the pulse over a distance \(\sqrt{2D\tau}\) comparable to the test spot diameter. Peak irradiance (W/cm²) is shown for reference only. It is not the right figure of merit for CW damage.

Beam size, safety factors, and S-on-1

  • Beam size. Most damage starts at sparse defects. A small test beam may sample no defect at all, while a large beam almost certainly hits several (Porteus and Seitel 1984). A threshold measured with a small beam can therefore be optimistic for a much larger one. Thorlabs measures with beams under 1 mm and notes that for beams larger than about 5 mm the threshold in J/cm² starts to depend on beam size. There is no universal scaling law, so add margin when your beam area is much larger than the test beam’s.
  • S-on-1 and incubation. Under millions of pulses, thresholds can fall well below 1-on-1 values. Use S-on-1 data for production systems.
  • Contamination. Dust, fingerprints, and outgassed films absorb strongly and can reduce the effective threshold by a large factor. Keep optics clean, and purge enclosures for UV and high-power IR.
  • Safety factor. Operate at no more than 1/2 to 1/3 of the scaled threshold. Sill Optics, for example, recommends staying below 50 % of the LIDT.

Worked example

A mirror is rated 10 J/cm² (peak) at 1064 nm, 10 ns. You want to use it at 532 nm with 20 ns, 5 mJ pulses in a 1.0 mm beam. The pulse-duration factor is \(\sqrt{2} = 1.414\). For wavelength, the conservative rule takes the linear factor of 0.5 (the √λ rule would give 0.707). The scaled LIDT is \(10 \times 1.414 \times 0.5 = 7.07\) J/cm². The operating peak fluence is \(2 \times 5\ \text{mJ}/(\pi \times 0.05^2\ \text{cm}^2) = 1.27\) J/cm², so the margin is 5.6×. That is comfortably inside a 2× safety factor.

Assumptions and limits

  • The scaling laws are empirical. They estimate thresholds for comparing options and for specifying optics, and they do not guarantee survival.
  • Each pulse is treated on its own. Pulses of 100 ns or longer can also cause CW-like thermal damage, so check the CW rating as well. At high repetition rates, heat from successive pulses can build up, so compare both the peak and the average power with the CW rating (Thorlabs); the thermal diffusion tool estimates when that starts.
  • Normal incidence is assumed. At oblique incidence, the fluence on the surface falls by cos θ, and ISO 21254 includes that factor in the effective area, but coating damage also depends on polarization and the field distribution.
  • The beam diameter is the 1/e² diameter at the optic, not at focus. Hot spots, diffraction rings, and M² > 1 can raise local fluence above the Gaussian estimate.
