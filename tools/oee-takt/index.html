OEE & Takt Time Calculator

Overall equipment effectiveness (OEE) is the share of planned production time that a machine or line spends making good parts at full speed; the rest is lost to stops, slow running, and rejects. Takt time is the pace a line has to hold to meet customer demand. Enter one shift’s numbers to see where the time went and whether each station of a line keeps up with takt.

Key terms
OEE
Overall equipment effectiveness: the share of planned production time spent making good parts at the ideal speed. It multiplies availability, performance, and quality, so three factors of 90 % each give only 73 %.
Planned production time
The time the equipment was scheduled to produce: the shift minus breaks, meetings, and scheduled maintenance. Every stop inside it counts as a loss.
Downtime
Time the equipment was stopped when it should have been running: breakdowns, changeovers and setups, adjustments, and waiting for material. It lowers availability.
Ideal cycle time
The shortest time per part the process is designed or proven to sustain. Running slower than this, or stopping briefly, lowers performance.
First-pass quality
The share of parts that were good the first time. Scrap and parts that needed rework count as losses.
Six big losses
The classic causes from Total Productive Maintenance (TPM): equipment failures and setups (availability), minor stops and reduced speed (performance), and defects and start-up rejects (quality).
Takt time
Available working time divided by customer demand: one unit must be finished every takt to keep up. It comes from the customer, not from the equipment; a line that must make 840 units in 450 minutes has a takt of 32 seconds.
Station cycle time
The time a station needs for one unit. The slowest station sets the line’s maximum output, and a well-balanced line loads every station close to, but below, takt.

OEE inputs

The time the equipment was scheduled to produce: shift length minus breaks, meetings, scheduled maintenance, and any time with nothing scheduled. An 8-hour shift with 30 minutes of breaks gives 450 min.

Every stop during planned production time: breakdowns, changeovers and setups, adjustments, waiting for material or an operator. Log stops longer than a set threshold, often 2 to 5 minutes; shorter ones show up as performance loss.

The shortest time per part the process can sustain: the design (nameplate) rate or the best rate proven over a long run. Not the average, and not a standard time with allowances built in.

parts

Every part made in the period, good or bad, including scrap and parts sent for rework.

parts

Parts that passed the first time, without rework.

    How OEE is calculated

    OEE compares the good parts actually made with the most that could have been made at the ideal rate during planned production time. Seiichi Nakajima, who made it the yardstick of Total Productive Maintenance (TPM), built it from three factors, each covering a different kind of loss:

    \[ \begin{gathered} A = \frac{\text{run time}}{\text{planned production time}} \\[4pt] P = \frac{\text{ideal cycle time} \times \text{total count}}{\text{run time}} \\[4pt] Q = \frac{\text{good count}}{\text{total count}} \end{gathered} \]
    \[ \begin{gathered} \text{OEE} = A \times P \times Q \\[4pt] {}= \frac{\text{good count} \times \text{ideal cycle time}}{\text{planned production time}} \end{gathered} \]

    Run time is planned production time minus downtime. The second form is a useful check: OEE is the fraction of planned time spent making good parts at full speed. The chart above follows the time down this chain. Downtime turns planned production time into run time. Slow cycles and short stops turn run time into net run time, the time the parts made would have taken at the ideal rate (ideal cycle time × total count). Rejects leave the fully productive time (ideal cycle time × good count). Each factor is the ratio of one bar to the bar above it.

    The six big losses

    Each factor covers two of the six big losses that Nakajima named as the targets of TPM:

    • Availability (downtime losses): equipment failures, and setup and adjustment, including changeovers.
    • Performance (speed losses): idling and minor stoppages, and reduced speed below the design rate.
    • Quality (defect losses): process defects, including parts that must be reworked, and reduced yield between start-up and stable production.

    The calculator takes one downtime figure and one count of rejects, so the chart shows the three pairs. To see all six, log breakdowns separately from changeovers, short stops separately from slow cycles, and start-up rejects separately from the rest. The three factors do not change.

    Is 85 % world class?

    The 85 % figure comes from Nakajima’s Introduction to TPM (p. 28). “Based on our experience,” he gave as ideal conditions an availability above 90 %, a performance efficiency above 95 %, and a rate of quality products above 99 %, and multiplied them to “85+%” (the product is 84.6 %). He added that all the companies that had won the PM Prize, the Japanese award for outstanding TPM plants, had an overall equipment effectiveness above 85 %, so the figure was “not just a remote goal.”

    What the source does not say matters as much. Nakajima calls these ideal conditions, not a world-class standard; his quality figure is 99 %, not the 99.9 % often quoted with it; and he gives no data beyond the remark about the prize winners. The figure describes Japanese TPM plants of the 1970s and 1980s, measured with his definitions. He also warned that companies calculate equipment effectiveness in widely different ways (p. 21), so 85 % in one plant need not mean what it means in another. Reported values for ordinary plants are much lower:

    Definitions that differ

    The formulas above follow Nakajima. Standards, software, and plants differ in what counts as planned time, which stops count against availability, and which cycle time sets the speed. These choices can move OEE by many percentage points, so state them whenever you report or compare a figure.

    • Planned time. Nakajima removes scheduled maintenance and meetings from the time base (pp. 22–23), and ISO 22400-2 removes planned downtime. Vorne’s widely used OEE.com guide instead lists planned maintenance under setup and adjustments, an availability loss. Either is defensible; mixing them is not.
    • Changeovers. Nakajima and ISO 22400-2 count setups and changeovers as downtime. A plant that schedules changeovers as planned stops reports a higher availability for the same day.
    • Ideal or planned cycle time. ISO 22400-2 measures speed against the planned run time per item: its effectiveness is planned run time per item × produced quantity ÷ actual production time. If the plan carries allowances, effectiveness can pass 100 % and hide speed losses that Nakajima’s ideal cycle time would show. Schiraldi and Varisco found that the standard’s two OEE formulations diverge from each other and from Nakajima’s, and proposed equipment-state definitions that reconcile them.
    • Utilization and TEEP. Dividing the fully productive time by calendar time gives TEEP = OEE × utilization, where utilization is planned production time ÷ calendar time. It shows how much capacity is left in hours that are not scheduled.
    • Semiconductor fabs. SEMI E79 builds overall equipment efficiency on the six equipment states of SEMI E10: productive, standby, engineering, scheduled downtime, unscheduled downtime, and non-scheduled time. Availability is equipment uptime ÷ total time, and the speed factor is split into an operational efficiency (productive time ÷ uptime, so a tool idle for lack of wafers counts as a loss) and a rate efficiency. Measured against total time, wafer-fab OEEs are typically below 50 %. De Ron and Rooda proposed an equipment effectiveness that leaves out time the tool waits for work, to separate the equipment’s own losses from those of the factory around it.

    Takt time and line balancing

    \[ \begin{gathered} \text{takt} = \frac{\text{available working time}}{\text{customer demand}} \\[4pt] N_\text{min} = \left\lceil \frac{\sum t_i}{\text{takt}} \right\rceil \\[4pt] \eta_\text{balance} = \frac{\sum t_i}{N \cdot t_\text{max}} \end{gathered} \]

    Takt time is the pace of demand. It is set by the customer, not by the equipment, so the available time excludes planned breaks but not changeovers or breakdowns: Rother’s rule is to leave losses out of takt, so that it shows the real demand. Cycle time is how long a station actually takes per unit. Every station must finish within takt, and in practice within a planned cycle time somewhat shorter than takt that leaves room for changeovers, short stops, and scrap; otherwise the line cannot meet demand without overtime. Rother suggests starting with a planned cycle time about 15 % shorter than takt, and keeping automatic machine cycles below about 90 % of it. A line timed exactly to takt that runs at the default example’s OEE of 79 % would deliver only 79 % of the demand.

    The slowest station, \(t_\text{max}\), sets the line’s maximum output: one unit every \(t_\text{max}\) at best. The minimum number of stations \(N_\text{min}\) is a lower bound that ignores the order of the tasks and the fact that a task cannot be split between stations, so a real line can need more. Line-balancing texts write the line efficiency as \(\sum t_i/(N c)\) for a cycle time \(c\). With \(c\) equal to takt, it measures how much of the stations’ time at the pace of demand is work (efficiency vs. takt, above). With \(c = t_\text{max}\), the pace the line can actually hold, it measures how evenly the work is spread (balance efficiency). Its complement, the balance delay, is idle time built into the line. The station chart above is an operator balance chart; the Lean Enterprise Institute recommends loading each operator to very nearly, but slightly less than, takt.

    Worked examples

    OEE. A 450-minute planned shift loses 45 minutes to downtime, leaving 405 minutes of run time (A = 90.0 %). At an ideal 30 s per part, 740 parts should take 370 minutes, so P = 370/405 = 91.4 %. 715 of those parts are good (Q = 96.6 %). OEE = 0.900 × 0.9136 × 0.9662 = 79.4 %, the same as 715 good parts out of a theoretical 900. The chart shows that the 45 minutes of downtime are the largest loss, ahead of 35 minutes of speed loss and 12.5 minutes spent making parts that were rejected.

    Takt. Demand of 840 units in 450 available minutes gives a takt of 32.1 s. Six stations with 173.5 s of total work need at least ⌈173.5/32.1⌉ = 6 stations, so the station count is right. But station 4, at 33.5 s, is 1.4 s over takt and limits the line to about 806 units per shift. Moving 1.5 s of work from station 4 to station 3 (24.0 s) brings every station under takt without adding labor, but leaves station 4 only 0.1 s of margin. Moving 3 s instead, if that work can be done earlier in the sequence, gives 27.0 and 30.5 s: station 2, at 31.0 s, becomes the slowest, every station has at least 1.1 s of margin, and the balance efficiency rises from 86.3 to 93.3 %. A planned cycle time 15 % shorter than takt, 27.3 s, would need at least ⌈173.5/27.3⌉ = 7 stations.

    Assumptions and limits

    • The ideal cycle time decides the answer. If it is set to the average actual rate, or to a standard time with allowances, performance looks perfect and speed losses disappear. A performance above 100 % means the ideal cycle time entered is longer than the process really takes, or the count is wrong.
    • State the definitions. What counts as planned time, downtime, and a good part changes the result; see definitions that differ.
    • Count rework as a loss. Quality should use first-pass good parts, as Nakajima’s process-defect loss does: parts that pass after rework still consumed run time.
    • Station times vary. The calculator treats each station time as fixed. A station that averages just under takt will still miss it on some cycles, and without buffers its neighbors then wait, so real output stays below one unit per \(t_\text{max}\). Plan station loads with margin for variation, walking, and handling, and confirm them with time studies.
