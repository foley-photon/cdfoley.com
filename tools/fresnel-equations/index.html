Fresnel Equations Calculator

How much light reflects from, and passes through, a bare (uncoated) surface between two transparent materials, such as air and glass, at any angle and for each polarization? Use it to estimate losses at uncoated windows and viewports, ghost reflections, Brewster windows, and total internal reflection in prisms.

Key terms
Refractive index, n
How much a material slows light. About 1.0003 for air, 1.33 for water, 1.45–1.52 for common optical glasses, and 4.0 for germanium in the infrared. It changes with wavelength.
Angle of incidence
The angle between the incoming beam and the surface normal, a line perpendicular to the surface. 0° is straight on.
s and p polarization
Light whose electric field oscillates parallel to the surface (s) or in the plane that contains the beam and the normal (p). Unpolarized light is an equal mix of the two.
Reflectance, R
The fraction of the power that is reflected: about 4 % per surface for glass in air at normal incidence.
Brewster angle
The angle at which p-polarized light is not reflected at all, about 57° for glass in air.
Total internal reflection
Going from a higher to a lower index, such as glass to air, light beyond the critical angle (about 41° for glass) is reflected completely.
Uncoated window
A plate with two surfaces. It loses roughly twice the single-surface reflectance; an anti-reflection coating reduces that.

Inputs

The material the light arrives from: 1 for vacuum or air, or the glass index for light leaving a prism or window.

Preset indices are at the stated wavelength and near room temperature. Index varies with wavelength, so use your material’s value at your wavelength (refractiveindex.info lists most materials).

Measured from the surface normal, 0–90°.

How it is calculated

Snell’s law gives the refraction angle, \(n_1\sin\theta_i = n_2\sin\theta_t\). The Fresnel amplitude reflection coefficients for s (TE, perpendicular to the plane of incidence) and p (TM, parallel) polarization are

\[ \begin{gathered} r_s = \frac{n_1\cos\theta_i - n_2\cos\theta_t}{n_1\cos\theta_i + n_2\cos\theta_t}, \\[10pt] r_p = \frac{n_2\cos\theta_i - n_1\cos\theta_t}{n_2\cos\theta_i + n_1\cos\theta_t}, \end{gathered} \]

with power reflectances \(R_s = |r_s|^2\), \(R_p = |r_p|^2\), and \(R = (R_s+R_p)/2\) for unpolarized light. For lossless media \(T = 1 - R\) in each polarization. (Sign conventions for \(r_p\) differ between textbooks, but the reflectances do not.)

\[ \begin{gathered} R_0 = \left(\frac{n_1-n_2}{n_1+n_2}\right)^2, \\[10pt] \theta_B = \arctan\frac{n_2}{n_1}, \\[10pt] \theta_c = \arcsin\frac{n_2}{n_1}\;\;(n_1>n_2). \end{gathered} \]

At the Brewster angle \(R_p = 0\): p-polarized light passes with no reflection loss, and the reflected beam is purely s-polarized. Laser tubes, Brewster windows, and polarizing plates use this. Above the critical angle, going from high to low index, no wave propagates into the second medium, and \(R = 1\) (total internal reflection). Only an evanescent field enters it, with an amplitude that falls by 1/e over \(\lambda_0 / \bigl(2\pi\sqrt{n_1^2\sin^2\theta_i - n_2^2}\bigr)\): 0.41 vacuum wavelengths at 45° in BK7, and longer close to the critical angle. A second surface that close lets some light tunnel across (frustrated total internal reflection), which this tool does not model.

An uncoated window has two surfaces. Summing the incoherent multiple reflections between them gives a transmission of \((1-R)/(1+R)\) per polarization; at normal incidence in air that is \(2n/(n^2+1)\), the “reflection factor” glass makers quote for uncoated plates. That is higher than \((1-R)^2\), the estimate that ignores multiple reflections: barely for glass (91.9 % instead of 91.7 % for N-BK7) but noticeably for high-index materials (47.0 % instead of 40.9 % for germanium).

Worked example

The default inputs: light in air (\(n_1 = 1\)) hits N-BK7 (\(n_2 = 1.5168\)) at 45°. Snell’s law gives \(\theta_t = 27.79°\). Then \(R_s = 9.60\) %, \(R_p = 0.92\) %, and unpolarized \(R = 5.26\) %, compared with 4.22 % at normal incidence. The Brewster angle is 56.60°. Going the other way, from BK7 into air, light is totally internally reflected beyond \(\theta_c = 41.25°\). That is why a 45° BK7 right-angle prism works as a mirror.

Assumptions and limits

  • Non-absorbing, isotropic media. Metals and strongly absorbing materials need a complex index \(\tilde n = n + ik\), which this tool does not support. Birefringent crystals (sapphire, quartz, calcite) have two indices, depending on the polarization relative to the crystal axis; the sapphire preset is the ordinary-ray index.
  • Single, uncoated, smooth interface. Coatings, thin films, and surface roughness change the reflectance completely. Use the coating vendor’s curves for coated optics.
  • Window transmission assumes incoherent addition: a thick window, or a beam with short coherence length. Thin plates with coherent light show etalon fringes: the transmission swings between \(\big((1-R)/(1+R)\big)^2\) and 1 and averages to the incoherent value. At normal incidence that is 84.5 % to 100 % for N-BK7, but 22.1 % to 100 % for germanium.
