Stopping Power & Range Calculator

How much energy a proton, alpha particle, or electron loses per millimeter as it passes through a material, and how far it gets before it stops. The numbers come from NIST’s reference tables (PSTAR, ASTAR, and ESTAR), interpolated to your energy, for 35 commonly used materials (32 of them for protons and alphas). The calculator also gives the energy left after a foil, window, or degrader of any thickness, the dose a beam deposits, and the stopping profile along the track (the Bragg peak).

Key terms
Stopping power
The energy a charged particle loses per unit path length, dE/dx. Ions lose most of their energy to the material’s electrons, so stopping power rises as they slow down, which is why protons deposit the most energy just before they stop.
Mass stopping power
Stopping power divided by the material’s density, in MeV·cm²/g. It depends little on density, so one value serves a gas at any pressure or a material of any packing; multiply by the density to get MeV per centimeter.
Electronic and nuclear stopping
Energy lost to the material’s electrons (ionization and excitation) and to whole atoms (elastic collisions with nuclei). Nuclear stopping matters only for slow, heavy ions, below roughly 10 keV for protons.
Collision and radiative stopping
For electrons: energy lost to atomic electrons, and energy carried away by bremsstrahlung x-rays. Radiative loss grows with energy and atomic number; in lead it equals collision loss near 10 MeV.
CSDA range
The path length a particle travels before stopping, if it lost energy smoothly at the average rate (the continuous slowing-down approximation). It is given as areal density (g/cm²), which divided by density gives a length.
Projected range
How deep along its original direction an ion gets on average. Scattering makes the path zigzag, so the projected range is shorter than the CSDA path length, most at low energy. For electrons, which scatter far more, the depth they reach is much less than the CSDA range.
Absorbed dose
Energy deposited per unit mass, in gray (1 Gy = 1 J/kg). For a beam crossing a thin layer it is the fluence (particles per cm²) times the mass stopping power.

How it is calculated

NIST tabulates, for each material on a fixed grid of energies, the mass stopping power split into its parts and the CSDA range (plus, for ions, the projected range). The calculator interpolates linearly in log(energy) and log(value) between neighboring grid points. Lengths come from the material’s density \(\rho\):

\[ \begin{gathered} S = \rho \left(\frac{S}{\rho}\right), \qquad \frac{S}{\rho} = \frac{S_\text{el}}{\rho} + \frac{S_\text{nuc}}{\rho}\ \ \text{(ions)}, \quad \frac{S_\text{col}}{\rho} + \frac{S_\text{rad}}{\rho}\ \ \text{(electrons)}, \\[8pt] R_\text{CSDA} = \int_0^{E} \frac{dE'}{S(E')}, \qquad \text{length} = \frac{R\ [\text{g/cm}^2]}{\rho\ [\text{g/cm}^3]}. \end{gathered} \]

Behind the tables, NIST computes electronic stopping of fast ions from Bethe’s theory with shell, Barkas, and Bloch corrections and the density effect, and at low energies fits experimental data; electron collision stopping uses the Bethe theory of ICRU Report 37 and radiative stopping the bremsstrahlung cross sections of Seltzer and Berger. The key material constant is the mean excitation energy \(I\) of the Bethe formula: 75 eV for water, 85.7 eV for dry air, and 173 eV for silicon in these tables.

Energy after a layer, dose, and the Bragg curve

In the continuous slowing-down approximation the residual range after a layer of thickness \(t\) is \(R(E) - \rho t\), so the exit energy is the energy with that range, found by inverting the range table the same way:

\[ E_\text{out} = R^{-1}\!\left(R(E) - \rho t\right), \qquad D\,[\text{Gy}] = 1.602\times10^{-10}\ \Phi\,[\text{cm}^{-2}]\ \frac{S}{\rho}\left[\frac{\text{MeV cm}^2}{\text{g}}\right]. \]

The dose uses the total stopping for ions and only the collision part for electrons (bremsstrahlung photons mostly escape a thin layer). The Bragg curve plots \(S\big(R^{-1}(R(E) - \rho x)\big)\) against depth \(x\): the stopping power each ion has when it reaches that depth, with no straggling, so its peak is sharper than a measured one.

Accuracy of the interpolation: removing each grid point and interpolating it from its neighbors (a grid twice as coarse as the real one) reproduces NIST’s values with a median error of 0.03–0.15 % and at most 1.5 % (protons near 1.5 GeV) in water, silicon, and lead. On the real grid the error is about four times smaller, well inside NIST’s own uncertainty.

Worked example

The default inputs: 100 MeV protons entering liquid water (ρ = 1.000 g/cm³), a 50 mm layer, and a fluence of 10⁹ cm⁻².

  • PSTAR gives S/ρ = 7.289 MeV·cm²/g at 100 MeV (a grid point, so no interpolation): 7.289 MeV/cm, or 0.729 keV/µm. The nuclear part is 0.04 % of the total.
  • The CSDA range is 7.718 g/cm², so 77.18 mm of water; the mean depth (projected range) is 77.07 mm.
  • After 50 mm the residual range is 7.718 − 5.000 = 2.718 g/cm², which belongs to an energy of 55.8 MeV: the layer absorbed 44.2 MeV per proton, more than its entrance stopping power times its thickness (36.4 MeV), because the protons slow down and lose energy faster as they go.
  • The entrance dose is 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁰ × 10⁹ × 7.289 = 1.17 Gy. The Bragg curve below shows the stopping power climbing steeply over the last few millimeters before 77.2 mm.

Data used

The tables are NIST Standard Reference Database 124 (ESTAR, PSTAR, and ASTAR, version 2.0.1), retrieved on 2026-11-06 with NIST’s default energy grids: 132 energies from 1 keV to 10 GeV for protons, 121 from 1 keV to 1 GeV for alphas, and 81 from 10 keV to 1 GeV for electrons. This page does not reproduce the full database: it carries NIST’s tables only for the materials in its menus (21 elements and 14 compounds and mixtures chosen as the ones most often needed for beams, detectors, windows, and dosimetry, of the 74 materials NIST tabulates for ions and 279 for electrons), and every result is interpolated from NIST’s tables for those materials. The page loads only the table for the particle you choose (60–130 kB, less when compressed), with only the columns used here, and NIST’s four significant figures unchanged. Densities and mean excitation energies are NIST’s, separately for the ion and electron sets. For any other material, or for a table at your own energies, use NIST’s programs directly.

Assumptions and limits

  • Mean values only. Real particles straggle: the energy loss and range vary from particle to particle by about 1 % of the range for protons in water and far more for electrons, which also scatter so much that their CSDA range is a path length, not a depth.
  • CSDA. The exit energy assumes all energy is lost continuously and every particle survives. Nuclear reactions remove about 1 % of protons per centimeter of water at 100 MeV, and an electron can lose a large fraction of its energy in one bremsstrahlung event.
  • Uncertainty of the tables. NIST quotes ICRU’s estimates. Protons and alphas: 1–2 % for elements and 1–4 % for compounds at high energies, 2–5 % at 1 MeV, 5–10 % at 100 keV, 10–15 % at 10 keV, and at least 20–30 % at 1 keV. Electron collision stopping: 1–2 % above 100 keV, and 2–3 % (low Z) to 5–10 % (high Z) from 10 to 100 keV; radiative stopping 2–5 %. NIST also notes that the values are accurate to three significant figures, with the fourth given for interpolation.
  • Materials. Compounds use NIST’s fixed compositions; for compounds without measurements NIST combines the elements by Bragg additivity. Changing the density keeps the tabulated density-effect correction, which for electrons above about 1 MeV differs between a gas and a condensed material.
  • Other ions. For a heavier ion at the same speed, a rough estimate is S ≈ z²eff × Sproton(E/A) with an effective charge below z at low speed; use a dedicated heavy-ion code for accurate values.
  • The range is where the particle stops in the material, not where it stops ionizing a detector: detector windows, dead layers, and gas gaps add to the material in the path. For beam trajectories through electrode and magnet fields before the target, use the Ion Optics Workbench.
