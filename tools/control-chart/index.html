Control Chart Calculator

A control chart answers one question: is a process stable, or has something changed? Paste measurements in time order to get the control limits, the band that routine variation stays inside, for an X̄–R, X̄–S, or individuals (I–MR) chart. The tool plots both charts and flags the points and patterns that signal a change, using the Western Electric run rules and a trend test.

Key terms
Control chart
A plot of a measured quantity in time order, with a center line and an upper and a lower control limit. Points inside the limits with no unusual pattern are treated as routine variation.
Subgroup
A few measurements taken together under the same conditions, such as five consecutive parts every hour. The chart plots one average and one range (or standard deviation) per subgroup; 4 or 5 parts per subgroup is typical.
Control limits (UCL, LCL)
The upper and lower lines, set three standard deviations of the plotted value above and below the center line. They come from the process’s own variation, not from the drawing or the specification.
Range and moving range
Simple measures of spread. A range is the largest minus the smallest value in a subgroup; a moving range is the difference between two consecutive single values. Their averages (R̄ and MR̄) set how far apart the limits are.
Common and special causes
Common causes are the many small sources of variation that are always present. A special cause is something specific that changed, such as a worn tool, a new material lot, or a focus shift. Finding and removing special causes is what the chart is for.
Signal and run rule
A signal is a point beyond a control limit or a pattern that is unlikely by chance, such as eight points in a row above the center line (a run rule). It says “look for a cause”; it does not mean the parts are out of specification.
False alarm and average run length
A false alarm is a signal from a process that has not changed. The average run length (ARL) is the average number of points plotted before a signal: about 370 for a stable process when only the 3σ limits are used.
Phase I and Phase II
Phase I computes trial limits from past data and checks whether the process was stable. Phase II uses limits fixed from a stable period, or a known mean and σ, to judge new data.

Inputs

X̄–R for subgroups of up to about 10 values, X̄–S for larger ones, and I–MR when each measurement stands alone (one value per batch, shift, or day).

2–25

Measurements per subgroup, typically 4 or 5. Every subgroup must have the same size.

Separate values with spaces, tabs, commas, or new lines, and use a period as the decimal mark. Consecutive blocks of n values form the subgroups, so a missing value would shift every later subgroup. Use at least 20–25 subgroups (or 50–100 single values). The example is an ablation depth in µm, 25 subgroups of 5, with a small upward shift starting around subgroup 18.

Control limits

Phase I estimates the center line and σ from these data: use it first, on a run of past data. Phase II judges new data against a known mean and σ, usually from an earlier stable Phase I study.

units
units

The short-term standard deviation of single measurements, not of subgroup averages: for example R̄/d₂ from a stable Phase I study.

Run rules

Extra rules catch small shifts sooner but add false alarms. With known limits, a stable process gives a false alarm every 370 points on average with rule 1 alone, every 92 with rules 1–4, and every 78 with the trend rule too.

Results

Out-of-control signals— A signal means “look for a special cause,” not “adjust the process.” In Phase I, find the cause, delete those subgroups from the data, and recalculate.

How the control limits are calculated

A Shewhart chart puts its limits three standard deviations of the plotted statistic (three standard errors) either side of the center line. The standard deviation is estimated from variation within subgroups, so that shifts between subgroups show up as signals instead of widening the limits. From \(k\) subgroups of size \(n\), the tool takes the grand mean \(\bar{\bar x}\) (the mean of the subgroup means), the average range \(\bar R\) (the mean of the subgroup ranges, largest minus smallest value), and the average standard deviation \(\bar s\) (the mean of the subgroup standard deviations, each with the \(n-1\) divisor). From \(N\) individual values it takes their mean \(\bar x\) and the average \(\overline{MR}\) of the \(N-1\) moving ranges \(|x_i - x_{i-1}|\). The limits are:

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar X\text{–}R:\quad \mathrm{UCL}, \mathrm{LCL} &= \bar{\bar x} \pm A_2 \bar R \\ \mathrm{UCL}_R &= D_4 \bar R \\ \mathrm{LCL}_R &= D_3 \bar R \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \bar X\text{–}S:\quad \mathrm{UCL}, \mathrm{LCL} &= \bar{\bar x} \pm A_3 \bar s \\ \mathrm{UCL}_S &= B_4 \bar s \\ \mathrm{LCL}_S &= B_3 \bar s \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{I–MR}:\quad \mathrm{UCL}, \mathrm{LCL} &= \bar x \pm 3\frac{\overline{MR}}{1.128} \\ &= \bar x \pm 2.660\,\overline{MR} \\ \mathrm{UCL}_{MR} &= 3.267\,\overline{MR} \end{aligned} \]

The constants come from the distribution of the range and the standard deviation of \(n\) independent normal values. The mean range is \(d_2\sigma\), with \(d_2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \bigl[1 - \Phi(x)^n - \bigl(1-\Phi(x)\bigr)^n\bigr]\,dx\) for the standard normal distribution function \(\Phi\); the standard deviation of the range is \(d_3\sigma\); and the mean of \(s\) is \(c_4\sigma\), with \(c_4 = \sqrt{2/(n-1)}\,\Gamma(n/2)/\Gamma\bigl((n-1)/2\bigr)\). So \(\hat\sigma = \bar R/d_2 = \bar s/c_4\), \(A_2 = 3/(d_2\sqrt n)\), \(A_3 = 3/(c_4\sqrt n)\), \(D_3, D_4 = 1 \mp 3d_3/d_2\), and \(B_3, B_4 = 1 \mp 3\sqrt{1-c_4^2}/c_4\), with negative values set to zero. With a standard given (Phase II), the X̄ limits are \(\mu_0 \pm 3\sigma_0/\sqrt n\), the range chart runs from \(D_1\sigma_0\) to \(D_2\sigma_0\) around \(d_2\sigma_0\), with \(D_1, D_2 = d_2 \mp 3d_3\), and the S chart runs from \(B_5\sigma_0\) to \(B_6\sigma_0\) around \(c_4\sigma_0\), with \(B_5, B_6 = c_4 \mp 3\sqrt{1-c_4^2}\). A moving range is the range of two values, so the individuals chart uses the \(n = 2\) factors: \(3/d_2 = 3/1.128 = 2.660\), \(D_4 = 3.267\), and, with a standard given, limits \(\mu_0 \pm 3\sigma_0\) and \(D_2\sigma_0 = 3.686\,\sigma_0\).

The average moving range is the standard estimate of σ for individual values. When a few very large moving ranges inflate it, the median moving range \(\widetilde{MR}\) is a more robust alternative, with limits \(\bar x \pm 3.145\,\widetilde{MR}\). Do not use the standard deviation of all the values: any shift or drift in the data inflates it and widens the limits, which hides the very signals the chart looks for (Wheeler, “Individual charts done right and wrong”).

Run rules

Points beyond the control limits are not the only sign of a special cause. The zone rules of the Western Electric handbook catch smaller sustained shifts sooner. They divide each side of the center line into zones at 1σ and 2σ, where σ is the standard deviation of one plotted point (σ/√n for subgroup means). The tool checks these rules:

These are the Western Electric (1956) versions of rules 1–4, which count the points on each side of the center line separately, plus the six-point trend test from Nelson’s (1984) list of eight tests. A point beyond 3σ also counts as beyond 2σ and beyond 1σ, and a point exactly on the center line breaks a run. Nelson’s run test uses nine points in a row rather than eight, which raises the in-control ARL with rule 1 from 153 to 217 points. His list also flags 14 points in a row alternating up and down, 15 in a row within 1σ (stratification), and 8 in a row beyond 1σ on either side (a mixture), which the tool does not check.

The tool applies the selected rules to the X̄ or individuals chart, and only rule 1 to the range, S, and moving-range charts. Ranges and standard deviations of small subgroups have skewed distributions that the symmetric zones do not fit, and consecutive moving ranges share a value, so they are not independent: Wheeler and Stauffer advise against rules 2–4 on a moving-range chart.

False alarms and how fast a shift is caught

Every rule added makes the chart react sooner to a small shift, and more likely to signal when nothing has changed. The average run length (ARL), the average number of points plotted before the first signal, measures both. The shifts are in σ of one plotted point: on an X̄ chart of subgroups of 5, a 1σ shift is a change of 0.45 process σ. The first three rows are exact, by the Markov-chain method of Champ and Woodall (1987), who give 91.75 for rules 1–4; the trend row is simulated, to within ±0.1.

The range, S, and moving-range charts add false alarms of their own, because these statistics have long upper tails: with known σ, a point crosses the UCL about once every 220 subgroups on an R chart with n = 5, and once every 110 points on a moving-range chart. In Phase I, where the limits come from the same data, what matters is the chance of at least one false signal in the whole data set. With every rule on, a perfectly stable set of 25 subgroups of 5 shows at least one signal about one time in four (27 % in a simulation that runs stable normal data through this tool), and a stable 100-point individuals series usually does (83 %). Investigate signals; do not adjust the process for every one.

The trend rule earns its place least. Davis and Woodall (1988) found that it is of virtually no help in detecting a drift of the mean once the other run rules are in use, and Wheeler and Stauffer (2017) advise against it. In the table it adds about 18 % more false alarms for no real gain, and for a drift of 0.1σ per point it shortens the ARL only from 12.1 to 12.0 points. Clear its box unless a trend is the failure you are watching for.

Worked example

The default data are 25 subgroups of 5 ablation-depth measurements. The grand mean is 12.138 µm and \(\bar R = 0.7032\) µm, so the X̄ limits are \(12.138 \pm 0.577 \times 0.7032 = 11.732\) to \(12.544\) µm. The R-chart UCL is \(2.114 \times 0.7032 = 1.487\) µm, and the within-subgroup σ is \(0.7032/2.326 = 0.302\) µm.

No point crosses a control limit, but the zone rules catch four low means out of five (subgroups 8–12, rule 3). Late in the run they catch the upward shift: rule 2 at subgroup 24, rule 3 at 24, and rule 4 at 25. The realized shift was about 0.3 µm, roughly 1 process σ. Because the Phase I grand mean includes the shifted subgroups, those subgroups sit only about 1.5σ of X̄ above the center line, and rule 1 alone would have missed the shift. In Phase I, find the cause, remove the affected subgroups, and recalculate the limits before using them for monitoring.

With a standard given instead (Phase II, μ₀ = 12.0 µm and σ₀ = 0.30 µm), the X̄ limits are \(12.0 \pm 3 \times 0.30/\sqrt 5\), 11.598 to 12.402 µm, and the shift is unmistakable: rule 1 flags subgroups 20, 23, and 24, and the zone rules flag every subgroup from 20 to 25. The R chart runs from 0 to \(D_2\sigma_0 = 4.918 \times 0.30 = 1.475\) µm around \(d_2\sigma_0 = 0.698\) µm.

Assumptions and limits

  • Rational subgroups. Measurements within a subgroup should be made close together under the same conditions, so within-subgroup variation reflects only common causes. Mixing machines, heads, or material lots within a subgroup inflates R̄, widens the limits, and hides real shifts.
  • Equal subgroup sizes. Every subgroup must hold n values. The tool ignores an incomplete last subgroup; data with subgroups of different sizes need limits that change from subgroup to subgroup, which the tool does not compute.
  • Independence. Strongly autocorrelated data, such as slow thermal drift or high-rate sensor streams, produce too many false alarms. Subsample the data, or chart the residuals of a time-series model (Montgomery, Section 10.4).
  • Approximate normality. X̄ charts are robust to non-normal data because subgroup means are close to normal. An individuals chart of skewed data gives more false alarms on the long-tail side: for a chi-square distribution with 4 degrees of freedom, 1.4 % of points fall above the upper 3σ limit, an ARL of about 71 instead of 370. Wheeler found that 3σ limits still cover more than 98 % of every mound-shaped distribution he examined, so the chart still works, but check a histogram before reading much into points just beyond the limit on the long tail.
  • Enough data. Limits from fewer than about 20–25 subgroups (or 50–100 individual values) are uncertain: treat them as trial limits and revise them as data accumulate. Even 25 subgroups of 5 leave the limits uncertain. In a simulation with rule 1 only, one Phase I study in ten gives limits with an in-control ARL below 130 points and one in ten above 850, against the nominal 370; with 100 subgroups the range narrows to about 220–570, and for 100 individual values it is about 120–1200. Jensen et al. review this effect of estimating the limits.
  • Control limits are not specification limits. Control limits describe the process; specification limits describe the customer requirement. Use the capability calculator to compare the two.
