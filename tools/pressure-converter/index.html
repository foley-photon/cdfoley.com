Vacuum Pressure Unit Converter

Type a pressure in any unit and the rest update. The panel also shows the vacuum range for that value, which gauge types can measure it, and how far a nitrogen molecule travels between collisions at that pressure. Use it for Torr to mbar, mbar to Pa, mTorr to Pa, and psi to Torr conversions.

Key terms
Absolute and gauge pressure
Absolute pressure is measured from a perfect vacuum, and every value here is absolute. Gauge pressure (psig, barg) is measured from the surrounding air, so a gauge-pressure dial shows a vacuum as a negative number.
Pascal, millibar, and torr
The pascal (Pa) is the SI unit, one newton per square meter. A millibar is 100 Pa and is common on European equipment; a torr is 1/760 of a standard atmosphere, about 1.333 mbar, and is common in North American labs.
Standard atmosphere
Exactly 101 325 Pa (1013.25 mbar, 760 Torr, about 14.7 psi): a fixed reference close to average sea-level air pressure. Real air pressure changes with the weather and the altitude.
Millimeter of mercury
The pressure under a 1 mm column of mercury, using a conventional density and standard gravity. It differs from the torr by less than one part in seven million.
Vacuum range
A named band of pressure (rough, medium, high, ultra-high, or extreme-high vacuum) with its own pumps, gauges, and gas behavior. The boundaries are conventions, and two versions are in use.
Gas-dependent gauge
Pirani and ionization gauges work out the pressure from a property of the gas and are calibrated for nitrogen, so in other gases they read wrong unless corrected. Diaphragm gauges measure the force itself and read the same in any gas.
Mean free path
How far a molecule travels between collisions with other molecules. When it is longer than the chamber, molecules fly from wall to wall without meeting each other.

How the conversions work

Everything converts through the pascal, \(1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N/m}^2\). The defining relations are

\[ \begin{aligned} 1\ \text{atm} &= 101\,325\ \text{Pa}, \\ 1\ \text{Torr} &= \frac{101\,325}{760}\ \text{Pa} \\ &\approx 133.322\,368\ \text{Pa}, \\ 1\ \text{mbar} &= 100\ \text{Pa}. \end{aligned} \]

Every value in the table is exact; “…” marks a decimal that does not end. The standard atmosphere is defined as 101 325 Pa by the 10th CGPM (1954), and the bar, 10⁵ Pa, is one of the non-SI units listed in the SI Brochure. The conventional mercury units assume mercury of density 13.5951 g/cm³ (its density near 0 °C) under standard gravity, \(g_n\) = 9.806 65 m/s², and the pound-force is 0.453 592 37 kg × \(g_n\). NIST SP 811 lists the same factors to the seven digits it prints (1.333 224 × 10² Pa for the torr and the conventional mmHg, 3.386 389 × 10³ Pa for the conventional inHg, and 6.894 757 × 10³ Pa for the psi).

Handy anchors for vacuum work: 1 Torr ≈ 1.333 mbar, 1 mbar ≈ 0.750 Torr, and 1 mTorr ≈ 0.1333 Pa. A torr and a millimeter of mercury differ by about one part in seven million, which is below the accuracy of any vacuum gauge. The two are interchangeable in practice, though only the torr is defined exactly from the atmosphere.

Vacuum ranges

The converter names the range of each value with the boundaries in the first table. Down to 10⁻⁷ mbar they are those of the first edition of ISO 3529-1 (1981), which many handbooks and gauge makers still use; extreme-high vacuum is everything below 10⁻¹² mbar (10⁻¹⁰ Pa), as in CERN’s vacuum courses. Each range includes its lower boundary, so 10⁻³ mbar is medium vacuum and 10⁻⁷ mbar high vacuum. The converter also calls anything from 900 to 1100 mbar atmospheric, since ambient air pressure varies about that much with weather and elevation. Treat the ranges as conventions for talking about a system, not as physical thresholds.

The current edition, ISO 3529-1:2019, keeps the boundaries down to 10⁻³ mbar but ends high vacuum at 10⁻⁸ mbar, because modern pumps reach that with elastomer seals and at most a mild bake-out, while lower pressures need metal seals and a bake-out of 150 °C or more. Its extreme-high vacuum is everything below 10⁻¹¹ mbar, its low vacuum reaches up to the prevailing atmospheric pressure, and each of its other ranges includes its lower boundary too. Between 10⁻⁸ and 10⁻⁷ mbar and between 10⁻¹² and 10⁻¹¹ mbar the two schemes use different names, and there the panel above shows the ISO name as well. Two points matter when reading a gauge. Thermal-conductivity (Pirani) and ionization gauges are gas-dependent and are normally calibrated for N₂, so in helium or argon they can be off by a large factor. Capacitance diaphragm gauges measure force per area directly and read the same for any gas. And no gauge is as precise as the seven digits above: a hot-cathode ionization gauge is typically good to about ±10 %, a cold-cathode gauge to ±25–50 %, and a Pirani to about 10 % of the reading between 10⁻³ and 1 mbar and to about 30 % over its whole range.

Worked example

A turbo-pumped chamber reads 5 × 10⁻⁶ Torr. That is 5 × 10⁻⁶ × 133.322 = 6.67 × 10⁻⁴ Pa, or 6.67 × 10⁻⁶ mbar, which falls in the high vacuum range of both schemes. At 20 °C the N₂ mean free path is about 6.48 mm·Pa ÷ 6.67 × 10⁻⁴ Pa ≈ 9.7 m, much longer than the chamber. Molecules cross the chamber without hitting each other, so the flow is molecular.

Assumptions and limits

  • All units are absolute pressures. Gauge pressure (psig, barg) is measured relative to local atmosphere. Add the ambient pressure, about 14.7 psi or 1013 mbar at sea level, before converting.
  • Mercury-column units use the conventional density and standard gravity. Real manometer readings need temperature and local-gravity corrections.
  • The vacuum range and the gauge types are for orientation. Measuring ranges differ between models, and each scheme’s boundaries are a convention.
  • Mean free path and number density assume an ideal gas at 20 °C. The N₂ mean free path uses Kennard’s hard-sphere diameter of 3.75 Å, derived from the viscosity at 15 °C (see the mean free path calculator). The panel flags pressures above about 17 atm, where λ falls under 10 molecular diameters and the gas is no longer dilute.
