How it is calculated

The spectral radiance of a blackbody at temperature \(T\) is given by Planck's law, in W m⁻² sr⁻¹ per meter of wavelength. A gray body scales it by a wavelength-independent emissivity \(\varepsilon\):

\[ B_\lambda(\lambda, T) = \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\,\frac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda k_B T} - 1} . \]

The spectral exitance (power per area per wavelength into the hemisphere) of a Lambertian surface is \(\pi B_\lambda\). Integrating over all wavelengths gives the Stefan–Boltzmann law, \(M = \varepsilon\sigma T^4\), with \(\sigma = 5.670374\times10^{-8}\) W m⁻² K⁻⁴.

Where is the peak?

The answer depends on the variable. Per unit wavelength, \(B_\lambda\) peaks at Wien's displacement \(\lambda_\text{max} = b/T\), with \(b = 2.897772\times10^{-3}\) m·K. Per unit frequency (or wavenumber), \(B_\nu\) peaks at \(h\nu_\text{max} = 2.821439\,k_BT\). That corresponds to a wavelength about 1.76× longer than \(\lambda_\text{max}\). For the Sun's 5772 K effective temperature, the peaks are at 502 nm per unit wavelength and 339 THz (883 nm) per unit frequency. Neither one is “the” color of the source.

Band fraction

The fraction of total power emitted between 0 and \(\lambda\) depends only on the product \(\lambda T\). With \(x = hc/\lambda k_BT\), it can be summed exactly:

\[ \begin{aligned} F_{0\to\lambda} &= \frac{15}{\pi^4}\int_x^\infty \frac{t^3}{e^t-1}\,dt \\ &= \frac{15}{\pi^4}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-nx}}{n} \\ &\quad\times\left(x^3 + \frac{3x^2}{n} + \frac{6x}{n^2} + \frac{6}{n^3}\right). \end{aligned} \]

The in-band fraction is \(F_{0\to\lambda_2} - F_{0\to\lambda_1}\). At long wavelengths both terms approach 1, and subtracting them would lose precision, so for \(x \le 2\) the tool instead sums the complement \({1 - F_{0\to\lambda}}\) from the Bernoulli-number series of the same integral. The photon-number fraction uses the same idea with \(t^2\) in place of \(t^3\), normalized by \(2\zeta(3)\). The total photon exitance is \(M_p = 1.5205\times10^{15}\,\varepsilon T^3\) photons s⁻¹ m⁻² K⁻³.

Worked example

Take a 5772 K blackbody. \(M = \sigma T^4 = 6.294\times10^{7}\) W/m², or 6294 W/cm². The spectral radiance at 500 nm is \(2.624\times10^{4}\) W m⁻² sr⁻¹ nm⁻¹. The 200–400 nm band carries 12.0 % of the total power, and the 400–700 nm visible band carries 36.6 %. Raising the temperature to 15 000 K moves \(\lambda_\text{max}\) to 193 nm and puts most of the power in the ultraviolet.

Plasmas, arcs, and lamps

Hot plasmas and arc or discharge lamps are often described by an effective, brightness, or color temperature. In local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE), Kirchhoff's law means no thermal source can exceed the blackbody spectral radiance at its own temperature. An optically thick region approaches that limit, while optically thin regions emit less. Real plasma spectra also contain atomic and ionic lines on top of a continuum, and their emissivity varies with wavelength. For a source in LTE, treat the Planck curve at its actual temperature as an upper bound and a scaling guide, not a prediction of a specific source. A quoted color, brightness, or effective temperature does not set that bound: each matches the blackbody in only one respect (the spectral shape, the radiance at one wavelength, or the total exitance). Line emission from a non-LTE discharge (a low-pressure sodium lamp, for example) or from a laser can exceed the Planck curve at that temperature by orders of magnitude.

Assumptions and limits

  • Gray body. \(\varepsilon\) is constant with wavelength. Real materials (metals, oxides, glasses) have strongly wavelength-dependent emissivity.
  • Lambertian emitter, hemispherical exitance. Radiance is the same in all directions. Exitance and power count emission into the full hemisphere from one side of the area.
  • Emission only. Net radiative transfer to cooler surroundings is \(\varepsilon\sigma(T^4 - T_\text{env}^4)\) for a small body in a large enclosure.
  • Vacuum wavelengths. In a medium of index \(n\), the total radiance and \(B_\nu\) scale by \(n^2\), \(B_\lambda\) per unit wavelength in the medium by \(n^3\), and wavelengths shorten by \(n\).
