Blackbody Radiation Calculator

How much light and heat does a hot surface give off, and at which wavelengths? This Planck’s law calculator gives the blackbody spectrum at any temperature, where it peaks, the total power radiated per unit area, and the share of the power and of the photons that falls in a wavelength band you choose: the visible, a detector’s range, or a radiation thermometer’s window. An emissivity below 1 models a real (gray) surface. The band fraction is computed from an exact series, not read from a table.

Key terms
Blackbody
An ideal surface that absorbs all radiation falling on it. At a given temperature it also emits the most thermal radiation any surface can, with a spectrum set by the temperature alone (Planck’s law). A small opening into a uniformly heated cavity comes very close.
Emissivity and gray body
Emissivity, from 0 to 1, is how much a real surface emits compared with a blackbody at the same temperature. A gray body has the same emissivity at every wavelength, which is how this calculator treats a real surface. Near room temperature, polished metals are at about 0.02–0.1, and paints and skin at 0.8–0.98, whatever their visible color.
Spectral radiance
The power leaving a surface per unit area and per unit solid angle (steradian), in a narrow slice of wavelength (here per nanometer) or of frequency. It is what a camera, spectrometer, or radiation thermometer aimed at the surface responds to.
Exitance
The total power leaving a surface per unit area, into the whole hemisphere in front of it, in W/m². For a blackbody it is σT⁴ (the Stefan–Boltzmann law): about 460 W/m² at room temperature and 63 MW/m² at the surface of the Sun. For a surface that looks equally bright from every direction (Lambertian), it is π times the radiance.
Wien peak
The wavelength where the spectrum, counted per unit wavelength, is highest: 2898 µm·K divided by the temperature, so 9.7 µm at room temperature and 502 nm for the Sun. Counted per unit frequency, the same spectrum peaks at a wavelength 1.76 times longer.
Band fraction
The share of the total emitted power that falls between two wavelengths. It depends only on wavelength times temperature; for the Sun, 37 % falls between 400 and 700 nm.
Photon exitance
The number of photons leaving the surface per second per unit area. Photon detectors, solar cells, and photochemistry respond to the number of photons rather than to their energy.
Color temperature
The temperature of the blackbody whose color matches that of a light source, or for most lamps comes closest to it (the correlated color temperature). It is close to a physical temperature only for incandescent sources: a 3000 K LED lamp is nowhere near 3000 K and does not emit a Planck spectrum, so this calculator does not describe it.

Inputs

The temperature of the emitting surface (K = °C + 273.15). The lamp preset is CIE illuminant A, the standard model of a tungsten-filament lamp. For lamps, stars, and plasmas described by a color, brightness, or effective temperature, read “Plasmas, arcs, and lamps” below first.

0–1

1 for a blackbody. For a real surface, its emissivity over the wavelengths that matter at this temperature: near room temperature, about 0.02–0.1 for polished metals and 0.8–0.98 for paints and skin (table below). Every result except the peaks, the band fraction, and the mean photon energy scales with it.

The vacuum wavelength at which to report the spectral radiance, for example a radiation thermometer’s operating wavelength or the center of a filter’s passband.

The range of interest, as vacuum wavelengths: 400–700 nm for visible light, or the range of a detector or filter. The fraction depends only on wavelength times temperature.

Optional: the area of the emitting surface, counted on one side, to turn the exitance into watts.

How it is calculated

The spectral radiance of a blackbody at temperature \(T\) is given by Planck’s law. Per unit wavelength, in W m⁻² sr⁻¹ per meter of wavelength, and per unit frequency, in W m⁻² sr⁻¹ Hz⁻¹, it reads

\[ \begin{aligned} B_\lambda(\lambda, T) &= \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\,\frac{1}{e^{x} - 1}, \\[6pt] B_\nu(\nu, T) &= \frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\,\frac{1}{e^{x} - 1}, \end{aligned} \]

with \(x = hc/\lambda k_BT = h\nu/k_BT\), where \(\lambda\) is the wavelength in vacuum. The two describe the same radiation but are densities per different units, so \(B_\nu = B_\lambda\lambda^2/c\). Per unit wavenumber \(\tilde\nu = 1/\lambda\), the unit infrared spectroscopy uses, \(B_{\tilde\nu} = cB_\nu = \lambda^2B_\lambda\); the tool reports it per cm⁻¹. Dividing \(B_\lambda\) by the photon energy \(hc/\lambda\) gives the photon spectral radiance, \(2c/[\lambda^4(e^x - 1)]\). The first radiation constant for spectral radiance, \(2hc^2 = 1.191\,042\,972\times10^{-16}\) W m² sr⁻¹, and the second radiation constant, \(hc/k_B = 1.438\,776\,877\times10^{-2}\) m·K, are exact, because \(h\), \(c\), and \(k_B\) have fixed values in the SI. A gray body multiplies every radiance and exitance by a wavelength-independent emissivity \(\varepsilon\).

A blackbody looks equally bright from every direction (it is Lambertian), so its spectral exitance, the power per area per wavelength into the hemisphere, is \(\pi B_\lambda\), and its total radiance is \(M/\pi\). Integrating over all wavelengths gives the Stefan–Boltzmann law, \(M = \varepsilon\sigma T^4\), with \(\sigma = 2\pi^5k_B^4/(15h^3c^2) = 5.670\,374\,419\times10^{-8}\) W m⁻² K⁻⁴. Counting photons instead of energy gives the photon exitance

\[ \begin{aligned} M_p &= \varepsilon\,\frac{4\pi\zeta(3)}{c^2}\left(\frac{k_BT}{h}\right)^{3} \\ &= 1.5205\times10^{15}\,\varepsilon T^3\ \text{s}^{-1}\,\text{m}^{-2}\,\text{K}^{-3}, \end{aligned} \]

where \(\zeta(3) = 1.202\,057\). The ratio of the two, the mean photon energy \(M/M_p = \pi^4k_BT/[30\zeta(3)] = 2.701\,k_BT\), depends on the temperature alone.

Where is the peak?

The answer depends on the variable. Per unit wavelength, \(B_\lambda\) peaks at Wien’s displacement law, \(\lambda_\text{max} = b/T\) with \(b = 2.897\,771\,955\times10^{-3}\) m·K. Per unit frequency (or wavenumber), \(B_\nu\) peaks at \(\nu_\text{max} = b'T\) with \(b' = 5.878\,925\,757\times10^{10}\) Hz/K, that is, at \(h\nu_\text{max} = 2.821\,439\,k_BT\). That frequency corresponds to a wavelength 1.76 times longer than \(\lambda_\text{max}\). For the Sun’s 5772 K effective temperature, the peaks are at 502 nm per unit wavelength and at 339 THz (883 nm) per unit frequency. Neither one is “the” color of the source: both curves describe the same light, counted in slices of equal wavelength or of equal frequency.

Written in terms of \(x\) at a fixed temperature, each way of counting is proportional to \(x^p/(e^x - 1)\): \(p = 5\) for \(B_\lambda\), 3 for \(B_\nu\), 4 for power per unit logarithm of wavelength (\(\lambda B_\lambda = \nu B_\nu\)) and for photons per unit wavelength, and 2 for photons per unit frequency. The peak solves \(x = p(1 - e^{-x})\), exactly \(x = p + W(-p\,e^{-p})\), where \(W\) is the Lambert W function: \(x\) = 4.965 114, 3.920 690, 2.821 439, and 1.593 624 for \(p\) = 5, 4, 3, and 2. CODATA gives the constants for the \(B_\lambda\) and \(B_\nu\) peaks, \(b\) and \(b'\); the other forms are just as valid:

Band fraction

The fraction of the total power emitted between 0 and \(\lambda\) depends only on the product \(\lambda T\). With \(x = hc/\lambda k_BT\), it is summed exactly from the series for this integral (Abramowitz and Stegun, eq. 27.1.2):

\[ \begin{aligned} F_{0\to\lambda} &= \frac{15}{\pi^4}\int_x^\infty \frac{t^3}{e^t-1}\,dt \\ &= \frac{15}{\pi^4}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-nx}}{n} \\ &\quad\times\left(x^3 + \frac{3x^2}{n} + \frac{6x}{n^2} + \frac{6}{n^3}\right). \end{aligned} \]

The in-band fraction is \(F_{0\to\lambda_2} - F_{0\to\lambda_1}\). At long wavelengths both terms approach 1, and subtracting them would lose precision, so for \(x \le 2\) the tool sums the complement from its Bernoulli-number series instead (eq. 27.1.1, which converges for \(x < 2\pi\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} 1 - F_{0\to\lambda} &= \frac{15x^3}{\pi^4}\Big(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{x}{8} + \frac{x^2}{60} \\ &\quad - \frac{x^4}{5040} + \frac{x^6}{272\,160} - \cdots\Big). \end{aligned} \]

The photon-number fraction uses the same two series with \(t^2\) in place of \(t^3\), normalized by \(\int_0^\infty t^2/(e^t - 1)\,dt = 2\zeta(3)\). Checked against 60-digit reference values for 1500 random bands, the computed in-band fractions agree to better than 1 part in 10⁹.

Worked example

The defaults: a 5772 K blackbody (\(\varepsilon = 1\)) with an area of 1 cm², its radiance at 500 nm, and the 200–400 nm band.

  • Peaks. \(\lambda_\text{max} = b/T = 502.04\) nm, and \(\nu_\text{max} = b'T = 339.33\) THz, which is 883.5 nm.
  • Radiance at 500 nm. Here \(x = hc/\lambda k_BT = 4.985\,37\) and \(2hc^2/\lambda^5 = 3.811\,34\times10^{15}\) W m⁻³ sr⁻¹. Dividing by \(e^x - 1 = 145.257\) gives \(B_\lambda = 2.624\times10^{13}\) W m⁻³ sr⁻¹, which is 2.624 × 10⁴ W m⁻² sr⁻¹ nm⁻¹. Times π, the spectral exitance is 8.243 × 10⁴ W m⁻² nm⁻¹; times \(\lambda^2\), the radiance per wavenumber is 656 W m⁻² sr⁻¹ (cm⁻¹)⁻¹.
  • Totals. \(M = \sigma T^4 = 6.294\times10^{7}\) W/m², or 6294 W/cm², so 6.294 kW from 1 cm², and \(L = M/\pi = 2.003\times10^{7}\) W m⁻² sr⁻¹. Over a sphere of the IAU nominal solar radius, 6.957 × 10⁸ m, the total is 3.828 × 10²⁶ W, the IAU nominal solar luminosity, because the nominal temperature was derived from the Stefan–Boltzmann law.
  • Band. At 200 and 400 nm, \(x\) is 12.4634 and 6.2317, and the exponential series gives \(F_{0\to\lambda}\) = 0.001 478 and 0.121 770. The band holds the difference, 0.120 29, or 12.03 % of the power: 7.571 × 10⁶ W/m², or 757.1 W from 1 cm².
  • Photons. \(M_p = 1.5205\times10^{15}\times 5772^3 = 2.924\times10^{26}\) s⁻¹ m⁻², or 2.924 × 10²² s⁻¹ cm⁻². The band holds 4.33 % of the photons, 1.267 × 10²¹ s⁻¹ cm⁻², less than its share of the power because ultraviolet photons each carry more energy than the mean, \(2.701\,k_BT\) = 1.344 eV.

Raising the temperature to 15 000 K moves \(\lambda_\text{max}\) to 193 nm and puts 74 % of the power below 400 nm, in the ultraviolet.

Plasmas, arcs, and lamps

Hot plasmas and arc or discharge lamps are often described by an effective, brightness, or color temperature. In local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE), Kirchhoff’s law means that no thermal source can exceed the blackbody spectral radiance at its own temperature or, for a non-uniform plasma, at the highest temperature along the line of sight. An optically thick region approaches that limit, while optically thin regions emit less. Real plasma spectra also contain atomic and ionic lines on top of a continuum, and their emissivity varies with wavelength. For a source in LTE, treat the Planck curve at its actual temperature as an upper bound and a scaling guide, not a prediction of a specific source. A quoted temperature of another kind does not set that bound, because each matches the blackbody in only one respect: a color temperature matches the color (chromaticity), a brightness (radiance) temperature the spectral radiance at one wavelength, a distribution temperature the shape of the spectrum over a range, and an effective temperature the total exitance, \(\sigma T_\text{eff}^4\). Line emission from a non-LTE discharge (a low-pressure sodium lamp, for example) or from a laser can exceed the Planck curve at the temperature of the gas or the device by orders of magnitude.

Assumptions and limits

  • Gray body. \(\varepsilon\) is constant with wavelength. Real materials (metals, oxides, glasses) have strongly wavelength-dependent emissivity, so for a band result use the emissivity averaged over that band (weighted by the Planck spectrum), and for the total use the total hemispherical emissivity.
  • Lambertian emitter, hemispherical exitance. Radiance is taken to be the same in all directions, which is exact for a blackbody. Real surfaces depart from it, most at grazing angles, so use the normal emissivity for the radiance seen head-on and the hemispherical emissivity for exitance and power. Exitance and power count emission into the full hemisphere from one side of the area.
  • Emission only. The net radiative loss of a small body to a large enclosure at \(T_\text{env}\) is \(\varepsilon\sigma(T^4 - T_\text{env}^4)\). Between two large parallel gray plates it is \(\sigma(T_1^4 - T_2^4)/(1/\varepsilon_1 + 1/\varepsilon_2 - 1)\) per unit area.
  • Vacuum wavelengths. In a transparent medium of refractive index \(n\), the radiance at each frequency and the total radiance are \(n^2\) times the vacuum values, and so is \(B_\lambda\) per unit vacuum wavelength, the form the CIE gives. Per unit wavelength measured in the medium, which is \(n\) times shorter, \(B_\lambda\) is \(n^3\) times larger when \(n\) does not vary with wavelength.
  • Thermal equilibrium, far field. Planck’s law describes a body in (local) thermal equilibrium, much larger than the wavelength, seen from many wavelengths away. Across vacuum gaps comparable to or smaller than the thermal wavelengths (about 10 µm at room temperature), near-field transfer can exceed the blackbody limit.
