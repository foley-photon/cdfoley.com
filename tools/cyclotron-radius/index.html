Cyclotron Radius & Frequency Calculator

A charged particle in a magnetic field moves in a circle, or a helix if it also moves along the field. This calculator gives the size of that circle and how often the particle goes around it, for electrons, protons, or any ion at any energy, from electronvolts to GeV. Use it to size a bending magnet or an energy analyzer, to check whether a field confines a beam or a plasma, or to find the ion cyclotron resonance (ICR) frequency of an m/z in a Penning trap.

Key terms
Cyclotron (Larmor) radius
The radius of the circle a charged particle follows in a uniform magnetic field, also called the gyroradius. It grows with the particle’s momentum and shrinks as the field gets stronger: a 1 keV electron in a 10 mT field circles with a radius of about 1 cm.
Cyclotron frequency
How many times per second the particle goes around. At low energy it depends only on the charge-to-mass ratio and the field, not on the energy: 28 GHz per tesla for an electron and 15.2 MHz per tesla for a proton.
Magnetic rigidity (Bρ)
The particle’s momentum divided by its charge, in tesla-meters. It is the field times bending radius a magnet must provide: a beam of rigidity 0.46 T·m (10 MeV protons) bends on a 0.46 m radius in a 1 T dipole.
Pitch angle
The angle between the particle’s velocity and the magnetic field. At 90° the particle moves in a flat circle; at smaller angles it spirals along the field, and only the velocity across the field sets the radius.
Lorentz factor
γ, the ratio of a particle’s total energy to its rest energy. Relativity makes a fast particle behave as if heavier by this factor, so its orbit is larger and its cyclotron frequency lower than the classical formulas give.
Ion cyclotron resonance (ICR)
Measuring an ion’s cyclotron frequency in a strong, very uniform magnetic field to find its m/z. FT-ICR mass spectrometers reach the highest mass resolution of any type, because a frequency can be measured very precisely.

Radius vs. kinetic energy

How it is calculated

The magnetic force \(q\,\mathbf v \times \mathbf B\) is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it changes the particle’s direction but never its speed or energy. Across the field the particle moves on a circle; along it, it coasts. For mass \(m\), charge \(q = ze\), and kinetic energy \(K\):

\[ \begin{gathered} \gamma = 1 + \frac{K}{mc^2}, \qquad pc = \sqrt{K^2 + 2Kmc^2}, \qquad v = \frac{pc^2}{\gamma m c^2}, \\[8pt] r_c = \frac{p \sin\alpha}{|q| B}, \qquad \omega_c = 2\pi f_c = \frac{|q| B}{\gamma m}, \qquad B\rho = \frac{p}{|q|}. \end{gathered} \]

The helix advances \(v\cos\alpha \cdot T_c\) along the field per turn. Classically \(\gamma = 1\) and \(p = \sqrt{2mK}\), which gives the familiar \(r_c = mv_\perp/|q|B\) and an orbit frequency \(f_0 = |q|B/2\pi m\) that does not depend on energy. That independence is why a classical cyclotron can accelerate with a fixed radio frequency, and why ICR measures m/z from a frequency alone:

\[ \frac{m}{z} = \frac{e B}{2\pi f_m}\ \ \text{(in kg; divide by 1 u for u/e)}, \qquad f_0 \approx 15.356\ \text{MHz} \times \frac{B/\text{T}}{(m/z)/\text{u}}. \]

Relativistically, the orbit frequency falls as \(1/\gamma\). For 10 MeV protons γ = 1.011, so a fixed-frequency cyclotron would slip out of step after a few hundred turns: the reason isochronous cyclotrons shape their field to rise with radius, and synchrocyclotrons sweep the frequency.

In practical units, \(B\rho\,[\mathrm{T\,m}] = 3.3356\, pc\,[\mathrm{GeV}]/|z|\) (the factor is \(10^9/c\)), and the electron cyclotron frequency is 27.992 GHz per tesla (2.80 MHz per gauss).

Worked example

The default inputs: a 10 MeV proton (m = 1.007276 u, mc² = 938.272 MeV) in a 1.5 T field, moving across it.

  • γ = 1 + 10/938.272 = 1.01066, and \(pc = \sqrt{10^2 + 2 \times 10 \times 938.272} = 137.35\) MeV, so v = pc²/(γmc²) = 0.1448 c = 43 420 km/s.
  • Bρ = 137.35 MeV / (299.79 MeV per T·m) = 0.4582 T·m, and r = 0.4582/1.5 = 0.3054 m. The classical formula gives 0.3046 m, 0.27 % smaller.
  • f = eB/(2πγm) = 22.868 MHz / 1.01066 = 22.627 MHz, so one turn takes 44.2 ns. A 1.5 T cyclotron for 10 MeV protons therefore needs a pole radius of a little over 30 cm.

Assumptions and limits

  • A uniform, static magnetic field and no electric field. In crossed E and B fields the guiding center also drifts at E/B; in a trap, the electric field shifts the measured frequency (the reduced cyclotron frequency of a Penning trap is lower than f₀ by roughly the magnetron frequency, and space charge shifts it further). Calibrate an ICR mass scale against known ions.
  • No energy loss: radiation (synchrotron radiation matters only for electrons at high energy and field), collisions, and space charge are ignored. In a gas the particle may not complete one orbit; compare the period with the collision time in the plasma parameters calculator.
  • Field gradients and fringe fields change real orbits and focusing. For trajectories through real magnets and electrodes, use the Ion Optics Workbench for full field solutions.
  • Masses of atomic and molecular ions are atomic masses minus z electron masses (AME2020, CODATA 2022); chemical binding energies are neglected.
