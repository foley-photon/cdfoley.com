Motion Blur & Exposure Time Calculator

A part that moves while the camera’s shutter is open comes out smeared. Use this calculator when parts move past a camera: it finds the longest exposure or strobe pulse that keeps the smear within your pixel budget, the blur at the exposure you use now, and the frame or line rate needed to image every millimeter of a conveyor or web.

Key terms
Exposure time
How long the sensor collects light for one image, set in the camera (also called shutter or integration time).
Strobe
A light that flashes briefly while the shutter is open. When the station is shielded from other light, the flash length, not the shutter time, sets the blur.
Motion blur
The smear a moving part leaves in the image, counted here in pixels: 1 px means the part moved the width of one pixel during the exposure.
Field of view (FOV)
The length of the part that one image covers. Here it is measured along the direction of travel, or across it for the line-scan camera.
Pixel size on the part
The length of the part that one pixel covers: the field of view divided by the number of pixels across it.
Frame overlap
The share of each image that repeats in the next one, so that a feature cut by one image’s edge appears whole in another.
Line-scan camera
A camera with a single row of pixels that builds the image one line at a time as the part or web moves past. Its line rate is the number of lines it takes per second.
Global and rolling shutter
A global shutter exposes every pixel at the same time. A rolling shutter starts and ends the rows one after another, so a moving part comes out skewed.

Inputs

Motion and area camera

The conveyor or line speed while the image is taken. For stop-and-go (indexed) motion with the part at rest during the exposure, there is no motion blur.

px

The field and the sensor pixels in the direction of travel: for example 2048 when the short side of a 2448 × 2048 sensor runs along the conveyor.

px

≤ 0.5 px for sub-pixel gauging, ≤ 1 px for general measurement, up to ~2 px for presence checks.

With strobed lighting, enter the light pulse width (valid when ambient light is shielded or much dimmer than the strobe).

%

At least the largest feature length as a % of the FOV along the motion, plus a margin for trigger jitter, so every feature appears whole in at least one frame.

Line-scan camera (optional)
px

The width of web the line covers, and the line sensor’s length in pixels from its datasheet. Leave both blank if you have no line-scan camera.

How it is calculated

While the shutter is open (or the strobe is on), the part moves \(v\,t\). Expressed in object-side pixels of size \(p_o = \text{FOV}/N_\text{px}\), the blur and the maximum exposure are

\[ b = \frac{v\,t_\text{exp}}{p_o}\ \text{pixels}, \qquad t_\text{max} = \frac{b_\text{allowed}\,p_o}{v}. \]

To image every part of a moving stream with an area camera, a new frame must be taken before the part travels one field of view, less the chosen overlap \(o\) (as a fraction, 20 % = 0.2):

\[ f_\text{frame} \ge \frac{v}{\text{FOV}\,(1 - o)}. \]

At this rate, a feature of length \(L\) appears whole in at least one frame when \(o\,\text{FOV} \ge L\).

A line-scan camera builds the image one row at a time. To get square pixels, the line period must equal the time the web takes to move one cross-web pixel, so the line rate is

\[ f_\text{line} = \frac{v}{p_\text{across}}, \qquad p_\text{across} = \frac{\text{FOV}_\text{across}}{N_\text{px, line}}. \]

The line period, \(1/f_\text{line} = p_\text{across}/v\), is also the longest exposure each line can use.

The light budget

The number of photons each pixel collects is proportional to the irradiance on the sensor times the exposure time. If blur forces a shorter exposure, the lighting must be brighter by the same factor to keep the same image brightness and signal-to-noise ratio. That is why fast lines use short, intense LED strobe pulses, often only microseconds long, rather than continuous lighting. Raising the camera gain instead brightens the image but amplifies the noise with it, so the signal-to-noise ratio does not improve.

Worked example

Parts move at 500 mm/s under a camera with 2048 pixels across a 60 mm field along the motion:

  • \(p_o = 60\ \text{mm}/2048 = 29.3\ \mu\text{m}\) per pixel, so for ≤ 1 px of blur the exposure must be ≤ 29.3 µm ÷ 500 mm/s = 58.6 µs.
  • A 100 µs exposure smears features over 1.71 pixels (50 µm of travel). Holding the image brightness at 58.6 µs needs 1.71 times as much light.
  • With 20 % overlap, the camera must capture at least 500/(60 × 0.8) = 10.42 frames per second, one frame at least every 96 ms.
  • A 4096-pixel line-scan camera covering 300 mm across the web has 73.24 µm pixels and needs a 6.827 kHz line rate (a 146 µs line period) for square pixels at this speed.

Assumptions and limits

  • Constant speed and a global-shutter camera. Rolling-shutter sensors expose rows at different times, which skews moving parts even with a short exposure. Either use a global-shutter sensor, or fire a short strobe while all rows are integrating at once, with ambient light kept low. That common window exists in global reset release mode (Sony’s “global reset function”), which starts every row at the same moment, or whenever the exposure is longer than the frame readout time.
  • With a strobe, the pulse width sets the blur only if the ambient light collected while the shutter is open is negligible. Otherwise ambient light adds a faint smear \(v\,t_\text{shutter}\) long. Shield the station or shorten the shutter to just cover the pulse.
  • Motion blur combines with optical blur (defocus, diffraction, aberrations): the system MTF is the product of the individual MTFs. For roughly Gaussian blur shapes the widths add in quadrature (root-sum-square). A uniform motion smear is not Gaussian, so treat that sum as an estimate.
  • Speed variation and vibration matter for line-scan cameras. Use an encoder to trigger lines rather than a fixed rate when the speed is not tightly controlled.
