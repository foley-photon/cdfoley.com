Gaussian Beam Aperture Transmission Calculator

How much of a laser beam’s power gets through a round opening (an iris, a lens mount, a mirror), through a slit, or past a knife edge, and how large must the opening be to pass a target fraction? Use it to size irises, mounts, scanner mirrors, and spatial-filter pinholes, and to read knife-edge beam-size measurements. It assumes an ideal, round Gaussian (TEM₀₀) beam.

Key terms
Gaussian (TEM₀₀) beam
The bell-shaped intensity profile of an ideal single-mode laser beam: brightest in the center and fading smoothly outward, with no sharp edge.
1/e² diameter
The usual way to state a laser beam’s size: the diameter where the intensity falls to 13.5 % of the center value. A round opening of that size passes 86.5 % of the power.
Clear aperture
The usable open diameter of an iris, lens mount, or mirror. Outside it, the optic’s performance is not specified.
Clipping (truncation)
Cutting off the outer part of a beam with an edge. The blocked power heats the mount, and the hard edge diffracts what gets through.
Slit
A long, narrow opening that passes a strip of the beam.
Knife-edge profiling
Measuring a beam’s size by moving a razor blade across it while a power meter records how much light gets past.
Diffraction ripples
Rings and intensity ripples that a hard edge puts into the beam downstream of it.

Inputs

1/e² diameter where the beam meets the aperture (for a spatial-filter pinhole, the focused spot diameter). A laser datasheet gives it at the output; it usually grows with distance from the laser (the Gaussian beam tool gives it anywhere along the beam).

Clear aperture of the iris, mount, or mirror, centered on the beam.

%

The fraction you want to get through. The tool gives the size, or blade position, that passes it.

Optional: shows how many watts land on the edge.

How it is calculated

A TEM₀₀ beam has irradiance \(I(r) = I_0\,e^{-2r^2/w^2}\), where \(w = D_b/2\) is the 1/e² radius. Integrating over the open area gives closed-form results:

\[ \begin{gathered} T_\text{circle} = 1 - \exp\!\left(-\frac{2a^2}{w^2}\right) \\[4pt] T_\text{circle} = 1 - \exp\!\left[-2\left(\frac{D_t}{D_b}\right)^{2}\right] \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} T_\text{slit} = \operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}\,s/2}{w}\right) \\[4pt] T_\text{edge}(x_0) = \tfrac12\left[1 + \operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}\,x_0}{w}\right)\right] \end{gathered} \]

Here \(a\) is the aperture radius, \(s\) the full slit width, and \(x_0\) the distance from the beam center to the blade edge (positive while the blade has not yet reached the center). The inverse relations give the aperture (or blade position) needed for a target transmission \(T\):

\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{D_t}{D_b} = \sqrt{\frac{-\ln(1-T)}{2}} \\[8pt] \frac{s}{D_b} = \frac{\operatorname{erf}^{-1}(T)}{\sqrt{2}} \\[8pt] \frac{x_0}{w} = \frac{\operatorname{erf}^{-1}(2T-1)}{\sqrt{2}} \end{gathered} \]

For a circular aperture: 99 % throughput needs \(D_t \ge 1.52\,D_b\), 99.9 % needs \(1.86\,D_b\), and 99.99 % needs \(2.15\,D_b\). A common design rule (Siegman’s 99 % criterion) is to make every aperture at least \(\pi w \approx 1.57\,D_b\) across. That rule is about power; the last column, explained next, is about beam quality.

Clipping is more than lost power

A hard edge diffracts. Behind a round aperture of radius \(a\), the intensity on the beam axis in the near field differs from that of the unclipped beam by the factor

\[ \frac{I(0,z)}{I_\text{G}(0,z)} = \left|\,1 - e^{-a^2/w^2}\,e^{\,i\pi a^2/(\lambda z)}\right|^2 , \]

for a collimated beam with its waist at the aperture, in the paraxial (Fresnel) approximation. As the distance \(z\) changes, this factor swings between \((1 - e^{-a^2/w^2})^2\) and \((1 + e^{-a^2/w^2})^2\): a ripple of about \(\pm 2e^{-a^2/w^2} = \pm 2\sqrt{1-T}\). So clipping 1 % of the power (\(D_t \approx 1.52\,D_b\)) makes the on-axis intensity downstream swing by about ±20 %, and the \(\pi w\) rule still leaves about ±17 %. Keeping the ripple within ±1 % takes \(D_t \approx 4.6\,w\), about 2.3 times the 1/e² diameter (Siegman, §17.1).

The clipped edge also puts weak rings around the focused spot. Ripples and rings can cause hot spots on downstream optics and spread energy outside the processing spot, and a hard-edged profile inflates a second-moment (ISO 11146) M² measurement. A deliberately truncated beam, such as one filling a scanner’s pupil, focuses to a larger spot: see the focused spot tool for the apodization (APO) factor. The power that is clipped is absorbed or scattered by the mount, which matters at kilowatt levels.

Knife-edge beam profiling

Translating a blade through the beam while recording the transmitted power traces out \(T_\text{edge}(x_0)\). For a Gaussian beam this is an error function, the cumulative normal distribution with a standard deviation of \(w/2\), so:

  • The 15.87 % and 84.13 % points are separated by exactly \(w\), one 1/e² radius. (The rounded 16 % and 84 % clip levels give \(0.994\,w\).)
  • The 10 % and 90 % points are separated by \(1.2816\,w\), so \(D_b = 2w \approx 1.561\,\Delta x_{10\text{–}90}\).
  • Fitting the whole recorded curve with the error function uses every data point instead of two (Khosrofian and Garetz, 1983; de Araújo et al., 2009).

These conversions are exact only for a Gaussian profile. ISO 11146-1 defines beam width by the second moment of the intensity (D4σ), which a single knife-edge width can only approximate. Siegman, Sasnett, and Johnston (1991) showed that a knife-edge width taken at clip levels between about 8.5 % and 11.6 %, and scaled by the factor that makes it exact for a Gaussian beam (10 %/90 % with 1.561 is one such pair), comes out only slightly off the second-moment width for a wide range of higher-order and poorer-quality beams.

Worked example

The default inputs: an 8 mm beam passing a 12 mm iris \((D_t/D_b = 1.5)\) transmits \(1 - e^{-4.5} = 98.89\,\%\). Entering an input power of 500 W (the field is blank by default) shows that about 5.6 W lands on the iris. The on-axis intensity downstream ripples by about −20 % to +22 %. Getting to 99.9 % needs an aperture of \(1.858 \times 8 = 14.9\) mm.

Assumptions and limits

  • Ideal TEM₀₀ profile, centered on the aperture. Pointing offsets reduce transmission.
  • Round (stigmatic) beam. For an elliptical beam, the slit and knife-edge results apply with the 1/e² diameter across the slit or along the blade travel. The circular-aperture formula does not apply.
  • The calculator evaluates power at the aperture plane only. The ripple formula and table column above describe the on-axis near field behind a circular aperture; the full diffracted pattern needs a diffraction calculation.
  • Multimode and top-hat beams have heavier or sharper edges. Use measured profiles for those.
